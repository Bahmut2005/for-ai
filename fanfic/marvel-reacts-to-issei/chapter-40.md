# Chapter Forty: Cardinal Crimson Promotion

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Avengers (Captain America, Iron Man, Captain Marvel, Thor, Vision, Hulk) • Cosmic Heavy Hitters (Silver Surfer, Gladiator, Uatu, Heimdall, Karnak) • Gods & Monarch-Class Villains (Hercules, Loki, Doom, Mephisto, Thanos)

The steel-grey banner had one segment left, and the theatre knew what steel-grey finales cost.

For nine nights the machine had run its arc like a tribunal. It had shown them a boy elected protector by a village and had made T'Challa and Doom argue the weight of crowns until dawn. It had shown them a slave market burning and a tyrant spared and a tyrant not spared, and the room had spent an entire evening fighting about where his line was before realizing, collectively, that they had memorized it. Heroes, kings, and monsters — the arc's three doors, and the machine had marched Issei Hyoudou past each one and refused, every night, to shove him through any of them.

Tonight the last segment lit, and the floor of the theatre dimmed into sixty-four vast alternating squares.

"Chess night," said Sam Wilson, very quietly, the way a man says *incoming*.

Because everyone remembered the last chess night. Everyone remembered the trident, and the arithmetic wearing armour, and the little procession of pieces around the gacha wheel's outermost band: pawn, pawn, pawn, knight, rook, bishop — and behind them, greyed out and locked for twenty chapters, crowned in a crimson the room had learned to watch for, a queen.

The wheel came down. The queen was not grey anymore.

**ARC FINALE,** printed the screen, in letters that weighed. **HEROES, KINGS AND MONSTERS: VERDICT.**

**YOU HAVE ASKED, FOR NINE NIGHTS, WHICH OF THE THREE HE IS.**

**THE ANSWER IS A CHESS PIECE. IT IS NOT THE KING.**

"Oh," said Steve Rogers, and sat forward, because he had coached long enough to know an answer arriving. Across the aisle, Doom's gauntleted hand came to rest on the closed cover of the notebook he had famously stopped keeping — not opening it. Touching it. The way one touches a sidearm.

The vision opened on a stadium the size of a nation's pride: a Rating Game, the formal kind, broadcast to the whole of the underworld — and on the far side of a shattered field stood an opponent the theatre already loved.

Sairaorg Bael. The devil with no powers. The heir who had been discarded for being born empty and had built himself back with nothing but training, the man whose loss had taught a much younger Issei what dignity looked like on a defeated face. He was not empty tonight. He wore a Balance Breaker of golden lion plate — a lion-god Longinus wrapped around him like a second dawn — and he was hitting Issei Hyoudou so hard that the arena's reinforced wards were filing formal complaints.

"The kid's rematch," rumbled Ben Grimm, hands already flexing. "Aw, hell. It's the *homework* fight."

It was. The captions laid it out with the machine's brutal economy: everything Arc Four had shown them — the kingdoms, the mercy, the monsters — had been the world asking Issei what he intended to be, and here at the end stood the one rival who was the honest version of all three answers. A hero by effort. A king by right. A monster by workload. And Issei's Triaina, the trident the theatre had once gasped at, was *failing* — Knight too fragile for those fists, Rook too slow for that footwork, Bishop's charge a suicide against a man who crossed the field in half a breath.

"He has to choose," Vision observed, "and every choice is wrong. The trident's flaw was always singular: three answers, one at a time, to an opponent asking all three questions at once."

On-screen, inside a cracked helm, Issei laughed — winded, delighted, entirely without fear — and the theatre heard him say it:

"Ddraig. He's too good for pieces. Let's stop choosing."

"**Partner.**" The dragon-voice had banked pride in it, and something older — the sound of a door that had waited a long time to be opened. "**You know the colour it takes. You know whose colour that is. Hold it in your mind, and do not let go.**"

And the theatre's front row — eight women who had watched their own life become curriculum — went very still, because Rias Gremory had gone the colour of her own hair.

**"CARDINAL CRIMSON PROMOTION" — RARITY: MYTHIC.**

**[CHANGE.]** said the gear, and then, in a chord the theatre had never heard it strike — three system-voices at once, Knight and Rook and Bishop speaking in unison like a cathedral remembering it was one building:

**[CARDINAL CRIMSON — FULL DRIVE!!!]**

The green jewel blazed — and the armour changed colour.

Not upgraded. *Dyed.* The battle-scarred scarlet of twenty chapters of Scale Mail deepened, richened, poured over every plate into a crimson the room recognized on sight, because it sat in their front row: the exact shade of Rias Gremory's hair, the colour of the woman who had spent eight Pawns on a dying pervert. The silhouette that rose out of that light was leaner than the Rook, denser than the Knight, and over each shoulder the Bishop's cannon-housings sat folded like patient wings. On the wheel above the theatre, the crimson queen slid to the centre of the board and stood.

**DESIGNATION: CARDINAL CRIMSON PROMOTION. THE TRUE QUEEN.**

**THE QUEEN MOVES AS THE KNIGHT, STRIKES AS THE ROOK, RANGES AS THE BISHOP. THE PIECES NO LONGER OBJECT. THE BOARD NO LONGER OBJECTS. THERE IS NO LONGER ANY PART OF HIM THAT OBJECTS.**

**HE HAS STOPPED CHOOSING.**

Then the vision let him move, and the theatre's heavy-hitter rows learned what integration meant.

Sonic booms in drumroll — Knight-speed — but when Sairaorg's counter came in like a landslide, Issei did not dodge it. He *took* it, Rook-frame ringing like a bell and holding, and inside his forearms the theatre saw new machinery cycle: pistons of compressed draconic force slamming forward as his fist met the lion's jaw —

**[SOLID IMPACT — BOOSTER.]**

— and the punch detonated twice. Once on contact, once from the piston, a double concussion that folded the air, and Sairaorg Bael, who had walked through the old Rook's best blow with a smile, left the ground and did not stop until a wall received him. Then, before the dust admitted anything, the shoulder housings snapped open — no minute of kneeling, no allies bleeding to buy the charge, three seconds of shrieking accumulation — and a lance of red took the arena's far quadrant out of the conversation.

**CRIMSON BLASTER. CHARGE TIME: REDUCED FROM SIXTY SECONDS TO THREE.**

Quicksilver's chair went over backward again. He didn't bother standing it up. "He kept the speed," Pietro said, to the ceiling, in the tone of a man reading his own eviction notice. "He kept the speed AND the tank AND the artillery, that's — you can't — those were supposed to be *trade-offs*—"

"Flaw analysis," said Carol Danvers, without turning her head. "Karnak. Go."

Karnak of the Tower of Wisdom had been staring at the crimson figure since the colour change, utterly motionless, and the theatre realized it had been waiting for his reading the way one waits for a second opinion on an X-ray.

"I have been looking," Karnak said, "since it stood up." A pause that cost him. "The Knight's flaw was one touch. The Rook's flaw was one sidestep. The Bishop's flaw was sixty seconds. Each form was a bet, and I could read the bet. This form is the three bets *underwriting each other* — the speed guards the cannon's charge, the armour forgives the speed's failures, the cannon punishes anything patient enough to out-position the armour. The flaws have not been removed. They have been arranged in a circle, each one standing behind the next." He folded his hands. "There is a crack in everything. In this, the crack has been promoted to load-bearing. I decline to call that a flaw. It is a philosophy with plating."

The screen, in its long-running habit, stamped the vision the instant he finished:

**THE TRIDENT'S THREE PRICES HAVE NOT BEEN WAIVED. THEY HAVE AGREED TO PAY EACH OTHER.**

"*Stop doing that,*" said Karnak, with feeling.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 3.**

**"DIVIDING WYVERN FAIRIES" — RARITY: EPIC.**

On-screen, Sairaorg came out of the wall — of course he came out of the wall; the theatre would have rioted otherwise — and the golden lion's aura doubled down, and the fight became the kind of fight that gets its own national holiday. And around the crimson armour, without an order, small lights kindled.

Wyverns. Tiny ones — each the size of a hawk, plated in crimson and pale silver, wheeling around Issei in a loose orbiting flock like a solar system that had unionized. The captions introduced them with what the theatre could only interpret as fondness: semi-autonomous dragon familiars, born of the armour itself, each one carrying a fraction of the gear's authority. One clamped onto Issei's shoulder and spoke in a chirp of the system-voice — **[BOOST!]** — and his aura climbed. Two peeled off to Sairaorg's blind side and *bit down on the lion's aura itself*, and the golden light visibly thinned, halved, sipped away like a levy.

"They divide," said Vision, and his voice did the instrument-glow thing. "Observe — those two are not adding to their master. They are *subtracting from the opponent.* Halving hostile output on contact and remitting it home. That is not his native mechanic. Boost doubles; this... this is the inverse function." A pause of precisely the wrong length. "He has incorporated his rival's mathematics. The gear is running both halves of an equation that was designed as a war."

Up in the god-rows, Loki had gone from lounging to leaning in the space of one wyvern. "Little tax collectors," the trickster murmured, delighted despite every plan he owned. "He's flying with a *treasury*. Do you see it, thunderer? Any power raised against him is assessed, halved, and remitted to the crown. Odin's ravens gather *information*. His gather *revenue*."

"Aye," said Thor, who was not laughing, whose knuckles were white on the arm of his seat, whose eyes had the firelight of old stories in them. "And see what else they do."

Because on-screen, one wyvern had left the fight entirely. It flew to the arena's edge, where Issei's teammate lay with a broken guard, and unfolded itself over her like a made bed — becoming, in a wash of light, a lattice of crimson plate. Armour, donated. A shield built out of the Queen's own body, standing over someone who could no longer stand.

"There it is," said Steve Rogers softly. "Twenty chapters, and every form presupposes allies. Even the one that finally doesn't need them — spends itself making sure of *them*." He shook his head slowly. "You could take everything else off the screen. I'd draft him off that one frame."

Tony Stark had been silent for a suspicious length of time, and when the room glanced over, he was on his feet with a hard-light schematic already half-built and dissolving between his hands, expression caught between larceny and grief. "Autonomous support drones, self-fabricating, dual-mode offense-defense, running on *empathy-adjacent targeting* — do you know how long I've been trying to make the House Party protocol do that? He grew it. It's not even his final form, I can *feel* it's not his final form, and it obsoletes four of my suits and one of my therapists."

**GACHA PULL: 3 OF 3.**

**"LONGINUS SMASHER IMPACT" — RARITY: MYTHIC.**

The duel had come down, as the great ones do, to the last exchange — two exhausted armors in a cratered arena, a golden king and a crimson queen, and Sairaorg Bael pouring every remaining watt of a lifetime's homework into one final aura that turned the sky above the stadium to noon.

And Issei Hyoudou's chest opened.

The theatre heard it before it saw it: a sound like a vault accepting its combination, plate after plate of the crimson cuirass unlocking, sliding, blooming outward from his sternum — and behind the armour, where a heart should politely be, there was a muzzle. Not a cannon mounted on the armour. The armour *was* the mounting. The boy was the emplacement, all of him, the entire compounding ledger of the Boosted Gear routed to a single aperture over his own heartbeat.

**[LONGINUS SMASHER.]**

The gear did not shout it. That was the detail the room would revisit in the dark for weeks: every technique for forty chapters had been announced at parade volume, and this one the system said the way surgeons say *scalpel*.

The beam that came out was not red. Red was what it left behind. The vision's sound cut — the theatre's reality declining, once again, to render — and a column of light the width of a city block crossed the arena, took Sairaorg's final aura, his last technique, the arena's horizon-grade wards, and the *evening itself*, and overwrote all of it. When sound crawled back, the stadium's sky — the actual sky, kilometres of it, horizon to horizon — was dyed crimson, a deep saturated bleeding red, as if the sunset had been repainted by something that only owned one colour and infinite conviction.

And the screen froze there — red sky, kneeling king, standing queen — and printed the assessment the entire night had been building toward, with Ddraig's voice speaking over the caption in the old low register of a creature who had watched empires misfile themselves:

"**Understand what you are looking at,**" said the dragon. "**The trident could level a battlefield. This levels the argument. In this form, my partner contends with Satan-class beings — the four thrones of the underworld, the beings that gods schedule around. He is sixteen, and the ceiling above him has become a floor.**"

**CARDINAL CRIMSON PROMOTION: SATAN-CLASS ENGAGEMENT CONFIRMED.**

The theatre did not erupt. Eruptions are for surprises, and this was worse than a surprise; it was a *promotion of the entire syllabus*. What happened instead was quieter and would be remembered longer:

Thor Odinson stood up.

Not to speak. He had no speech. The god of thunder rose from his seat the way warriors rise when a banner passes — slowly, entirely, hammer-hand loose at his side — and stood facing the red sky on the screen as though it were weather arriving over his own kingdom.

Across the aisle, with a sound of ceremonial metal, Victor von Doom stood as well. Doom, who bowed to nothing, who had declared the boy a constant twenty chapters ago, rose and stood in the villains' dark with his cloak settling around him, and offered the screen the one gesture in his entire vocabulary that was not contempt: his full height.

And between them, in the Avengers' rows, Carol Danvers got to her feet like paperwork catching fire.

"Reclassification," she said, and her voice carried to every tier because she made it. "Regional-calamity-class is struck. Effective tonight: *strategic actor, sovereign-tier.* That's not a threat category anymore — that's a *nation.* You don't scramble a response to that. You open an embassy." She stared at the red sky a moment longer. "And God help me, the kid I'd trust most with that beam is the one holding it. That's the whole assessment. That's what forty chapters bought."

"Hnh." The Hulk's rumble rolled down from his demolished quadrant, thoughtful as tectonics. "Sky's red." A pause. "Hulk's seen guys paint the sky. They were never the *nice* one." Another pause, longer. "First time it's the nice one." Nobody could improve on that, so nobody tried.

The Silver Surfer had drifted fully off his altitude, low over the aisles, chrome face lit crimson by the frozen frame. "Heralds are *given* such power, and it costs them their masters' errands," he said. "He built his, ascending cost by cost, and it answers to breakfast tables. The universe is usually crueller than this. I find I am watching it make an exception, and I do not know why. That — " and he looked, deliberately, up toward the Watcher's tier — "is the only question left in this room worth fearing."

Uatu did not answer. Uatu was watching the screen with an attention that had changed quality over ten chapters — no longer the unease of a being missing a record, but the vigilance of a being guarding one.

**ARC FOUR COMPLETE: HEROES, KINGS AND MONSTERS,** the screen printed. **HE IS NOT THE KING. HE IS THE PIECE THAT STANDS IN FRONT OF THE KING — AND IN FRONT OF EVERYTHING ELSE.**

And beneath it, one frame, the old line, steady tonight, almost serene:

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT.**

"Fitting," boomed Hercules into the settling quiet, lifting a flagon the theatre had never once seen him fill. "A toast, then, to the boy-queen of the red sky! Let the record show that Hercules, who has arm-wrestled hydras, would think *twice*—"

The sound was small.

That was the obscenity of it — after a night of muted apocalypses, the sound that stopped the theatre was barely a sound at all: a *tick*, crisp and mineral, like ice remembering it was once water. Hercules's flagon halted mid-arc. Several thousand heads turned.

In the upper right quadrant of the screen — the indestructible screen, the surface that had shrugged off thirty-nine chapters, the Hulk's fists in Chapter One, and the unrendered fury of the Dragon Blaster — there was a crack.

Hairline. A hand-span long. Spidering as they watched, one soft tick at a time, into three branches, then five, fine as a frost-flower and utterly, geologically wrong. The frozen red sky bent around it the way light bends at the edge of a lens, and — those closest would swear to this later, and Heimdall would not contradict them — the thin bright seams of the fracture did not reflect the crimson frame at all. They shimmered with a faint, static-edged wrongness the theatre had seen exactly once before, on the border of a child-shaped hole in a photograph.

"Machine," said Doom, still standing, and his voice had the flat courtesy he reserved for opposing heads of state. "Your theatre is damaged. Doom requires the mechanism."

The screen printed nothing.

"Karnak," said Carol quietly. "Read it."

Karnak looked at the crack for a long time. When he spoke, it was in the voice of a man reading a fracture in a load-bearing wall — the same voice from Chapter Twenty, and the room heard the rhyme and hated it.

"The flaw is not in the screen," he said. "The screen is the single strongest object I have ever assessed; it is stronger than everything it has shown us. The flaw is in the *load*. Something is asking this place to hold more than it was built for — and it was built," he gestured, once, at the impossible architecture, at forty chapters of gods and dragons and red skies, "for *this*. Consider carefully what exceeds it."

The lights went down without permission or ceremony, mid-crack, mid-silence — the first time in forty chapters the machine had ended a night without a banner, a chime, or a word. In the dark, the fracture went on glowing faintly, static-edged, patient.

Ddraig's voice came once, out of the black, very low, and it was not addressed to the room.

"**I know that sound,**" the dragon said. "**Something is testing the walls of the nest. Partner — wherever you truly are — sleep well while you can.**"

Tick, said the crack, softly, and the theatre sat with it until morning failed to come.
