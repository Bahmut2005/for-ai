# Chapter Forty-Eight: Drax Challenges the Emperor

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Guardians of the Galaxy (Drax, Star-Lord, Gamora, Rocket, Groot, Mantis, Nebula) • Avengers (Captain America, Thor, Iron Man, Captain Marvel) • Gods (Hercules, Loki, Heimdall)

**Gacha Pulls:**

2. "Lesson on Intention and Grief" — **Secret**

---

The trouble with letting the Ravager section operate the gacha machine, the theatre had decided somewhere around the fourth night of Arc Five, was that the machine had begun operating *them* back.

It had insulted Yondu's teeth. It had humiliated Rocket across four hundred contingency plans. It had given Nebula, of all people, the compatibility score of the arc, and the room was still recalibrating around the sight of the deadliest woman in the galaxy pretending very hard that a number on a screen had not mattered to her. The scoreboard from that night still hung in the upper corner of the cracked screen — and beneath it, in the fracture the theatre had learned not to look at directly, the static-edged seams went on glowing, patient as arithmetic.

Which is why nobody was surprised when Drax the Destroyer stood up, walked down the aisle to the operator's podium, and put his whole green fist on the pull lever.

"The metal woman received a number," Drax announced, to the room at large, in the voice of a man filing a formal grievance with the universe. "The rat received a heist. The tree received a prophecy. Quill received nothing, which was correct. I have received *nothing also*, which is an error. I do not want a number." He turned to face the screen, chin lifting. "I want the boy. In combat. To the death."

"Buddy," said Peter Quill, half out of his seat, "we've *watched* him erase a mountain—"

"Then he will have the advantage," Drax said, reasonably.

"He paints *skies*, Drax. There is footage. You were there. You clapped."

"I clapped because it was beautiful. I will fight him because it was beautiful. These are not contradictions." Drax pulled the lever. "Machine. Make it fair."

The wheel came down out of the dark with what several witnesses would later describe as enthusiasm.

**REQUEST LOGGED: HONOUR DUEL,** printed the screen. **RESTRICTION PROTOCOL ENGAGED. SUBJECT'S ARCHIVE ACCESS CAPPED AT: BOOSTED GEAR — SCALE MAIL, BASE STATE. NO TRIAINA. NO PROMOTION. NO TRANSFER RESERVE.**

**THIS IS THE MACHINE'S DEFINITION OF "FAIR."**

**IT IS STILL NOT FAIR.**

"Nothing about that statement goes over my head," said Drax, cracking his knuckles. "My reflexes are too fast. I would catch it."

Up in the Avengers' rows, Carol Danvers pinched the bridge of her nose. "Base Scale Mail is a heavy-hitter designation. Core-Avengers-alarm tier. That's the *cap*." She glanced at Steve Rogers. "You want to stop this?"

Steve was watching Drax roll his shoulders in the aisle with the expression of a coach who had seen this exact walk before, on other men, toward other doors. "No," he said quietly. "I want to see what the machine thinks he needs. It hasn't been wrong yet."

---

The theatre floor opened the way it had for Rocket's heist, and built an arena out of light.

It built it, the room noted, with care: a dead moon's surface under a bruised violet sky, ring of standing stones, no civilians, no structures, nothing to protect — a battlefield with the moral furniture deliberately removed. And into it the machine placed two figures. Drax, rendered knife-perfect down to the kill-tally scars. And across the ring, red armour assembling plate by plate out of green light, the visor's slit kindling like a struck match —

**[WELSH DRAGON — BALANCE BREAKER!!]**

— Issei Hyoudou, in the battered base Scale Mail of the middle chapters, before the trident, before the crimson queen. The theatre made the low sound of an audience greeting an old photograph. That armour had bled for Asia. That armour had lost to Sairaorg with dignity. That armour was, by the room's own filed assessments, still capable of removing the moon they stood on from most conversations.

Sim-Drax drew his knives and smiled the smile his crew privately called *the funeral invitation*.

"Red dragon!" he bellowed, joyous, already running. "I am Drax! Called the Destroyer! I have killed many things and been killed by several! You will be a magnificent addition to either list!"

**[BOOST.]**

The first exchange taught the room the shape of the whole fight, though almost nobody read it right away except Gamora and Steve Rogers, whose heads tilted at the same instant.

Drax fought like a man spending himself — every blow a full withdrawal, knives seeking arteries the armour didn't have, absolute commitment, zero retreat. It was, Hercules boomed approvingly, *proper* berserker work, the old style, the style you learn when you have decided your life is a weapon and weapons are for using up.

And Issei — capped, restricted, running on a fraction of his archive — *caught it*. Caught the knife-hand. Turned the hip-throw. Took a blow on the gauntlet that cratered the regolith behind him for thirty metres, and answered with — 

— a shove.

An open gauntlet to the sternum, measured, deliberate: enough to put Drax on his back in the dust, not enough to break anything. The sim's system-voice logged it with terrible neutrality: **CONTACT. FORCE OUTPUT: 4% OF AVAILABLE.**

The theatre's villain rows leaned forward as one.

Drax rolled up, delighted, and came in again. Again the catch, the turn, the redirect. Again a strike that could have gone through him like weather, arriving instead as a wrestler's pin he was allowed to break. Boost after Boost chimed — **[BOOST.] [BOOST.] [BOOST.]** — the doubling ledger climbing into numbers that made Rhodey's HUD-glasses flinch, and none of it, *none of it*, arriving in Drax's body.

"He's stockpiling and not spending," Tony Stark said slowly. "That's — okay, that's actually the most unsettling thing he could do with it. He's letting the meter run so the fight stays real, and then declining to bill."

"He is *toying* with him," snarled Nebula, whose entire nervous system had been built by someone who toyed.

"No." Gamora's voice cut flat across her sister's. She was watching the red visor, not the knives. "Toying wants the humiliation. Look at his feet. He gives ground whenever Drax needs it and takes it back when Drax can bear it. He's not playing with him." A pause; her jaw set. "He's *sparring* him. The way you spar someone you're trying to keep."

In the ring, sim-Drax finally stepped back, chest heaving, and levelled a knife at the dented red armour, and his roar had turned raw at the edges.

"WHY DO YOU NOT FINISH IT?" he thundered. "I am open! My organs are *right here*! I have arranged them conveniently! You strike like a wall and grip like a Kylosian vice-beast and you keep — *catching* me — as if I were a thing that was *falling!* Fight me to the death, Red Dragon! It is the only fight that counts!"

And the boy in the red armour stood there in the dust of the dead moon, aura banked like a furnace with its doors shut, and said — young, winded, and absolutely certain:

"No."

The vision froze on the word.

---

Secret-class pulls did not show the theatre footage. The room had learned this across five arcs. Secret-class pulls showed the theatre *the inside of something* — and what this one opened was the space between two warriors standing in a crater, one word old.

"No," said Issei again — the sim built out of the whole archive, speaking the way the archive knew he spoke. He reached up and released the helm; it folded back plate by plate, and under it was the least legendary face in two universes: seventeen, sweat-soaked, house-cut hair flattened, a boy who looked like he should be failing a math test somewhere. "You keep saying *to the death* like it's the good version. Like it's the fight with the honour in it." He shook his head slowly. "Old man, who told you that?"

"I am not old," said Drax. "I am *seasoned*. And no one told me. I learned it." He sat down on a standing stone that groaned under him, knives loose in his hands, suddenly not pointing at anything. "Ronan the Accuser came to my world. My wife was named Ovette. She danced. My daughter was named Camaria. She was — " the knives went still — "she was learning to. He killed them because they were in the place he wished to stand. Since that day I have fought to the death, every fight, because the death is the *point*. Every enemy I destroy is a stone I take out of the wall between me and the one who did it." He looked up at the boy with genuine, guileless confusion, and the theatre heard three thousand people not breathe. "If you do not fight to destroy, dragon — what do you carry into the fight? A fight is a container. It must hold something. Mine holds them."

And in the theatre, in the front of the Guardians' block, the real Drax the Destroyer sat with his forearms on his knees and did not move, and Mantis, beside him, had gone silver-eyed and silent with both hands pressed flat against her own sternum, feeling what he would never show.

On-screen, Issei sat down in the dust across from him. Not warily. The way you sit down across from someone at a kitchen table.

"I died," he said. "You know that, right? The machine shows everybody eventually. First girl who ever said yes to me put a spear of light through my chest on a park bench and told me the mistake was mine for being born what I am." He turned his left hand over; the green jewel in the gauntlet caught the violet light. "So I know what it's like to be the stone in someone's wall. And I had a friend — Asia. Gentlest person I ever met. Healed the people who spat on her. I watched her die on a church floor because a monster wanted the gift inside her more than the girl around it." His hand slowly closed. "And you know what I wanted, after? Exactly what you want. I wanted the fight where the death is the point. I got it, even. I beat the one who did it into the ground."

"And?" said Drax, leaning in. "Was it not glorious?"

"It was *empty*," said Issei. "That's the thing nobody puts in the war stories. I stood there with everything I'd wanted, and Asia was still dead, and my hands were still shaking, and the container you're talking about — the fight — I'd filled it with *her killer*. I'd made my best friend's murderer the thing I carry." He looked up, and his voice did the thing the theatre had heard exactly a handful of times, the register underneath the pervert and the punchlines, the one Ddraig answered to. "I got her back. I got lucky; my world had a way. But I kept the lesson. Now when I fight — and I fight *a lot*, old man, I fight things with too many heads and gods with grudges — I don't carry the enemy in. I carry *them* in. Asia. My master. My friends. My mom's cooking. Every dumb precious thing waiting on the other side of the fight. Destruction empties the container every time you win." He tapped the green jewel. "Protection *fills* it. That's the whole trick. That's why the doubling never breaks me. You can't overload a fight that's carrying people you love. There's always room."

The dead moon was very quiet.

"Your fists," Drax said at last, slowly, a man doing unfamiliar arithmetic. "When you struck me. Four percent. The rest was — them. You were holding them the entire time. That is why you caught me." His brow furrowed like continental drift. "You were not refusing to kill me. You were refusing to *drop what you were carrying* in order to kill me."

"There he is," said Issei, and grinned. "Nothing gets past you."

"Nothing does. My reflexes are legendary." Drax looked down at his knives for a long moment. Then, with enormous care, the way a man handles the ashes of a house, he said: "Ovette hated my fighting. I have never told anyone this. She said I came home from every victory with less of me. I believed she did not understand warriors." He sheathed the knives. "She understood warriors. She was trying to tell me what you just told me, and I outlived her misunderstanding her, and I have spent every year since putting her *killer* in the container instead of her dancing." His voice did not break. It did something worse; it went simple. "I do not know how to carry the dancing, Red Dragon. It is heavier than the rage."

"Yeah," said the boy softly. "It always is. Carry it anyway. That's the fight, old man. That's the one to the death — it just takes your whole life to finish."

**[BOOST,]** said the gear, once, very quietly, and the theatre would argue for weeks about what, exactly, it had doubled.

The simulation faded with the two of them still sitting there — the Destroyer and the Emperor, in the dust, saying nothing, in the manner of men at a graveside who have finally agreed whose grave it is.

---

The lights came up gently, which the machine almost never did.

Drax the Destroyer stood. The theatre braced for the grievance, the appeal, the demand for a rematch with the restriction protocols loosened.

"The machine's definition of fair was correct," Drax announced instead, to every tier at once. "I was not fighting one boy. I was fighting everyone he loves. No warrior has ever won that fight. It cannot be done. The numbers are absurd." He put his fist over his heart, facing the dark screen, and bowed his head — Drax, who bowed to nothing, whose respect had a body count for an entry fee. "Issei Hyoudou. Dragon Emperor. When we meet — and the tree's prophecy says the sovereign comes, so we will meet — I will not challenge you to the death." A pause. "I will ask you to spar. And I will bring my family to carry. Both of them dance."

"Aye," said Thor, low, from the Avengers' rows, in the voice of a man who had buried a mother, a father, a brother twice, and half a realm. "That is the oldest lesson there is, and the machine had to cross a multiverse to find someone young enough to say it plainly." Hercules, beside him, had nothing loud to offer for the first time in five arcs; he refilled a flagon that could not be filled and raised it anyway. Even Loki was quiet — covetous eyes narrowed at the green jewel, recalculating, as ever, what the dragon was actually guarding.

"Filed," said Carol Danvers, quietly, into her assessment log. "Subject fights at four percent against anyone he intends to keep. Addendum: he intends to keep *everyone*. That's the threat profile. That's the whole threat profile."

Mantis finally lowered her hands from her chest and touched Drax's arm as he sat back down. "It is still there," she whispered. "The grief. It did not get smaller."

"No," Drax agreed. "It got *carried*. This is different. I will explain it to you, but I will explain it badly for several years first."

The screen let the room have the moment. Then, in small print, almost tender by its standards:

**LESSON ARCHIVED. THE RAVAGER SECTION IS ADVISED TO REST.**

**TOMORROW'S PULL CONCERNS A FUNERAL.**

And in the upper corner, softly, patiently, where the frozen dark bent at the seams — *tick*, said the crack.
