# Chapter Thirty-Four: The Slave Market

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Ravagers & Cosmic Pirates (Yondu, Stakar, Kraglin, Rocket, Gamora, Nebula) • Wakanda (T'Challa, Nakia, Okoye, M'Baku) • Governments (Fury, Ross) • The Antihero Row

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Raid on a Slave Market" — **Epic**
2. "Children Freed" — **Mythic**

---

Four nights into the steel-grey banner, the theatre had learned that Arc Four did not ask *how strong is he.* It asked *what does he do with it* — a crown he never wanted, a hunting law kept like scripture — and the room had settled into the rhythm of a seminar. Kings argued with kings. It was almost civilized.

Then the wheel came down running red at the seams, and the Ravager tiers — thirty-three chapters of heckling from the cheap seats — went quiet before the pull even landed. Later, nobody could say how they'd known. Kraglin said you could smell it. Yondu didn't say anything at all.

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2.**

**"RAID ON A SLAVE MARKET" — RARITY: EPIC.**

"Hnh," said Rocket, in the tone of a raccoon getting his feet up on the seatback and absolutely not gripping the armrest. "Epic. Sure. Roll it."

The theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

Under-market. Neutral ground, in the way a knife is neutral — a cavern warded against every treaty ever signed, lit the color of old coins. Cages in tiers, like the theatre's own seating, and the theatre felt that comparison arrive in several thousand minds at once and hated it.

The merchandise was catalogued in chalk. Youkai, half-bloods, Sacred Gear carriers taken young enough to train. Lot numbers. An auctioneer's dais. Collars that drank aura, sized in rows on a rack, and some of the sizes were very small.

The vision gave the audience one full minute of the market operating normally — bidding paddles, a broker's laugh, a fox-eared boy in lot forty-one pressing his face to the bars to watch for his mother, three cages over and unable to reach him — and the theatre understood the minute was not scene-setting. It was the machine entering evidence.

Then the wards died. All of them, at once, the way a candle dies in a doorway.

Issei Hyoudou walked in through the front entrance, because of course he did, and the theatre knew the walk by now — they'd seen it approach Raynare's church, Kokabiel, every closed door in his life. But the face over it was one they hadn't seen. Not rage. Rage was loud, and they'd have recognized it. This was arithmetic.

**[BOOST.]**

"Gentlemen," said the auctioneer, professionally, reaching under the dais.

"One question," said Issei Hyoudou. "Who holds the keys?"

Every slaver in the room had the same thought at the same time — it was written on them — *he's one boy, and there are forty of us* — and the theatre, four arcs educated, made the sound of a room watching men step on a rake in unison.

**[BOOST. BOOST. BOOST.]**

What followed took ninety seconds, and the screen did not linger, and neither did he. That was the part the theatre would argue about all night — not the violence, the *economy* of it. They had watched this boy fight for thirty-three chapters, and he fought the way he lived: loudly, wastefully, taking hits he didn't need to take so someone behind him wouldn't. He checked for surrender the way other men checked their footing.

Not here. Scale Mail closed over him like a verdict, Ascalon came out, and he offered no terms and left none of them alive — the broker with the laugh, the wardsmith who'd made the small collars, the ones who ran and the two who knelt. A guard threw down his weapon and raised his hands, and the theatre leaned forward, because *this* was where Issei Hyoudou always, always stopped.

"You sized the collars," Issei said, and did not stop.

It was fast every time. It was never cruel. It was a door being closed forty times.

"**Partner,**" came Ddraig's voice, low, when it was done and the aura settled — and the theatre noticed the dragon had not spoken once during, had not needed to counsel or restrain, had simply *attended*. "**None of the usual sermon tonight?**"

"They kept people, Ddraig." He was already moving down the cage rows, armor retracting from his hands so the hands reaching back through the bars would touch skin. "You can come back from almost anything — I keep saying that, right? Everybody keeps score of who I let walk." He tore a cage door off its hinges, gently, which should not have been possible and was. "There's no *walking back* from selling a kid a collar in his size. That's not a fight. That's a thing you *are.*"

"**I have lived inside a cage for a very long time,**" said the Welsh Dragon Emperor, softly, to his partner and to a listening multiverse. "**Sealed in this Gear by a god's hand, passed from wielder to wielder like a lamp. You are the first who ever asked me what I wanted. So understand that I say this as an expert and not a moralist: you have my blessing. Burn it.**"

He got the last cage open first. Then he burned it — dragon-fire, floor to vault, thorough as an audit, chalk and ledger and the rack of collars going up while he stood in the entrance with a child on each arm, and the vision held on the fire until it wasn't a market anymore. It wasn't anything anymore.

---

The lights came up on a theatre that had watched him kill before — strays, monsters, Apophis in a war — and had never once watched him kill like *that*, and the first sound was Frank Castle, from the antihero row, exhaling like a man setting down a weight.

"There it is," the Punisher said. "Thirty-four chapters. I knew he had a floor. Everybody's got one."

"That was not a floor, Castle, that was an *execution docket*—" General Ross was on his feet in the government block. "Forty sentient beings, no trial, no authority, no—"

"No survivors to rebuild the network out of," said Nick Fury, not standing. "Be mad, Ross. But I've spent forty years raiding these operations *legally*. You seize the ledger, flip the middlemen, extradite the broker, and eighteen months later the same market's open two systems over with better wards. The market was never the men. It was the demand, and the only thing that touches demand is cost." A pause. "He just repriced the industry. Ask me tomorrow if I'm okay with it, 'cause tonight I don't have a better answer."

"The kneeling one," Daredevil said quietly, from the dark, and the antihero row turned, because Matt Murdock's voice had the sound of a man arguing with himself and losing. "He surrendered. Issei killed him anyway. I've built my whole life on the space between those two seconds."

"He sized the collars," said Jessica Jones, flat as slate, and Matt didn't answer, and that was the street-level tier's whole debate, four words and a silence.

But the theatre's center of gravity had already moved, because up in the Ravager tiers, Yondu Udonta was standing with his hand flat over his fin like a man at an anthem nobody else could hear.

---

"Say it," said Stakar Ogord.

"Ain't sayin' nothin'."

"Say it, Yondu."

"*Twenty years,*" Yondu said, and his voice came out wrong, scraped, the drawl burned off it. "Kree battle slave. Twenty years 'fore you bought me out, an' you know what I 'membered tonight? Not the fightin'. The *chalk*. They wrote my weight on the pen wall in chalk. Little numbers." His hand came down off the fin. "Weren't nobody ever walked into *that* market an' asked who held the keys."

"I did," Stakar said. "I walked into yours."

"You *bought* me out, Stakar. Legal-like. Left the market standin'." Yondu's teeth showed, and it wasn't a grin. "Boy didn't leave the market standin'."

"They *took me apart*," said Rocket, and the tier went still, because Rocket Raccoon did not volunteer, ever, and here he was with his claws dug into the seatback. "Cage, chalk, lot number. Subject Eight-Nine-P-One-Three. Nobody came. I chewed my own way out, an' you wanna know the sick joke? I used to lie in that cage an' make up exactly this guy. Big idiot, kicks the door in, doesn't negotiate, torches the paperwork so they can't restock." He unhooked his claws one at a time. "Kid me owes the screen an apology. Turns out I wasn't makin' him up. I was just early."

"He killed them all," Nebula said, from the row above, in that ruined-machinery voice. "The ones who ran and the ones who knelt. Good. When Thanos took us, whole worlds knelt. Kneeling is what they do *when the strong show up.* It is not repentance. It is inventory." She looked at her sister. "The green one disagrees."

"The green one," Gamora said slowly, "is remembering that we were merchandise that got *adopted* by the buyer, and nobody burned that market either. No. I don't disagree. I'm deciding whether that frightens me." She watched the screen. "It doesn't. That frightens me."

It was Kraglin who asked the actual question — Kraglin, who'd been quiet, whose voice cracked in the middle of it. "Cap'n. Stakar, sir. What he done down there. Is that... does that *count?* As code?"

And the theatre saw Stakar Ogord — Starhawk, a hundred flames' admiral, the man who had exiled his own best captain for trafficking children and grieved him every day of the exile — stand up and take his time.

"Ravagers don't deal in kids," Stakar said. "One law. We're thieves, smugglers, liars, and killers, and we hold *one line*, and I have burned brothers off my flame for crossing it, and it cost me more than any of you know." He didn't look at Yondu. He put his hand on Yondu's shoulder without looking, which was worse, and better. "Every captain in this room's been asking whether the dragon-boy's got a code. You're asking it backwards. Codes are what we *wrote down* because we couldn't trust ourselves to be him. He steals nothing. He frees the hold. He spaces the slavers. He burns the manifest." Stakar's voice dropped into the register of a man at a colors ceremony. "That's not *like* the code. That's the thing the code is a copy of. Boy's been flying Ravager colors his whole damn life. Nobody told him."

"So we tell him," said Yondu, and whistled — one note, low, and the yaka arrow rose out of its holster and burned a slow crimson circle over the tier, the Ravager salute, the flame they light when one of theirs comes home. Ninety-nine flames of Stakar's fleet stood up in the dark, tier over tier, and matched it.

"He's a *devil*," someone from the government block protested, faintly.

"He's a *Ravager*," said a hundred pirates, in the tone of men correcting a rounding error.

---

Down in the Wakandan delegation, Nakia had not spoken through any of it. She was watching the replay-echo the screen kept faintly cycling — the fox-eared boy, lot forty-one, watching for his mother — and when she finally spoke it was to her king, and it carried.

"The river camp," she said. "The night you came for me, T'Challa. I was in-country with seventeen women taken for sale and I had a choice between my cover and their chains, and you know what I chose, and you know I have never once filed the paperwork on what happened to the men holding them." She lifted her chin at the screen. "Do not let this room pretend the question is exotic. It is the oldest question our people ever answered. Wakanda watched the ships pass. We hid, and we survived, and the hiding kept us — and every Wakandan child learns both halves of that sentence and is never told which half to be proud of."

"Which is why we do not get to be neutral," T'Challa said quietly. "We built a fortress and called it wisdom while a trade in human beings emptied the coast beyond our border. My father's fathers made that choice a thousand times, and I have stood in the place where the ancestors live and asked them if it was right, and they did not answer me either." He watched the fire on the screen. "I have killed for Wakanda. I have spared for Wakanda. I do not know if the boy's arithmetic is just. I know that if the ancestors ever show me a market like that one, I hope I have his nerve, and I notice that I did not say his restraint."

"He has no vibranium, no army, no border," Okoye said. "And no one will ever again be sold in that cavern. My general's opinion is brief."

"The man-ape tier concurs," rumbled M'Baku, with no comedy in it at all. "The Jabari kept apart from everything, even Wakanda. Apart is a kind of safe. Tonight I find I do not admire *apart* very much."

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2,** said the screen, before anyone could recover.

**"CHILDREN FREED" — RARITY: MYTHIC.**

---

Dawn, above ground, on a hillside gone silver with frost. Thirty-one children and nineteen adults who had been lot numbers an hour ago, wrapped in whatever Issei had been able to tear loose from the market's stores before the fire, and the boy himself — armor gone, jacket gone, jacket being currently worn by three children at once as a tent — kneeling in the grass with his gauntleted hand out flat.

**[TRANSFER.]**

Warmth moved through the crowd like sunrise given a schedule — boosted power, the theatre realized, the doubling engine that had erased mountains, stepped down and poured out as *heat*, into small shaking bodies, because that was a thing you could apparently do with the fires of the Red Dragon Emperor if it never once occurred to you that they were only for war.

The fox-eared boy from lot forty-one stood apart, at the exact distance of a child who has learned what adults are for. His mother was free three steps away and he was watching Issei instead, because his arithmetic was newer and worse.

"Are you going to sell us too?" he asked.

The theatre did not breathe. Issei Hyoudou sat down in the frost — sat, so he'd be shorter than the boy — and thought about it honestly, visibly, the way the room had watched him fail math tests. "No," he said. "I'm the guy they tell scary stories about at slaver bedtime. It's a good job. Dental's weird." And then, when the joke didn't land, because it wasn't meant to, and the boy just kept watching him: "Nobody's going to hold you again unless you run to them. That's the whole rule. That one's forever."

The boy ran to his mother. Then — the vision held it, gently — he came back, and stood beside Issei, not touching, and after a moment took hold of one crimson finger of the gauntlet with his entire fist, in the manner of a small child claiming a very large object, and the Red Dragon Emperor sat perfectly still in the frost, the way you do when something wild decides you're safe.

And the machine did the thing the theatre would carry out of the room that night. As the Gremory circles bloomed on the hillside — Rias, Asia's healing light, Kiba with blankets, Yasaka's escort come down from Kyoto for the youkai young — the screen began printing names. Every child, one at a time, white letters, unhurried. *Renji, son of Chiyo, of the Kyoto fox clans. Mirra, Sacred Gear carrier, aged seven.* Thirty-one names, fifty in all, a census of the recovered, and it did not stop until every single soul on that hillside had been entered into the record.

In the front row, eight women sat very close together, and did not say the thing they were all thinking, about a photograph, about a child the record refused. Koneko's jaw was set. Asia's eyes shone. The machine named the fiftieth freed soul, held the full list on screen for a long, deliberate moment — *see how easy it is,* the display seemed to say, *when nothing has gotten there first* — and went dark.

"**Nest law,**" said Ddraig, into the theatre's silence, to the children on the screen and the pirates in the tiers and possibly to something beyond both. "**Nothing kept in a cage while the dragon draws breath. He did not learn it from me. I checked.**"

**NEXT PULL,** printed the screen, small and cold, over the dying image of the hillside. **HE SHOWED YOU THE LINE. NEXT: WHAT LIVES ON THE OTHER SIDE OF IT. A TYRANT WILL KNEEL. HE WILL NOT DIE.**

"Now hold on," said Frank Castle.

The lights went down on a hundred Ravager flames still burning crimson in the dark, holding the salute all the way to black.
