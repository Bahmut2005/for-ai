# Chapter Thirty-Three: The Law of the Jungle

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Ravagers (Yondu, Rocket, Kraglin, Stakar) • Wakandans (T'Challa, Okoye, M'Baku) • X-Men (Wolverine, Storm) • Midnight Suns (Blade, Elsa Bloodstone, Nico Minoru)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Dragon Philosophy on Hunting vs. Killing" — **Rare**
2. "Speech to a Monster-Hunting Village" — **Rare**

---

Three chapters into the steel-grey banner, the theatre had still not entirely warmed back up. The gold had gone out of the ceiling on the night of the photograph, and Arc Four had offered iron in its place: a title nobody had explained — *Emperor of Hope* — hung over the corpse of a god-serpent, and then a kingdom that had lined up, of its own free will, and asked a loudmouthed pervert to rule it.

Tonight the wheel came down at the front of the house, over the hunters' rows — and the hunters' rows had grown.

They had been accreting since Chapter Fifteen, the way people who work nights find the same diner, and the diner had annexed the block. Blade at the aisle, sword across his knees. Elsa Bloodstone with her boots on the chair in front of her, a policy position by now. Nico Minoru and the notebook that took its own notes. Frank Castle at the end, with his sightlines. And spreading up the rows like a rumor: Logan, who had drifted down from the X-Men's block after Chapter Thirty-One and simply stayed, Storm a row behind him; the Wakandan delegation, T'Challa flanked by Okoye's spear and M'Baku's opinions; and, sprawled across six seats they had aggressively colonized, the Ravagers — Yondu with his fin catching the screen-light, Kraglin, Rocket perched on a seat back like a gargoyle with a grudge, Stakar Ogord sitting apart the way flag officers do.

"Lotta professionals down front tonight," observed Logan.

"Machine's been seating us," said Blade. "You noticed."

"I noticed."

The wheel spun once, hard, and dropped its token like a shell casing.

**PULL ONE: DRAGON PHILOSOPHY ON HUNTING VS. KILLING — RARE.**

"Philosophy," said Rocket. "Love it when the loot box assigns homework."

The screen opened on snow.

---

Mountains, north and nameless, years along the boy's timeline — these were the shoulders of the man from the war chapters, the one the serpent had died in front of. Issei Hyoudou walked a ridgeline with a contract seal glowing on one gauntlet: sanctioned work, posted and paid, the same trade the theatre had learned in a flooded quarry eighteen chapters ago.

The quarry had been the apprenticeship. This was the master's round.

The thing he'd been sent for had killed eleven people in three villages, and the gacha filmed the hunt the way it had filmed the Stray — glimpses, patience, procedure. Track. Confirm. Cut the angles down to one line that touched nothing but the target. The kill took a single Dragon Shot and less than a second, and the thing died mid-lunge, fast, unhumiliated, never played with.

And then the theatre watched the Emperor of Hope stand in the snow beside six hundred kilos of dead man-eater and send a column of red flame skyward, and stand at attention until it went out.

"Still does it," said Elsa Bloodstone, quietly. "All these years on. Every kill."

The second beast arrived twenty minutes later — a young wyvern-thing, ribs showing through its hide, dragging a farmer's sheep over a fence. The contract entitled him to destroy this one too. The theatre watched Issei look at the starving animal, and at the dead sheep, and sigh from his boots.

He did not kill it. He ran it off — one flare of the aura that had ended Apophis, dialed down to *housecat, no* — then herded it eleven kilometers to a valley with wild goats and no farms, dropped a haunch of something in front of it, and flew back to overpay the farmer for the sheep out of his own fee, complaining the entire time.

"**You realize,**" said Ddraig, amused, "**the posting entitled you to two kills tonight.**"

"The first one ate people. It knew what it was doing. This one ate a *sheep* because it's a dumb hungry teenager in a bad winter. Not the same animal." He landed on the ridge and looked back at the valley. "Ddraig. When you were free — big — eating whatever you wanted. Did you ever feel bad about it?"

A silence with ten thousand years in it.

"**No,**" said the dragon at last. "**And yes. Attend, and I will give you the oldest law I know. Everything alive is eating something. The hawk is not evil; the wolf pack is not a war crime. The jungle has one law and the law is hunger, and there is no cruelty anywhere in it — hunger kills as fast as it can, because slow is expensive. Cruelty had to be invented. It takes a *person* to kill slow on purpose. It takes a person to kill what he does not need, for the pleasure of the fear, or the story, or the skull on the wall. No true animal has ever done it. That is the one sin the jungle never taught.**"

"So the line's not killing," Issei said slowly, working it the way the theatre had watched him work everything since Chapter One — badly, doggedly, all the way to the end. "Everybody draws it at killing, and that's the wrong place. Killing's the jungle. The line is *why.* If you kill because you have to — to eat, to protect the people behind you — and you do it fast, and you don't enjoy the hurting part, and you carry it after: that's clean. That's just being an animal, and animals are innocent. But if you kill because it makes you feel big — for a trophy, for a wall, to watch something be afraid of you — that's not hunting. That's cruelty wearing hunting's clothes. And it's the only killing that's actually evil, and it's the one kind no animal ever does. Just us."

"**Just persons,**" agreed Ddraig. "**Dragons included. You burn your kills skyward because the fire says: you were not a trophy. You were hunger meeting hunger, and I will remember you. Keep the rite, partner. The day a hunter stops paying for his kills, he starts collecting them.**"

The screen dimmed to argument-light.

---

"Okay," said Rocket, with none of his usual velocity. "So the dragon and the idiot just casually solved a thing I've been chewing on my whole life, and I *hate* that." His voice came out flat and wrong. "You know what a trophy is, from the wall's point of view? I do. I was somebody's project. There ain't a lot of daylight between a trophy wall and a lab shelf — both of 'em are some person lookin' at somethin' alive and seein' proof of how big *they* are. Hunger woulda been kinder. Hunger woulda been *over.*"

Yondu didn't laugh, which by itself quieted three rows. "Where I come out of," he said, "law of the jungle meant biggest eats first. Kree taught me that with a chain on. Kid's version's got an extra rule welded in — biggest one keeps *watch* instead." He tipped his fin at the screen. "Eleven klicks in the snow to relocate a lizard he was legally paid to shoot. Ain't decided if that's the dumbest or the richest thing I ever seen."

"Both," said Stakar Ogord. "That's usually how codes work."

Logan stood next, and Logan standing carried the same weight down front that Blade standing did.

"I'll translate for the rows that don't do this," he said. "You heard the dragon: cruelty had to be invented. Well, I've met the inventors. I've been the animal on both ends of the hunt — the thing in the woods and the thing they sent into the woods — and I'll tell you the exact width of the line the kid just drew. Me and Sabretooth got the same claws. Same nose, same teeth, same jungle. Different *why.* That's the entire distance between us, and some nights it ain't wide, and the kid's speech is the fence I've walked my whole life without the words for it." He sat back down. "Kid found the words at seventeen. Took me a hundred years and a lot of buried friends."

"There is no wickedness anywhere in nature," said Storm, who never needed to raise her voice. "I was worshipped once as a goddess of the rains. I have watched lions kill and I have watched poachers kill, and only one of them ever left the head and took nothing else."

Which was when the mezzanine cleared its throat.

"At last," said Kraven the Hunter, rising out of the villains' dark like something surfacing, "a *civilized* evening. You misunderstand the boy, and you misunderstand me. I have never taken an unworthy trophy — the helicopter men, the poison men, vermin, I despise them as he does. The hunt is a contest of worth, the oldest sacrament, the proving of oneself against the great prey. The boy honors his kills and so do I." He spread his hands. "I claim kinship."

"Denied," said Logan, without turning around. "You said it yourself, bub. *Proving of oneself.* Every altar you ever built, you're the god on it. The kid runs sheep-math in a snowstorm so a farmer's kids eat, and the fire he burns ain't about him — you heard the dragon, it's a receipt. You hunt to be the apex." One claw slid out, lazily, and back in. "He hunts so nobody has to know there was ever anything to be apex *of.* Ain't the same church. It ain't even the same species of prayer."

Kraven stood very still for a moment, and sat back down without conceding — but he stayed leaned forward for the rest of the night, elbows on knees, like a man at a trial that had turned out to be his.

**PULL TWO: SPEECH TO A MONSTER-HUNTING VILLAGE — RARE.**

---

The village's name rendered as Harrow's Gate, and it had survived four hundred years on the edge of a cursed forest by killing what came out of it. It was, the theatre understood within seconds, a town-sized version of the front rows: watchtowers, bell-lines, a hunters' lodge older than most nations, and a long outer wall of skulls.

Issei had been invited — the region's sanctioned protector since the war — to bless their autumn festival. The elders, the roll of the dead read aloud, four centuries of names; his face through all of it was the theatre's favorite face, the open one. These were his people.

Then they showed him the new wing of the lodge, and the face closed.

The old skulls each bore a name-plate and a death it had answered for. The new wing was *décor* — a generation of prosperous young hunters, raised safe behind walls the war had made secure, going into a quieted forest and bringing back heads for status. A denning mother-beast that had threatened no one, taken with her young for a first-kill rite. Small harmless things killed for the plumage. Trophies of nothing, answering for nothing.

The village waited for the blessing. Issei stood before the wall of skulls for a long, terrible moment, and gave them something else.

"Your grandmothers' wall is holy," he said, "and this one is garbage, and I'm going to tell you the difference, because your grandmothers aren't here to."

He did not shame their history — he honored it, name by name, four hundred years of standing between the forest and the children: *that was the jungle's law, and you kept it, and it kept you.* Then he drew the line where a dragon had taught him to draw it. "Hunting fed you. Hunting guarded you. Hunting is what the strong owe the weak, and it's clean — as long as the kill is for the *walls.*" He pointed at the old wing, then the new. "The moment it's for the *wall*, you're not hunters anymore. You're the thing your grandmothers built this town to stop, with better manners. That mother wasn't your enemy. She was your neighbor, and you killed her for a story, and nothing in that whole cursed forest has ever done anything that low. Cruelty isn't the jungle. Cruelty is what people bring *into* the jungle." He let it stand. Then, quieter: "Keep hunting. I'll train your watch myself — things will come out of that forest again someday, and I want this town standing. But from tonight: you kill what threatens you, you kill it fast, you use what you kill or you burn it skyward and you *remember it* — and the new wall comes down. Not because I said so. Because you're hunters, and hunters don't keep garbage."

The village took the wall down that night. The vision ended on a whole town at attention around a column of flame, learning a rite older than every nation watching, and Issei with his hand on a young trophy-taker's shoulder, teaching him where to look.

---

"There it is," said Blade, and stood, sword-point grounded like a chairman's gavel. "The pull I've been waiting thirty-three chapters for. Everybody down front carries a code, and mine's kept me on the right side of a hunger for a long lifetime, so believe me on one thing: a code you only keep when it's easy is a costume. That kid walked into a room full of people who love him, holding a festival in his honor, and told them their new wall was garbage — and *then* offered to train their watch, because the code isn't there to make him feel clean, it's there to keep the town alive. A code you enforce on your own side, at your own cost, when nobody's making you?" He sat back down. "That gear holds. I'd work with him tonight."

"The Jabari," announced M'Baku, in the voice he used for pronouncements the whole theatre deserved, "apologize to the prey. We thank it, we waste nothing, and we mock anyone who mounts heads — which is to say we mock the lowlanders *constantly.* The dragon would make a passable Jabari. That is the highest review I have issued in thirty-three chapters."

"My country's power began with a hunt," said T'Challa, more seriously. "The goddess, the herb, the first Black Panther — and the first law Bast gave was not *be strong.* It was *the strength is for them.* Every colonizer who came for Wakanda came with trophies of my continent on his walls; I know which side of the boy's line those men lived on. He has re-derived, alone on a ridge, what my nation took a goddess and ten thousand years to codify: predation with stewardship is guardianship. Predation without it is appetite with a flag."

"The spear is for the people behind you," said Okoye. "He holds it correctly. That is the entire review."

Frank Castle had been silent through all of it, and the front rows knew what that silence was for.

"Say it, Frank," said Blade.

"*Respect your prey* is a bedtime story," said Frank, flatly, aiming the words at the screen like he was zeroing them. "The man-eater didn't feel honored. Dead things don't have a point of view — that's what dead means. Skip the funeral and the target's exactly as neutralized. All that ritual is sentiment, and here's the operational problem with sentiment: the day the prayer makes you slow — the day you're lining up on something about to eat a kid and part of you is already composing the eulogy — that's the day the eulogy's for the kid. Respect the living. The dead don't file complaints."

"You're wrong," said Elsa Bloodstone, boots coming off the chair, "and you're wrong in a way I can *document*, because my father agreed with you. Ulysses Bloodstone: no rites, no last words, mouths are for trophies, sentiment gets you killed. Maximum efficiency, thirty years, never slowed down once. And I've read every journal he left, and I watched what he was at the end, and I'm telling you the rites aren't for the prey, Frank — they never were. They're maintenance. On the *weapon.* The fire is how the kid checks, every single kill, that he still knows the difference between a receipt and a collection. Skip it for thirty years and you get my father's wall. And then you get my father."

Frank thought about that the way he did, like an argument was a structure to clear room by room.

"Maintenance, I'll allow," he said at last. "Calibration, I'll allow — I gave the Gremory girl's last-words rule that much, and I'll extend it to the fire. But I'm keeping the asterisk, and this arc's coming for it fast: it's easy to respect your prey when your prey has claws. His whole pretty code got built on monsters that look like monsters." He nodded once at the dark screen. "Chapter Fifteen, I said one day the thing eating kids would have a pulse and a face and paperwork, and I'd want to see that chapter. Machine's been listening since Thirteen." He settled back. "So roll it."

The machine, which had indeed been listening, rolled it.

The lights had barely begun their dim when the screen flickered up its parting gift: a starfield, a station, a docking manifest scrolling in cold system font — weights, lots, cage dimensions — and over a hold full of small frightened faces, a single stamped word: **LIVESTOCK.**

The Ravager rows came out of their seats as one body. Yondu was standing with his arrow floating an inch off his hip, fin blazing, and nobody down front said one word about sentiment.

**NEXT: THE SLAVE MARKET.**

"Jungle's got one law," said Rocket, very quietly, into the dark. "That ain't it."
