# Chapter Thirty-Three: The Law of the Jungle

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Ravagers (Yondu, Stakar, Rocket, Kraglin) • Wakandans (T'Challa, Okoye, M'Baku) • X-Men (Wolverine, Storm, Beast) • Midnight Suns (Blade, Elsa Bloodstone, Robbie Reyes, Magik, Nico Minoru)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Dragon Philosophy on Hunting vs. Killing" — **Rare**
2. "Speech to a Monster-Hunting Village" — **Rare**

---

Three chapters into the steel-grey banner, the theatre had still not entirely warmed back up.

The Family-Class gold had gone out of the ceiling on the night of the photograph, and what Arc Four had offered in its place was iron: a title nobody had explained — *Emperor of Hope* — hung over the corpse of a god-serpent, and then a kingdom that had lined up, of its own free will, and asked a loudmouthed pervert to rule it. The political rows were still fighting about that one. Doom had called the coronation *correct*. Steve Rogers had called it *concerning*. Namor had called it several things in Atlantean that the theatre's captioning had declined to render.

Tonight, though, the wheel came down at the front of the house, over the hunters' rows — and the hunters' rows had grown.

They had been accreting since Chapter Fifteen, the way people who work nights find the same diner, and by now the diner had annexed the block. Blade at the aisle, sword across his knees. Elsa Bloodstone with her boots on the chair in front of her, a policy position at this point. The Midnight Suns behind — Robbie Reyes and his idling shadows, Magik with one armored boot swinging, Nico Minoru and the notebook that took its own notes. Frank Castle at the end with his sightlines. And now, spreading up the rows like a rumor: Logan, who had drifted down from the X-Men's block around Chapter Thirty-One and simply stayed; Storm and Beast a row behind him; the Wakandan delegation, T'Challa flanked by Okoye's spear and M'Baku's opinions; and, sprawled across six seats they had aggressively colonized, the Ravagers — Yondu with his fin catching the screen-light, Kraglin, Rocket on the seat back like a gargoyle with a grudge, and Stakar Ogord sitting apart the way flag officers do.

"Lotta professionals down front tonight," observed Logan.

"Machine's been seating us," said Blade. "You noticed."

"I noticed."

The wheel spun once, hard, and dropped its token like a shell casing.

**PULL ONE: DRAGON PHILOSOPHY ON HUNTING VS. KILLING — RARE.**

"Philosophy," said Rocket. "Great. Love it when the loot box assigns homework."

The screen opened on snow.

---

Mountains, somewhere north and nameless, years along the boy's timeline — the audience could read his age now by the set of his shoulders, and these were the shoulders of the man from the war chapters, the one the serpent had died in front of. Issei Hyoudou walked a ridgeline in half-manifested armor with a contract seal glowing on one gauntlet: sanctioned work, posted and paid, the same trade the theatre had learned in a flooded quarry eighteen chapters ago.

The quarry had been the apprenticeship. This was the master's round.

The thing he'd been sent for had killed eleven people in three villages, and the gacha filmed the hunt the way it had filmed the Stray — glimpses, patience, procedure. Track. Confirm. Cut the angles down until only one line remained, a line that touched nothing but the target. The kill, when it came, took a single Dragon Shot and less than a second, and the thing died mid-lunge, fast, unhumiliated, without ever being played with.

And then the theatre watched the Emperor of Hope stand in the snow beside six hundred kilos of dead man-eater and send a column of red flame skyward, and stand at attention until it went out.

"Still does it," said Elsa Bloodstone, quietly. "All these years on. Every kill."

"**You are brooding, partner,**" said Ddraig, as Issei started down the far slope.

"I'm *thinking.* It's different. It makes a different face."

"**It does not.**"

What he was thinking about arrived twenty minutes later, in the form of the second beast — a young wyvern-thing, ribs showing through its hide, caught in the act of dragging a farmer's sheep over a fence. The contract seal flickered: the farmer had petitioned to have this one destroyed too. The theatre watched Issei look at the starving animal, and look at the dead sheep, and sigh from his boots.

He did not kill it. He ran it off — one flare of the aura that had ended Apophis, dialed down to *housecat, no* — then chased it eleven kilometers through the passes to a valley with a wild-goat population and no farms, dropped a boosted haunch of something in front of it, and flew back to overpay the farmer for the sheep out of his own contract fee, complaining the entire time.

"**You realize,**" said Ddraig, amused, "**that the posting entitled you to two kills tonight.**"

"The first one ate people. It knew what it was doing — you told me yourself, that kind knows. This one ate a *sheep* because it's a dumb hungry teenager in a bad winter. That's not the same animal." He landed on the ridge and looked back at the valley, and the gacha settled in, because the audience had learned this settling: the machine wanted them to hear what came next.

"Ddraig. When you were — you know. Free. Big. Eating whatever you wanted. Did you ever feel bad about it?"

A silence with ten thousand years in it.

"**No,**" said the dragon at last. "**And yes. Attend, and I will give you the oldest law I know. Everything alive is eating something. The hawk is not evil. The wolf pack is not a war crime. The jungle has one law and the law is hunger, and there is no cruelty anywhere in it — hunger kills as fast as it can, because slow is expensive. I ate kingdoms' herds and I felt nothing, because I was hunger with wings, and hunger is innocent.**" A pause. "**Cruelty had to be invented. It takes a *person* to kill slow on purpose. It takes a person to kill what he does not need, for the pleasure of the fear, or the story, or the skull on the wall. No true animal has ever done it. That is the one sin the jungle never taught.**"

"So the line's not killing," Issei said slowly, working it the way the theatre had watched him work everything since Chapter One — badly, doggedly, all the way to the end. "Everybody draws it at killing and that's — that's drawing it in the wrong place. Killing's the jungle. The line is *why.* If you kill because you have to — to eat, to protect the people behind you — and you do it fast, and you don't enjoy the hurting part, and you carry it after: that's clean. That's just being an animal, and animals are innocent. But if you kill because it makes you feel big — for a trophy, for a wall, to watch something be afraid of you — that's not hunting. That's cruelty wearing hunting's clothes. And it's the only kind of killing that's actually evil, and it's the one kind no animal ever does. Just us."

"**Just persons,**" agreed Ddraig. "**Dragons included. I have known dragons who hunted to eat and dragons who hunted to be feared, and I am telling you, partner, the second kind made worse corpses and worse dragons. You burn your kills skyward because the fire says: you were not a trophy. You were hunger meeting hunger, and I will remember you. Keep that rite. The day a hunter stops paying for his kills, he starts collecting them.**"

The frame held on the man and the mountain, and the screen dimmed to argument-light.

---

"Okay," said Rocket, into the pause, with none of his usual velocity. "So the dragon and the idiot just — casually — solved a thing I've been chewing on my whole life, and I *hate* that."

"Say the thing," said Kraglin.

"The thing is *trophies.*" Rocket's voice came out flat and wrong. "You know what a trophy is, from the wall's point of view? I do. I was somebody's project. There ain't a lot of daylight between a trophy wall and a lab shelf — both of 'em are some person lookin' at somethin' alive and seein' proof of how big *they* are." He scratched at the seat back, hard. "Hunger woulda been kinder. Hunger woulda been *over.*"

Yondu didn't laugh, which by itself quieted three rows. "Where I come out of," he said, "law of the jungle meant biggest eats first. Kree taught me that with a chain on. Kid's version's got an extra rule welded in — biggest one keeps *watch* instead." He tipped his fin at the screen. "Eleven klicks in the snow to relocate a lizard he was legally paid to shoot. Ain't decided yet if that's the dumbest or the richest thing I ever seen."

"Both," said Stakar Ogord, from his flag seat. "That's usually how codes work."

It was Logan who stood up next, and Logan standing carried the same weight down front that Blade standing did.

"I'll translate for the rows that don't do this," he said. "You heard the dragon: cruelty had to be invented. Well, I've met the inventors. I've *been* the invention — I've been the animal on both ends of the hunt, the thing in the woods and the thing they sent into the woods, and I'm gonna tell you the exact width of the line the kid just drew. Me and Sabretooth got the same claws. Same nose, same teeth, same jungle. Different *why.* That's it. That's the entire distance between me and him, and some nights it ain't wide, and the kid's little speech is the fence I've been walking my whole life without the words for it." He sat back down. "Kid found the words at what, seventeen? Good for him. Took me a hundred years and a lot of buried friends."

"The why is everything," said Storm. She hadn't raised her voice; she never needed to. "I was worshipped once as a goddess of the rains, and I watched droughts come, and lions take the weakest of the herds, and I will tell the room what the plains taught me: there is no wickedness anywhere in nature. I have seen a lion kill and I have seen poachers kill, and only one of them ever left the head and took nothing else."

Which was when the mezzanine cleared its throat.

"At last," said Kraven the Hunter, rising out of the villains' dark like something surfacing, "this theatre produces a *civilized* evening. You misunderstand the boy, all of you, and you misunderstand me. I have never taken an unworthy trophy. The helicopter men, the poison men, the fence-and-rifle men — vermin, I despise them as he does. The hunt is a *contest of worth*, the oldest sacrament, the proving of oneself against the great prey — the boy honors his kills and so do I. He and I keep the same faith." He spread his hands. "I claim kinship."

The hunters' rows considered this.

"Denied," said Logan, without turning around. "You just said it yourself, bub. *Proving of oneself.* Every altar you ever built, you're the god on it. The kid runs sheep-math in a snowstorm so a farmer's kids eat, and pays for the sheep out of his own pocket, and the fire he burns ain't about him — you heard the dragon, it's a receipt. You hunt to be the apex." He popped one claw, lazily, and let it slide back in. "He hunts so nobody has to know there was ever anything to be apex *of.* Ain't the same church. It ain't even the same species of prayer."

Kraven stood very still for a moment, the way Deadpool once had, and sat back down without conceding — but the theatre noticed he stayed leaned forward for the rest of the night, elbows on knees, like a man at a trial that had turned out to be his.

**PULL TWO,** said the screen. **SPEECH TO A MONSTER-HUNTING VILLAGE — RARE.**

---

The village had a name the captioning rendered as Harrow's Gate, and it had survived four hundred years on the edge of a cursed forest by killing what came out of it, and it was, the theatre understood within seconds, a town-sized version of the front rows: watchtowers, bell-lines, a hunters' lodge older than most nations, and a long outer wall of skulls.

Issei had been invited — the Red Dragon Emperor, the region's new sanctioned protector since the war — to bless their autumn festival. The vision walked him through it: the honored elders, the roll of the dead read aloud, four centuries of names. His face through all of that was the theatre's favorite face, the open one. These were his people. This was Chapter Fifteen with four hundred years of practice.

Then they showed him the new wing of the lodge, and the face closed.

The old skulls on the wall were man-eaters, each with a name-plate and a death it had answered for. The new wing was *décor* — a generation of safe, prosperous young hunters, raised behind walls the war had made secure, going out into a quieted forest and bringing back heads for status. A denning mother-beast that had threatened no one, taken with her young for a first-kill rite. Small harmless things killed for the plumage. Trophies of nothing, answering for nothing.

The village waited for the blessing. Issei stood in front of the wall of skulls for a long, terrible moment, and gave them something else.

"Your grandmothers' wall is holy," he said, "and this one is garbage, and I'm going to tell you the difference, because your grandmothers aren't here to."

The gacha gave the speech in full, and the theatre sat still for it. He did not shame the village's history — he honored it, name by name, the four hundred years of standing between the forest and the children: *that was the jungle's law and you kept it and it kept you.* And then he drew the line where a dragon had taught him to draw it. "Hunting fed you. Hunting guarded you. Hunting is what the strong owe the weak, and it's clean — as long as the kill is for the *walls.*" He pointed at the old wing, then the new. "The moment it's for the *wall*, you're not hunters anymore. You're just the thing your grandmothers built this town to stop, with better manners. That mother out there wasn't your enemy. She was your neighbor, and you killed her for a story to tell, and no animal in that whole cursed forest has ever once done anything that low. Cruelty isn't the jungle. Cruelty is what people bring *into* the jungle." He let that stand. Then, quieter: "Keep hunting. I'll train your watch myself — things are going to come out of that forest again someday, and I want this town standing when they do. But from tonight: you kill what threatens you, you kill it fast, you use what you kill or you burn it skyward and you *remember it* — and the new wall comes down. Not because I said so. Because you're hunters, and hunters don't keep garbage."

The village took the wall down that night. The vision ended on the fire — a whole town at attention around a column of flame, learning a rite older than every nation watching, and Issei in the front of the crowd with his hand on a young trophy-taker's shoulder, teaching him where to look.

---

"There it is," said Blade, and stood. "That's the pull I've been waiting thirty-three chapters for." He turned to face the rows, sword-point grounded like a chairman's gavel. "Everybody down front carries a code. Mine's kept me on the right side of a hunger for a long lifetime, so believe me on this one thing: a code you only keep when it's easy is a costume. That kid just walked into a room full of people who love him, holding a festival in his honor, and told them their new wall was garbage — and *then* offered to train their watch, because the code isn't there to make him feel clean, it's there to keep the town alive. A code you enforce on your own side, at your own cost, when nobody's making you?" He sat back down. "That gear holds. I'd work with him tonight."

"The Jabari," announced M'Baku, in the voice he used for pronouncements he had decided the whole theatre deserved, "apologize to the prey. We thank it, we waste nothing of it, and we mock anyone who mounts heads — which is to say we mock the lowlanders *constantly.*" He gestured at the screen with a fistful of festival snacks. "The dragon would make a passable Jabari. This is the highest review I have issued in thirty-three chapters. T'Challa, note it in whatever Wakanda uses instead of a soul."

"Noted," said T'Challa, dry, and then, seriously: "My country's power began with a hunt — the goddess, the herb, the first Black Panther. And the first law Bast gave was not *be strong.* It was *the strength is for them.* Every colonizer who ever came for Wakanda came with trophies of my continent on his walls; I know exactly which side of the boy's line those men lived on. He has re-derived, alone, on a snowy ridge, the thing my nation took a goddess and ten thousand years to codify: predation with stewardship is guardianship. Predation without it is only appetite with a flag."

"The spear is for the people behind you," said Okoye, simply. "He holds it correctly. That is the entire review."

Frank Castle had been silent through all of it, and the front rows had learned what that particular silence was for.

"Say it, Frank," said Blade.

"Respect your prey," said Frank, flatly, "is a bedtime story." He didn't stand this time; he just aimed the words at the screen like he was zeroing them. "The man-eater didn't feel honored. The fire's not *for* the dead thing — dead things don't have a point of view, that's what dead means. Kid could skip the funeral and the target's exactly as neutralized. All that ritual is sentiment, and sentiment's a luxury, and here's the operational problem with it: the day the prayer makes you slow — the day you're lining up a shot on something that's about to eat a kid and some piece of you is already composing the eulogy — that's the day the eulogy's for the kid. Respect the living. The dead don't file complaints."

"You're wrong," said Elsa Bloodstone, boots coming off the chair, "and you're wrong in a way I can *document*, because my father agreed with you." Her voice had the hoarse edge it only got on family topics. "Ulysses Bloodstone: no rites, no last words, mouths are for trophies, sentiment gets you killed. Maximum efficiency, thirty years, never slowed down once. And I have read every journal the man left, and I watched what he was at the end, and I'm telling you the rites aren't *for the prey*, Frank, they were never for the prey — they're maintenance. On the *weapon.* The fire is how the kid checks, every single kill, that he still knows the difference between a receipt and a collection." She jabbed a thumb at the screen. "You said it yourself in Chapter Fifteen — he pays the bill in private, in full, and his ledger balances. The funeral *is* the bookkeeping. Skip it for thirty years and you get my father's wall, and then you get my father."

Frank thought about that for a long moment, the way he did, like the argument was a structure he was clearing room by room.

"Maintenance, I'll allow," he said at last. "Calibration, I'll allow — I said it about the Gremory girl's last-words rule and I'll extend it to the fire. But I'm keeping the asterisk, same one as before, and this arc's coming for it fast: it's easy to respect your prey when your prey has claws. His whole pretty code got built on monsters that look like monsters." He nodded once at the dark screen. "Chapter Fifteen I said one day the thing eating kids would have a pulse and a face and paperwork, and I said I'd want to see that chapter. Machine's been listening since Thirteen." He settled back. "So roll it."

The machine, which had indeed been listening, rolled it.

The lights had barely begun their end-of-chapter dim when the screen flickered up its parting gift: a starfield, a station, a docking manifest scrolling in cold system font — weights, lots, cage dimensions — and over a hold full of small frightened faces, a single stamped word: **LIVESTOCK.**

The Ravager rows came out of their seats as one body. Yondu was standing with his arrow already floating an inch off his hip, fin blazing, and nobody down front said a word about sentiment.

**NEXT: THE SLAVE MARKET.**

"Jungle's got one law," said Rocket, very quietly, into the dark. "That ain't it."
