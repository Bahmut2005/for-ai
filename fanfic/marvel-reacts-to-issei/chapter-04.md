# Chapter Four: His First Death

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Street-Level Heroes • Frank Castle • Wolverine • Midnight Suns

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Raynare's Betrayal Replay" — **SSR**
2. "Asia's Healing Hands" — **Rare**

---

The theatre was still warm from the Balance Breaker.

That was the only way to describe the mood, and everyone felt it even if no one said it. Three pulls in, the room had begun — cautiously, against its collective better judgment — to enjoy itself. Tony Stark and Reed Richards had spent the intermission sketching competing models of the Boost mechanism on a napkin that Doom kept correcting without being asked. Peter Parker had started a betting pool on what the armor's next form would look like. Somewhere in the Ravager block, someone had figured out that if you complained about the concessions loudly enough, the theatre produced concessions, and the discovery had done more for inter-factional relations than a decade of summits.

The boy on the screen had been murdered, resurrected, humiliated, and then — mountain by mountain, boost by boost — he had begun to become something, and the room had begun, in its fractious way, to root for him.

So when the great wheel of light spun up for the fourth time, ticking around and around with its roulette clatter, the dread had mostly gone out of the sound. The wheel was almost friendly now. The wheel gave you things.

The wheel stopped. The screen flooded gold — top rarity, the good stuff, and a ragged cheer actually started in the cheap seats before the letters finished stamping themselves across the light:

**"RAYNARE'S BETRAYAL REPLAY" — RARITY: SSR.**

The cheer died in its crib.

"No," said Peter Parker, quietly, to the screen, the way you'd talk to a misbehaving vending machine. "No, we — we saw that one. We already saw that one. You can't pull the same—"

"It's a gacha, kid," Rocket said, and for once there was no joke riding on the words. "Duplicates are the whole *point*."

In the back rows, Frank Castle had gone very still. He did not swear, and he did not stand, and anyone watching only his hands would have seen nothing at all. But Matt Murdock, four sections away, heard the change in him — heard a heartbeat that had been a slow patient engine drop into something slower and flatter still, the cadence of a man closing all the doors inside himself in advance, because he knew exactly what was about to come through the walls.

The theatre took its breath, and swallowed them.

---

It was worse the second time, and it was worse for a reason nobody had braced for: this time, the vision didn't begin with Issei.

It began with *her*.

Raynare, three weeks earlier, sitting on a power line above a school crossing like a crow in a fetish catalogue, watching a scrawny second-year walk home below with his hands in his pockets, and the theatre was folded inside her boredom the way they had once been folded inside his dying. They heard her thoughts as they had heard his, and her thoughts were worse, because they were so *administrative*. A surveillance assignment. A name on a list passed down from somewhere above her: *Issei Hyoudou, possible Sacred Gear, observe, and if the Gear proves dangerous — remove him.* They watched her watch him for days, filing him under harmless, filing him under tedious, and then they watched the second order arrive, and they watched her decide — the way you'd decide to take the shorter route home — that observation was more work than a spear.

They watched her rehearse. That was the detail that would keep half the street-level section awake for weeks. Yuuma Amano practicing her shy smile in a cracked mirror in an abandoned church on a hill, adjusting it, softening it, the way an angler files a hook. Choosing the uniform. Choosing the sunset. Choosing the *fountain*, because the sound of falling water covers a lot, and because parks empty out at dusk.

"She built it," Jessica Jones said, hollow. "The whole thing. It wasn't even cruelty. It was *prep*."

Then the date again — compressed now, mercifully, the theatre skipping like a stone across water it had already drowned them in once. The café. The cinema. The cheap pink scrunchie, and this time they saw her side of that moment: saw her pause in front of it deliberately, counting two full seconds, because a target who buys you a gift stops checking his exits. The room made a low, sick collective sound.

And then the fountain, and the failing light, and the whisper.

The vision did not linger on the spear this time. It had made its point about the spear. Instead — and this was the new cruelty, the SSR cruelty, the reason for the gold — as the boy folded down onto the gravel, the frame *split*. Half the screen stayed with him, bleeding out under the fountain spray with his small, unprofound, unbearable last thoughts. The other half followed Raynare into the sky, and stayed with her, and let the theatre hear that her mind had already moved on. Not to guilt. Not even to satisfaction. To *dinner*. To a mild complaint about the humidity. To the next item on her list, which — the vision let this hang for one poisonous second before it faded — was a name the theatre didn't recognize yet: *the Argento girl arrives Tuesday.*

And beneath both halves, small, at the bottom of the frame, like a footnote: a kitchen in a suburban house, and a mother setting a third place at the table, and leaving it set, and looking at the clock.

The vision ended.

---

Nobody spoke for a long time. The theatre's ambient light came up soft and reddish, the house lights of a place that had learned, apparently, something like tact.

Matt Murdock had his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands.

"I keep listening to it," he said finally, to Foggy's ghost, to Father Lantom's ghost, to nobody in the seats around him, though Luke and Jessica and Danny were all close enough to hear. "Her heart. It never moved. Three weeks, a whole — a whole *courtship*, and it never once moved. I've listened to killers my entire life. The worst ones spike when they do it. Something in the body knows, even when the man doesn't." He lifted his head, and behind the round dark glasses his ruined eyes were aimed at the dark screen like an accusation. "Hers didn't. That wasn't a murder to her. It was a *chore*. Somebody upstream wrote a memo, and a seventeen-year-old bled out by a fountain, and her pulse never moved."

"Orders," Luke Cage said heavily. "You catch that? *Observe, and if the Gear proves dangerous.* Somebody signed off on that kid."

"Somebody always signs off," said Jessica.

Three rows back and a world away, Logan sat with his forearms on the seat in front of him, and the claws were not out, and somehow that was worse. The people who knew him best — and Kurt Wagner, sitting beside him, knew him best — understood that Logan with the claws out was Logan handling it.

"Kid asked her to repeat it," Logan said at last, low, gravel dragged over gravel. "You clock that? *Would you die for me* — and he laughs, and he asks her to say it again. Because it ain't in him yet. The thing that hears the question under the question." He turned his head slightly; the reddish light caught the old, old fatigue in his face. "I've died more times than anybody in this room's had birthdays, and I'll tell you the part they never put in the reports: the first one's the only one that's *yours*. Every death after, some piece of you saw it comin'. The first one gets taken *from* you." A pause. "They took his in a park. On his first date. Over a memo."

"Logan—" Kurt began gently.

"An' one more thing." Logan's voice didn't rise. It sank, which from Logan was the dangerous direction. "*Blame God for putting a Sacred Gear in you.* That's what she says to him. That's the exit line. Kid gets born with somethin' in his arm he never asked for, never even heard of, and the people who *did* know decide that's a killin' offense." His jaw worked once. "I know that story. They ran that program on me. Called it Weapon X 'stead of Sacred Gear, but it's the same damn memo."

Nobody had anything to say to that.

Frank Castle said nothing at all, and that was its own event, and the people nearest him were careful not to make it one. He had watched the split screen to its end — had watched the half with the boy, and the half with the bored departing killer, and the footnote with the mother and the third plate — without moving. It was the fountain that did it, if anything did. He had been in a park once, on an ordinary bright day, with a carousel instead of a fountain, and he knew with a precision no one else in the building could match exactly how long the walk home takes for the family of someone who dies in a park because of business conducted over their head. He knew about the plate left set. He knew about the clock.

Karen Page — several seats down, watching him sidelong the way you watch weather — saw his lips move once, silently, and could not read them. Matt, who could hear a whisper across a stadium, could. It was two words, and the first one was a number.

"*Twice now*," Frank Castle said, to Raynare, wherever she was, in whatever cosmology. He had marked her in Chapter One's darkness, quietly, a name added to the ledger he kept where other men kept prayers. The replay had not changed the entry. It had underlined it.

Up in the Midnight Suns' block, the Ghost Rider was burning about a foot taller than usual, and Blade had shifted one seat away with the unhurried pragmatism of a man whose coat was flammable.

"Predator pattern," Blade said, to the group, clipped and clinical, because clinical was how he stayed level. "Textbook. Surveil, groom, isolate, kill, exit. I've dusted vampires who ran that exact play for four hundred years." His jaw tightened. "Difference is, they needed to eat."

"She didn't hunger," the Rider said. Its voice was the sound of a furnace door. The flame bent, all of it, toward the dark screen, as though something up there might catch. "There is no hunger in her to punish. Only *convenience*. Innocent blood, spilled to shorten a workday." The empty sockets flared white. "She will look into my eyes. In this universe or that one. It is already true; it is merely not yet *happened*."

Illyana Rasputina sat at the end of the row with her legs crossed and the Soulsword loose across her knees, and she had watched the grooming — the rehearsed smile, the counted seconds at the scrunchie stand — with the flat, ancient expression of a woman auditing a demon's homework.

"Limbo would have been kinder," she said, to no one in particular. "In Limbo, at least, the thing that lures a child *looks* like a monster if you squint. She practiced her face in a mirror." A beat. "I was taken by an expert too. I recognize the workmanship."

And that might have been where the room stayed — down in the cold, with the ledger-keepers and the mourners — except that the screen, which had a showman's instincts and a torturer's timing, chose that exact moment to spin the wheel again.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

The wheel ticked around, and around, and stopped on a modest, unassuming silver-green. No gold. No fanfare.

**"ASIA'S HEALING HANDS" — RARITY: RARE.**

"*The Argento girl arrives Tuesday,*" Matt Murdock quoted softly, going cold. "That's her. That's the next name on the list."

The theatre took its breath.

---

She was small, and blonde, and she thanked the train conductor twice, and she got lost immediately.

The theatre watched Asia Argento arrive in Kuoh with one battered suitcase and a nun's habit and a sense of direction that could most charitably be described as devotional — as though she trusted every street to be the right one — and the room, wrung out and raw, did not know what to do with her. She was sixteen. She apologized to a vending machine. When the wind took her veil, the boy who caught it for her — brown hair, brown eyes, a face that had never once played poker — handed it back and got a smile of such undefended sweetness that the entire audience heard Issei Hyoudou's internal monologue *stall*, and for once, not a single lecherous thought crawled out of the wreckage. He walked her to the church road. She healed the scraped knee of a crying boy in the park on the way — green light, warm as an oath, pooling from her ringed hands, and the wound closed like it was being forgiven.

*Twilight Healing,* the vision supplied. *Sacred Gear. Rarity: exceptional.*

"Oh no," said Kamala Khan, from the middle rows, in the voice of someone who had read enough stories to know what narrative sweetness this concentrated was *for*.

Because then the vision gave them her past, and it did it the way it had given them Raynare's preparation: patiently.

An infant left on the steps of a church. A little girl raised by nuns, devout the way only the abandoned are devout — loving the institution with her whole chest because the institution was the only thing that had ever picked her up. Eight years old, and a dying puppy healed in her cupped hands, and the sisters weeping, and the word *miracle*, and then the word *holy maiden*, and pilgrim lines around the block. They watched her heal the sick for years and take no coin and want nothing except to be useful to the God who had, as she understood it, saved her first.

And then they watched a wounded man stagger into her chapel after hours, bleeding badly, and they watched her heal him because he was bleeding and she could stop it — and they watched the wings come out of his back afterward, black-feathered, and the *thank you* he sneered on his way out the broken window.

And they watched the Church find out.

It was very fast. That was the obscenity of it — the deliberation took less time than her average healing. Her power worked on Devils. Therefore her power was not a miracle but a *defect*. Therefore the maiden was not holy but a witch, a heretic, an instrument of corruption wearing a saint's face. The theatre watched men in fine vestments, men she had called Father, explain to a sixteen-year-old that the gift they had built pilgrim lines around was proof of her impurity, because mercy — *their* mercy, correctly administered — was supposed to check credentials first. They watched her cast out with her one suitcase. They watched doors close, and then a whole continent of doors, and they watched her keep praying anyway, every night, to the God whose ground crew had thrown her away, thanking Him for the day.

They watched the only hands extended to her afterward belong to fallen angels. To a congregation squatting in an abandoned church on a hill above Kuoh — a building with a cracked mirror in it that half the theatre recognized with a full-body chill.

The vision surfaced, briefly, into the present: Issei Hyoudou, hearing this story from Asia over a shared lunch she was visibly rationing to make last, on the happiest afternoon of her entire remembered life because someone her own age had bought her food and asked her questions. And the room got to watch, from the inside, the exact moment the boy's newborn devil heart — perverted, ridiculous, unkillably kind — did the arithmetic on everything she wasn't saying, and arrived at a fury so clean and so total that Ddraig stirred in his arm like a banked fire feeling wind.

*She heals people,* the boy was thinking, over and over, stuck on it, unable to move past it. *That's the whole crime. She heals people TOO WELL. Who does that? Who throws away a person for that? I'll — she's going to have friends. She's going to have so many friends. Starting with me. Whatever's up on that hill, whatever they want her for—*

The vision ended there. Mid-fury. Deliberately.

---

Kurt Wagner was on his feet before the house lights finished coming up.

He didn't say anything at first. He stood in the aisle with his tail lashing slowly, a devout man with a demon's face and a rosary wrapped twice around his three-fingered hand, and everyone near enough to see him understood that they were watching a man have an argument with himself and lose in both directions.

"I want to say," he began finally, and his voice carried the way a trained acrobat's voice carries, up and out, "that this is not my Church. And it isn't. Wrong cosmology, wrong Heaven, wrong — everything. Their God apparently issues *equipment*." A ripple of weak laughter; he let it die. "But I would be lying before God — mine, anyone's — if I pretended I did not recognize the *shape* of it. An institution built to carry mercy, deciding instead to *ration* it. I have been the thing on the wrong side of that door, my friends. I have the face for it." He held up the rosary. "And I keep this anyway. So I need to say the rest out loud, or it will stay in my chest all night: they did not cast her out for healing devils. They cast her out because her mercy did not stop where their map did. And mercy that stops where the map stops—" his voice dropped, "—is not mercy. It is *policy*."

"Amen," said, of all people, Jessica Jones.

Matt Murdock stood up slowly, one hand on the seatback, and the room made space for him the way rooms did.

"I've spent my whole life," he said, "afraid that God keeps a ledger on me. Who I saved. Who I hit. Whether I enjoyed it. Every confession, I'm — negotiating the line items." A humorless breath. "And the one thing I never doubted, the *floor* under all of it, was that grace doesn't check ID. That it lands on the just and the unjust like rain, that's the whole — that's the *point*, that's the scandal of it, that's why it's good news and not a rewards program." He turned his blind eyes toward the dark screen, toward the memory of men in vestments. "That little girl is the only person in her entire story who understood her own religion. They excommunicated the one working part."

"Okay, but can I—" Kamala Khan was standing now too, hand half-raised out of sheer classroom reflex before she caught herself and put it down, cheeks flaming, voice steadying as she went, because this was the one subject on which she never wobbled. "I'm not Catholic, obviously, I'm coming at this from a whole different direction, but we have this teaching — it's in the Quran, my dad had it on the wall — that if you save one life, it's as if you saved all of mankind. *One life.* It doesn't come with an asterisk. There's no footnote that says *terms and conditions apply, check species first.*" She looked around at the assembled multiverse, sixteen years old and absolutely unintimidated by it, because being right does that. "She's a kid. Adults built a whole system, and pointed it at a kid, and when the system met one person it couldn't categorize, it decided *she* was the malfunction. That's not faith. Faith is what *she's* still doing — praying to a God whose middle management fired her. That's the strongest person we've seen on that screen, and I'm including the guy who erased a mountain."

From the Midnight Suns' block, the furnace-door voice.

"You are all arguing about the girl," the Ghost Rider said. "The girl requires no argument. The flame does not even *flicker* toward her — do you understand what that means? I have looked at saints and found sin. There is none in her. None." The skull turned, slowly, toward the screen. "But the men who cast her out fed a healer to the fallen and called it doctrine. And innocent blood does not care what collar the guilty wear." The flames leaned. "I have penance enough for a parish."

"Get in line," said Frank Castle, from the back, not loudly.

It was the first thing he'd said aloud all chapter, and the room heard it, and nobody laughed, because it hadn't been a joke. He was looking at the screen with the patient, terrible attention he had once reserved for dockyards and cartel warehouses, and he was thinking about a girl with a suitcase who had been thrown away by every structure that existed to protect her, and about the next name on a bored killer's list, and about the words *the Argento girl arrives Tuesday*, and the ledger where he kept names had, without ceremony, gained a second column.

Blade broke the silence, dry as dust. "For what it's worth — I've burned churches like that abandoned one before. The kind that keeps a congregation of fallen things and one innocent girl they're saving for *something*." He let that word sit there, doing its work. "They're never saving her for nothing."

"He knows," Logan said.

Everyone looked at him.

"The kid." Logan nodded at the screen, at the fury the vision had ended on. "You heard him. *Whatever's up on that hill.* He's got a two-week-old resurrection, one arm that works right, and a hit squad of fallen angels between him and a girl he met at *lunch*." The old man almost smiled, and it was the first warm thing in the theatre in an hour. "And he's goin' anyway. Tonight, if I read him right."

The screen, showman to its bones, chose that moment to flicker.

One frame only, white on black, gone almost before it landed — the same handwriting, so to speak, as the message from before, *HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT*. This one was shorter. This one said:

**NEXT: THE CHURCH ON THE HILL.**

And underneath it, small, in the corner, like a countdown that had already started:

**TUESDAY.**

Somewhere in the dark, Kurt Wagner began, very quietly, to pray — for a girl in another cosmology, to a God who he had to believe was bigger than any one map. Kamala Khan, who prayed differently, prayed too. Frank Castle did not pray. He waited, the way he was built to, for Tuesday.
