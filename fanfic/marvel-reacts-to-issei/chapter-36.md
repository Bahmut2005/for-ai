# Chapter Thirty-Six: The Tyrant He Did Not Spare

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Punisher • Daredevil • Winter Soldier • Villains • Heroes

**Gacha Pulls:**

2. "Funeral for the Slain" — **Secret**

---

The argument from the night before had never actually ended.

It had merely lost its microphone. When the lights had come up on the previous pull — on Vashtar, the Iron Duke, kneeling in the wreck of his own throne room and being *spared*, and on the Secret coda showing the Duke ten years on, grey and diligent, governing the Western March with a penitent's obsessive care while one of his old siege-generals slipped the leash and vanished into the border dark — the theatre had split down an old, old seam, and the seam had kept arguing in the aisles long after the screen went grey.

"He reformed," Matt Murdock had said, four separate times, to four separate people. "You all saw it. The man reformed. Mercy *worked*."

"The general," Frank Castle had answered, every time, in the same voice he used for weather. "Watch the general."

Now the steel-grey band of Arc Four lit along the ceiling, the wheel descended, and Frank Castle — who had not visibly slept, because Frank never visibly slept — leaned forward in the back rows with his forearms on his knees, like a man waiting for a bill he had already calculated to the cent.

The wheel ticked around and stopped on blue.

**"IMMEDIATE EXECUTION OF A SECOND TYRANT" — RARITY: RARE.**

"Rare," said Sam Wilson, reading it twice. "The machine's got him ending a king filed two tiers under *breakfast*."

"That is not a comment on the king," said Doom, from his aisle. "It is a comment on how often the boy is asked to choose. Attend."

The theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

The Ash March, the vision named it. A border territory in the Underworld's frontier dark, orphaned by the war, and the theatre knew the man on its scavenged throne before the system finished the introduction, because they had seen him once already, standing at the Iron Duke's shoulder in the Secret coda: *Malzeth. Former siege-general of the Western March. Self-styled King of the Ash.*

He had learned all the wrong lessons from his old master's fall. Vashtar had built armies; Malzeth built *stockpiles*. And the vision walked the audience through his arsenal with the system's flattest, most clinical captioning, down a long hall his engineers called the orchard, and the theatre understood, one row at a time, in a spreading sick silence, what the extraction cradles were for.

Sacred Gears cannot be manufactured. They are born in people — seeded in children, without pattern, peasant and noble alike. And they can be *removed*, if you have the old rites, and do not care that removal kills the host.

The cradles were small. All of them were small. Children's Gears, the caption explained, come out cleanest — the roots are shallow before adolescence. Rows of cradles, rows of little shapes under sheets, and at the hall's far end a rack of extracted Gears glowing softly in velvet, cataloged, ready to be socketed into Malzeth's officers like stolen jewelry.

In the theatre, in the front row, Asia Argento made one small sound and pressed both hands over her sternum — over the exact place where, once, on an altar, a laughing fallen angel had pulled Twilight Healing out of her living body and let her die on the stones. Rias's arm was around her before the sound finished.

"He knows," Koneko said quietly, to no one, watching the vision's sky. "He already knows what this is. He's felt it from the inside of losing her."

Then the wall of the orchard ceased to exist.

There was no siege. There was no ultimatum, no herald, no ninety-second rain of declarations the theatre had come to expect from him. There was Issei Hyoudou in the Welsh Dragonic Rook's massive crimson plate, walking out of the burning breach with the survivors' cradle-straps already cut behind him, and the vision's timestamp — the system had never before shown a timestamp — running cold in the corner of the sky.

Malzeth was a veteran. He read the armor, read the green gem, read his own odds, and did the intelligent thing, the *proven* thing, the thing that had saved his old master's life in front of this very audience one night ago. He dropped his blade. He went down on both knees in the wreckage, arms spread, head bowed, and began the words.

"I yield. Whatever penance—"

The timestamp said the walk across the hall took one point eight seconds. Ascalon was in Issei's hand for perhaps half of that. The theatre had watched this boy fight for thirty-five chapters — had watched him bleed out rounds he could have ended early, take punches to make a point, monologue at archdevils, weep over enemies — and so the theatre possessed, collectively, the exact expertise required to understand what it was seeing now, which was none of those things.

The holy sword went through him once.

No speech. No verdict read aloud. Not one word — the vision was very clear on this, and later, in the arguments, people would keep returning to it — Issei Hyoudou did not say *anything*. The King of the Ash folded into the cinders of his own orchard mid-sentence, and the Red Dragon Emperor was already turning away, already kneeling at the nearest cradle, already gentle, the gauntlet voice dropping to the register the theatre had heard him use on frightened children in a burning slave market two chapters gone.

**[TRANSFER.]**

Power flooded away from him, down the link to Asia's ring, into healing light for the seven small bodies that were still breathing. He stayed on his knees in the ash for a long time, an armored mountain making itself low and harmless, saying *you're okay, you're okay, we've got you, don't look at that side of the room*.

And Ddraig's voice, old and quiet, closed the vision like a door:

"**Partner does not negotiate over a nest.**"

---

The lights came up on a theatre that did not erupt. It *pressurized*.

"Clean," said Frank Castle.

That single word landed in the silence like a dropped magazine, and Matt Murdock came around in his seat. "Don't."

"Clean," Frank said again, without heat. "One motion, no fear pause, no anger tell, blade already moving before the target finished the first clause. I've watched him spare people for thirty-five chapters and I clocked it as softness. It wasn't softness." He looked at the frozen frame — the kneeling king, the sword. "It was a *line*. He always had a line. Last night your Duke knelt on the right side of it. This one knelt on the wrong side, and the kneeling stopped mattering." A beat. "Told you to watch the general. That kill is the bill for Chapter Thirty-Five, and the kid paid it."

"That kill is the bill for *the orchard*," Matt said, and his voice was low and shaking with the effort of precision. "Don't you dare fold them together. The Duke repented and ten years of the Western March proves mercy was correct. The general chose the cradles all by himself." He stopped. His jaw worked. "But I heard it, Frank. I heard it the way I heard *her*, in Chapter One. Malzeth's heart while he begged. It was calm. He was reading a script that worked on the Duke's judge and expected it to work twice. The repentance was counterfeit."

"So the kid made the right call."

"The kid didn't *make* a call!" Matt was on his feet now. "That's what none of you are seeing. He didn't listen and then decide. He didn't check. Yesterday he stood in front of a kneeling tyrant for ten minutes, weighing a soul, and it was the hardest thing we've watched him do. Today he crossed that hall in under two seconds, which means the verdict was in before the door came down — before any plea, true or false, could have reached him. What if it *had* been true? What if that man had knelt there with a wrecked, hammering, honest heart?"

"Then he'd be an honest man who harvested children," said the Winter Soldier.

Bucky Barnes had not spoken all chapter. He rarely did on kill nights; the theatre had learned to leave him the silence. Now he stood with his flesh hand in his pocket and the metal one resting on the seat back, and he spoke slowly, like a man walking a perimeter he'd walked before.

"I've been what you're afraid he is," he said. "A verdict that arrives before the door opens. No pause, no check, one motion, clean — I know exactly what that looks like from behind the trigger, and you're right to be scared of it, Murdock, and you're wrong about what you saw. Because the difference was never the speed." He nodded at the screen. "It's *whose line it is*. Every kill I made for seventy years, somebody else had drawn the line and I was just the pencil. That boy drew his own. Out loud. In front of everyone, chapter after chapter — he spares the repentant, he forgives his own murderers' colleagues, and the defenseless are *fenced off*, and everyone in his world has been told where the fence is." The metal hand flexed once. "A man who kills at random is a monster. A man who kills on orders is a weapon. A man with one fence, who tells you where it is, and never moves it in his own favor — that's neither. I don't have a word for it. But I sleep better knowing it exists."

"The word," said Steve Rogers quietly, "is the argument. Execution or protection. Pick one, and everything downstream changes."

"Protection," said half the room.

"Execution," said the other half, and not all of that half meant it as an accusation.

---

It was the villains' tier, of course, that did the mathematics.

"Observe what we have actually been given," Doom said, and the debate bent toward him whether it wanted to or not, because Doom on a dais was still Doom. "Two nights. Two kneeling tyrants. One variable changed. Chapter Thirty-Five: crimes of war, of conquest, of ambition — the window for repentance stands open, and genuine contrition is *honored*. Tonight: crimes against children — and there is no window. There was never a window. He did not close it in anger; it does not exist on that side of the fence. We have been handed," and the mask tilted, "a completed map of the Red Dragon Emperor's jurisprudence. Mercy is a door with exactly one lock."

"And every being in this room now holds the key's *negative*," Loki murmured, silk over wire. "Knowing precisely what triggers a dragon's lethality is priceless intelligence — and utterly symmetrical. It tells you what never to touch." He examined his nails. "I note, for the archive, that hostage-craft is dead as a discipline where this boy is concerned. A child in your hand does not buy you leverage over him. It buys you one point eight seconds."

Bullseye whistled at the frozen frame, the technician's whistle he'd given Raynare's spear a lifetime of chapters ago. "Half-second draw inside a walk. No wind-up. I've killed a lot of people slower than that man died." Nobody near him moved seats this time. They were all doing the same arithmetic.

And the Green Goblin, who had laughed at the fountain in Chapter One, who laughed at everything, started to laugh now — at the kneeling, at the mid-sentence — and stopped. Alone. The sound died in the villains' dark like something small falling down a well, and Norman Osborn sat very still with the map Doom had just drawn spread out in his mind, locating himself on it, on the wrong side of a fence that did not have a window, and said nothing at all for the rest of the night.

"Marvelous," said Mephisto softly, to no one. "The room's worst are checking their own ledgers against his fence line. He is not even *from* here, and he is already governing them."

Magneto's voice came down from the mutant rows like a gavel. "There is nothing to debate. I have stood over the small graves this world digs and been told that mercy toward the diggers is civilization. It is not. It is bookkeeping by the unbereaved. The boy's fence is the only law I have seen in this theatre worth the name." A pause. "He is gentler than I am. He turned away from the body. I would not have."

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

The wheel spun — and the theatre went quiet in a new way, because the colors drained through blue, past purple, past gold, and stopped on the rarity that had, last time, disclaimed itself.

**"FUNERAL FOR THE SLAIN" — RARITY: SECRET.**

"The tyrant gets a funeral?" someone said, appalled.

"No," said Frank Castle, already standing, the way he stood at gravesides. "The slain aren't the tyrant."

---

Night, in the Ash March. Three days later, the caption said. A frontier village at the orchard's edge, and behind its shrine, thirty-one graves.

Small. All of them small.

The delegation from the victorious coalition had come and gone at noon with banners and correct speeches. This — the Secret — was later: one figure coming back alone up the cinder road in the dark, out of armor, in plain human clothes with his collar turned up, carrying incense and a paper bag of convenience-store offerings, because the Underworld's frontier had no shops and he had gone home to get candy. Thirty-one small sweets. He set one on each grave, and knelt in the ash in front of the rows, seiza, palms flat on his thighs, and bowed until his forehead touched the ground.

"I'm sorry," said Issei Hyoudou, to the graves. "I was slow. Three days. If I'd been three days faster—" He stopped. Started over. "You don't have to forgive me. That's not what I came for. I just didn't want your first night out here to be one where nobody came."

He stayed until dawn. That was all. That was the entire Secret: no one in his world knew — not the coalition, not the village, not the wives in the front row, whose faces the theatre could not look at directly. He had told no one, ever. The vision's only other witness was the voice in his left hand, which said, very low, near sunrise:

"**Nest law, partner. Even the ones we lose are ours.**"

And then the screen did the thing the theatre would carry out of the chapter like a stone in the chest. The machine that had failed, once, catastrophically, in front of everyone — the machine that had printed **RECORD NOT FOUND** over a child-shaped hole in a photograph — began, unprompted, to print names.

**MIRA. TESSEL. YUUNA. KOVAK. DANE...**

Thirty-one names, one at a time, unhurried, each held on screen a full breath before the next. Frank Castle stood through all of them, spine straight, the way you stand for a folded flag. Bucky stood. Steve. Matt Murdock stood with his rosary out and his face wet and his argument, for the moment, laid down. By the fifteenth name the whole theatre was on its feet — aisles, tiers, the villains' dark, Magneto with his fist over his heart — and in the front row Asia Argento, who knew from the inside exactly how those children had died, whispered every name after the screen like a litany she intended to keep.

When the thirty-first name faded, the screen held dark for a moment. Then, small and white, almost gentle:

**THIS UNIT ITEMIZES. WHERE IT IS PERMITTED.**

**SOMEWHERE, A LIST EXISTS THAT THIS UNIT CANNOT READ. HE WILL STAND BEFORE THAT, TOO.**

The wheel rose. **NEXT PULL PENDING,** it said — and beneath it, almost apologetic after the weight, a preview band flickered: a summit table, a hundred pantheon banners, and Issei Hyoudou adjusting a tie with visible terror.

"Diplomacy," said Tony Stark hoarsely, into the dark. "God help every god in that room."
