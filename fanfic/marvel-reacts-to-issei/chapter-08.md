# Chapter Eight: Asia Argento

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Nightcrawler, Daredevil, Kamala Khan, Ghost Rider, the Watcher (Living Tribunal cameo)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Asia's Trial and Execution" — Trauma-Class
2. "Issei's Protective Rage" — Super Rare

---

The theatre had developed rituals by now, the way any prison does.

Seven viewings in, the factions had settled into a geography of temperament. The Avengers held the center rows like a garrison. The X-Men kept to the left flank, close enough to the mutants of other rosters that they could murmur among themselves. Doom sat alone in a box seat that had not existed until he decided it should, and the theatre — indestructible, unknowable, apparently accommodating — had let him have it. The Watcher stood where he always stood, above and behind them all, a pale moon in the dark.

And the gacha wheel spun when it wished to, indifferent to whether anyone was ready.

Tonight it made a sound none of them had heard before.

Every previous pull had announced itself with a chime — bright for the comedic ones, a low bell for the dramatic. This one came in with a sound like a cathedral door closing from the inside. The great golden wheel above the screen turned slowly, almost reluctantly, and when it stopped, the banner that unfurled was not gold or silver or the violet of Super Rare.

It was grey. The grey of ash. The grey of stone confessionals and winter mornings.

**ASIA'S TRIAL AND EXECUTION — TRAUMA-CLASS**

"Trauma-Class," Kamala Khan read aloud, and her voice cracked on the second word. "That's — we haven't seen that rarity before. Why is there a rarity called that?"

Nobody answered her. On the far left, Kurt Wagner — Nightcrawler, teleporter, acrobat, and the most sincerely devout Catholic in any row of this theatre — had already gone very still. He had been dreading something like this since Chapter Four, since the vision of the gentle blonde girl in the white habit who healed a wounded Devil because it was bleeding and she was there. He had said, then, that her Church would not thank her for it. He had hoped, quietly and against his own knowledge of institutions, that he was wrong.

The screen lit.

---

It was not a courtroom, exactly. That was the first cruelty. A courtroom implies law, and law implies the possibility of acquittal. This was a chamber of white stone and gold trim, high windows admitting light that fell in disciplined columns, and at the far end a raised bench where five figures in ecclesiastical vestments sat like a range of mountains. Below them, small enough that the room seemed designed to make her small, stood Asia Argento.

She was sixteen years old. She had folded her hands in front of her the way children are taught to fold them for prayer, and she had not stopped folding them, because she did not know what else to do with a body that had only ever been used for kindness.

"The charge," intoned the central figure — a bishop, or something above a bishop, his voice carrying the acoustics of a man who had never once been interrupted, "is corruption of the holy gift. Twilight Healing was granted to this child by Heaven's grace, that she might mend the faithful. Witnesses attest that she has laid those same hands upon a Devil. That the wound closed. That the creature walked free."

"He was hurt," Asia said.

She said it simply, without defiance, the way you would state that water is wet. In the theatre, three hundred beings of assorted cosmic significance heard the sentence land in the white chamber like a coin dropped down a well — and heard the silence that followed it, which was worse than shouting.

"It was a *Devil*, child."

"He was hurt," she said again, and now her voice trembled, because she was beginning to understand that the truth was not going to save her, and the truth was all she had brought. "I didn't — I didn't ask what he was. He was bleeding. The gift works when someone is bleeding. I thought — " Her folded hands tightened. "I thought that was what it was *for*."

One of the tribunal, an older woman with a face like a locked reliquary, leaned forward. "The gift discriminates in nothing. That is precisely the evidence against it. Heaven's true grace would recoil from the unclean. Yours embraces them. What does that tell us of its source, girl? What does that tell us of *you*?"

In the theatre, Matt Murdock made a sound low in his throat.

Daredevil had no eyes to watch the screen with, but the theatre — cruel or generous, no one had decided — translated everything for him: heartbeats, breath, the acoustic architecture of the chamber. So he heard what the others could only see. He heard that Asia Argento's heart was hammering like a rabbit's and that she was not lying, had never lied, possibly did not fully understand how. And he heard the five heartbeats on the bench: slow, regular, untroubled. Men and women sleeping soundly inside their own verdict.

"They've already decided," he said quietly. "This isn't a trial. Listen to them. There's no doubt anywhere in that room except hers. She's the only one up there interrogating her own soul, and she's the defendant."

"That is how such proceedings work," said Doom, from his box, without inflection. "The verdict precedes the evidence. The ceremony exists to sanctify what power has already chosen. Doom has presided over more honest tribunals." It was impossible to tell if this was condemnation or professional assessment. With Doom it was frequently both.

On screen, the questioning ground on. They asked her when the corruption had entered her. They asked whether she had felt pleasure when the Devil's wound closed. They asked her to renounce the act — not to apologize, note, but to *renounce* it, to stand in front of God and her own memory and declare that mercy had been a sin.

And Asia Argento, who had been found on a church doorstep as an infant, who had been raised entirely inside these walls, who had healed the sick in pilgrim lines for years and been called a holy maiden by the very mouths now weighing her — Asia Argento looked up at the five mountains and did the bravest and most fatal thing available to her.

"If he came back hurt," she said, very softly, "I would heal him again. I'm sorry. I would. I don't know how to see someone bleeding and decide they deserve it."

The gavel — there was a gavel, or something liturgical that served as one — came down.

The vision was merciful in the only way it knew how to be: it did not linger on what came next in flesh. It gave them the shape of it instead. The word *excommunicate*, pronounced with the satisfaction of a completed equation. The white habit taken from her. The doors of the only home she had ever known closing with her on the wrong side of them, and — the screen darkening at its edges like old film burning — figures waiting for her out in that cold, figures with black wings, because when a Church throws away a holy maiden, there are always collectors who come for the salvage. A fallen angel's smile. A ruined chapel. An altar being prepared, and a ritual that would take the Twilight Healing out of Asia Argento the way you take a pearl out of an oyster, which is to say: fatally.

*Execution*, the banner had said. The tribunal had merely opened the door. Raynare — the same fallen angel who had put a spear of light through Issei Hyoudou on his first date — was going to walk through it.

The screen dimmed to grey and held there, like a sky deciding whether to snow.

---

For a long moment, nobody in the theatre spoke.

Then Nightcrawler stood up. He did not teleport — no *bamf*, no sulfur — he simply stood, tail lashing once, and gripped the seat-back in front of him with both three-fingered hands, and anyone close could see he was shaking.

"I want to say they are not the Church," he said. His accent thickened when he was upset; it was thick now. "I want to say it very badly. I have spent my whole life saying it. Every time men in vestments did something monstrous, I said: that is not the faith, that is men *wearing* the faith, the way a wolf wears wool. And I believe that. I still — " He stopped. Started again. "I still believe that. But I watched five ordained souls look at the purest act of grace I have ever seen — she healed an enemy *because he was bleeding*, mein Gott, that is not heresy, that is the *entire Gospel* — and they called it corruption. Because it did not check credentials first. Because her mercy would not stay inside the fence they built for it."

"So which is it, Kurt?" Daredevil's voice came flat and tired from three rows down. He hadn't stood. He sat with his elbows on his knees, head bowed, the posture of a man in a pew at the back of a church he can't decide whether to leave. "Does the institution's cruelty indict the faith, or betray it? Because I've been running that question my whole life and I'll be honest with you, the ledger's ugly. I grew up on Hail Marys and guilt. I've met priests who were the best men I ever knew and I've met the other kind, and the Church protected the other kind more often than it protected kids like her." He lifted his head toward the grey screen. "She quoted their own doctrine back at them with her whole life — *I don't know how to see someone bleeding and decide they deserve it* — and they excommunicated her for it. Tell me that's a betrayal of the faith. Go ahead. Some days I believe it. Some days I think the cruelty *is* load-bearing. That the institution needs a fence more than it needs the grace, because grace doesn't take collections."

"Matthew." Kurt's voice was gentle, and it wobbled. "You still go to Mass."

"Yeah." Daredevil laughed once, without any humor in it. "Yeah, I do. Because the alternative is deciding she was wrong, and I can't. Somebody built something true enough that a girl raised inside its worst machinery came out the other end *like that*. Whatever made Asia Argento, I'm not ready to call it rotten. But the men on that bench drink from the same well I do, and that keeps me up at night."

It was Kamala Khan who cut through it, and she did it the way she did most things: by being seventeen and unable to pretend a complicated thing was more complicated than it was.

"Okay, but — can I just —" She was on her feet too now, hands doing that thing where they got slightly bigger without her noticing, which happened when she was upset. "You're both doing theology and I get it, I do, but from where I'm sitting? I'm Muslim. Different book, different arguments, some of the same ugly history, believe me, I've heard the aunties. And here's the thing my faith taught me, and I'd bet money hers taught her the exact same thing, because it's on like page one of *every single one of them*: whoever saves a life, it's as if they saved all of humanity. Full stop. No footnote that says 'humans only.' No asterisk. *She read the assignment.* She's maybe the only person in that whole building who read the assignment. And they failed her for it." Her voice went hoarse. "You don't need a tribunal to figure out who was holy in that room. It's not a hard question. It's only hard if your job depends on it being hard."

From the shadows at the back — where he preferred to sit, because the flames off his skull disturbed the civilians — the Ghost Rider spoke for the first time that evening.

Johnny Blaze's voice, when the Rider used it, came out sounding like a slow fire moving through a dry field.

"Innocent."

One word, and the temperature in the theatre rose two degrees.

"I don't do theology," the Rider said. "I do accounts. I have looked into the souls of murderers and saints and every mixed thing in between, and the Penance Stare has never once cared what anybody was wearing when they sinned. That girl —" the flame of his skull guttered, banked low, almost respectful — "there is nothing in her to burn. Nothing. Do you understand how rare that is? I could count on one hand. But the five on that bench…" The fire climbed again, white at the roots. "They took a child's mercy and called it filth, and they did it in Heaven's name, which means they forged the signature of God on a death warrant. That is on the ledger. All of it. Every hour of what happens to her next is on their page, not hers. And ledgers, in my experience, always come due."

He settled back into the dark. "I hope I'm there when hers does."

---

And then there was the Watcher.

Uatu had observed, by conservative estimate, more atrocities than any being in this theatre had heartbeats. He had watched worlds burn and pantheons devour their children and civilizations vote themselves cheerfully into extinction, and he had done it with the vast, sorrowing equanimity of a creature for whom witnessing was a sacred office. Several of the sharper occupants of the theatre — Doom, the Black Panther, Emma Frost — had taken to reading him the way sailors read a barometer. He never reacted. That was the point of him.

He reacted now.

It was small. It would not have registered on anyone who had not spent seven chapters learning his stillness. But the Watcher's great head inclined, perhaps a degree, and his eyes — which held galaxies as a matter of anatomy — closed, and stayed closed, for the length of one full human breath.

"I have seen this before," he said, and his voice was quieter than they had ever heard it. "Not this girl. This *shape*. The institution that devours its gentlest member to prove the fence is real. I have watched it occur on ten thousand worlds and I catalogued it each time, and I confess I do not know why this instance…" He paused, and the pause itself was unprecedented. "Perhaps it is because I have also been watching the boy. The one who fails, and stands, and fails, and stands. Their threads are converging. I can perceive it even now, beneath the vision's surface — her exile and his path are braiding into a single cord. And I find that I am not indifferent to where it leads. That is new. I am… uncertain whether it is permitted."

"Permitted by who?" Kamala asked.

The air answered before the Watcher could.

The theatre's light did not change, exactly, but it acquired *depth*, the way still water reveals its depth when something enormous passes far beneath. For an instant — no longer — a figure was present at the theatre's height without in any way having arrived: golden, threefold-faced, vast beyond the room's geometry and yet contained by it, because everything was contained by this room, which was itself a fact several cosmologists in the audience had privately agreed never to examine closely.

The Living Tribunal regarded the grey screen with one face, the audience with another. The third face, hooded, regarded nothing that could be seen.

**"THE SCALES NOTICE HER,"** it said, in a voice like the settling of continents. **"THEY NOTICE HER BECAUSE SHE HAS NEVER ONCE ASKED THEM TO."**

And it was gone, if it had ever been there, leaving behind it a silence so complete that Deadpool — who had been physically restraining himself for six minutes, visibly vibrating — decided, for the first time in recorded history, to say nothing at all.

---

The wheel turned again.

It turned fast this time, urgent, the chime returning — but the chime came in wrong, pitched low and rough, a bell with a crack in it. The banner that unfurled was violet shot through with red, and it read:

**ISSEI'S PROTECTIVE RAGE — SUPER RARE**

"Oh good," Spider-Man said weakly, from the middle rows. "Follow-up. Great. I definitely have the emotional reserves for a follow-up."

The screen lit on Issei Hyoudou, and for one grace-note of a moment it was the Issei they had spent seven chapters learning to read: rumpled uniform, absurd hair, the specific slouch of a boy whose greatest recorded ambitions were dishonorable and whose greatest recorded actions kept accidentally being the opposite. He was standing in a city park in the late afternoon, and he had just been told — the vision supplied the knowledge the way dreams do — where Asia Argento was. What the fallen angels occupying the abandoned church intended to do to her. What the ritual would take, and what would be left of her after.

The audience watched him receive it.

They had seen Issei angry before. Chapter Seven had been an anthology of Issei angry — angry at losing, angry at his own weakness, angry in the flailing, shouting, faintly ridiculous key that was his default register, the anger of a boy hitting a wall with his fists because the wall had the audacity to be a wall. Captain America had called it heart. Cyclops had called it trainable. It had never once been frightening.

This was not that.

The change came in stages, and the theatre's screen — which had lovingly documented every pratfall this boy had ever suffered — documented each one with the same terrible fidelity. First the stillness: Issei Hyoudou stopped moving, completely, the perpetual comic fidget of him switching off like a cut feed. Then the eyes. The warm brown of them didn't glow, didn't change color, did nothing supernatural at all, and that was somehow the worst part — they simply emptied of everything adolescent and filled with something that measured. And then, on his left hand, the Boosted Gear materialized without being summoned, red plates sliding over his forearm as if the gauntlet had felt the shift in its wielder and come running, and the green gem at its heart kindled from within like a furnace door swinging open.

"*They took her back once already,*" Issei said. His voice came out level. That was the new thing. Not a scream — level, and quiet, and completely without stutter. "Her own church threw her away, and the ones who caught her are going to *use her up*. She healed me. She didn't ask what I was either. She's the nicest person I've ever met, and every single group that was supposed to protect her looked at her and saw a *part they could harvest*."

**[Partner,]** Ddraig's voice rumbled from the gem, and those in the theatre who had grown attached to the old dragon's dry, paternal amusement noticed that the amusement was entirely absent. In its place was something almost cautious. Almost — attentive. **[Your heart rate has not risen. Do you understand what that means? Rage that heats up burns the wielder. Rage that cools down… that is the other kind. I have carried hosts for two thousand years. I know the other kind when I feel it wake.]**

"Is that a problem?" Issei asked.

**[No,]** said the Welsh Dragon, the Red Dragon Emperor, one of the two beings in that cosmology whom God Himself had needed sealing rather than fighting. And there was a note in the ancient voice that none of his previous commentary had ever contained: **[It is a beginning.]**

On screen, Issei began to walk toward the church on the hill. He did not run. Running was for the boy he had been that morning. He walked, and the sun went down behind him as he went, and the vision let the image hold: a slight, unremarkable teenage silhouette with a dragon's arm, walking at an execution like a debt collector, while somewhere ahead of him a gentle girl folded her hands and prayed to a Heaven that had already filed her under acceptable losses.

The screen faded — not resolving, not concluding. *Beginning.* The rescue, whatever it would cost, had started, and the theatre would have to wait to see it paid for.

---

The lights came up on a room that did not know what to do with itself.

"Okay," said Kamala, into the hush. "So that was — okay. Am I the only one who found that a little…" She hunted for the word and refused all the softer candidates. "Scary? Like — good scary? Justified scary? But scary. That was not 'oath scene in an anime' angry. That was 'the animal got out of the enclosure' angry."

"You are not the only one," T'Challa said quietly.

"The boy telegraphs everything," Shang-Chi said, frowning, replaying it behind his eyes. "It is his defining flaw as a fighter — we established this last chapter. Every intention broadcast, every feint legible. Just now he telegraphed *nothing*. His breathing didn't change. I have trained with masters who spent decades learning to do what grief just did to him in four seconds."

Captain America hadn't spoken. Sam — and Steve beside him — both wore the same expression, the one worn by men who have seen fresh recruits come back from their first real loss and known, with sorrow and certainty, that a door had closed behind the kid forever.

"That's the moment," Steve said finally. "Everybody who does this work has one. The moment it stops being about you. I just — " He exhaled. "He's sixteen. I'd have wished him another few years before he found it."

"Wish him nothing of the kind," said Doom, from his box, and there was — unmistakably, and to the visible discomfort of everyone who heard it — approval in the metallic voice. "The buffoon is dead. Something with a spine of dragon-bone stood up inside his skin tonight. The girl's tribunal made an enemy this evening that it entirely lacks the imagination to fear, and Doom, for one, anticipates their education."

The Ghost Rider's flame, back in the dark, burned steady and low, like a signal fire answering another signal fire a long way off.

And above them all, the Watcher looked at the dimmed screen for a long time. Nightcrawler, glancing up — because he had been praying, quietly, in German, for a girl who did not exist in any world he could reach — was the only one positioned to see it, and he would spend some days deciding whether he had truly seen it at all:

The Watcher's hand, closing slowly at his side. Not in judgment.

In hope.
