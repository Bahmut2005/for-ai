# Chapter Forty-Six: Groot Speaks Dragon

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Groot • Ddraig • Cosmic Linguists (Vision, Rocket, the Rigellian Recorder, the Allspeak-bearers) • Mystics (Doctor Strange, Wong, Agatha Harkness, Doctor Voodoo)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Conversation between Groot and Ddraig" — **Secret**
2. "Forgotten Draconic Prophecy" — **Mythic**

---

Five nights into the Ravager arc, the theatre had learned that the machine could be *heckled*.

It had been a rowdy banner. The console it had extruded for its temporary operators had insulted Kraglin's spelling, graded Yondu's whistling "ADEQUATE, SENTIMENTAL," and stamped **COMMON SENSE FAILURE** across the Ravager section so often the captains had started wearing it like a patch. It had let Rocket run his heist simulation into the ground and let a dragon impersonating a security AI catch him at the vault door. It had let a whistle-arrow and a Dragon Shot spar until half the room forgot to be afraid of either. The Ravager Rolls were, by broad consensus, the first arc anyone would have paid to attend.

So when the wheel came down on night six and the rarity band went the deep, lightless violet of a Secret pull, the room sobered the way a bar sobers when the music stops.

Kraglin put his hand on the operator plate, because that had been his job all week.

**OPERATOR INSUFFICIENT,** said the console.

"Rude," said Kraglin, "but fair."

Yondu tried. Stakar tried. Rocket tried twice, the second time with a bypass clip he'd built out of a seat spring, and the console printed **NICE TRY** and then, in larger type, letter by letter, an operator designation nobody had bet on:

**OPERATOR: GROOT.**

The theatre turned. In the Guardians' block, unhurried, Groot stood. He looked at the violet band for a long moment with an expression that no one — afterward, comparing notes — could agree on, and then he went down the aisle steps the way trees do everything: patiently, and all at once.

"Since when," Peter Quill said, to no one, "does the loot box know Groot?"

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2,** printed the screen.

**"CONVERSATION BETWEEN GROOT AND DDRAIG" — RARITY: SECRET.**

And for the first time in forty-five chapters, the theatre did not swallow them. No vision came. Instead the screen filled, edge to edge, with a single image the room knew better than its own flags: the round green gem of the Boosted Gear, vast as a sunrise, lit from within — and *live*. Not a recording. The theatre had heard Ddraig speak out of the dark before, on the bad nights, and every soul present recognized the difference between footage of a dragon and the attention of one.

Groot stopped at the front rail, tilted his head back, and spoke.

"I am Groot."

That is what the room heard. That is what the room *always* heard. And underneath it, in the same instant, every translation apparatus in a multiverse's worth of assembled genius failed simultaneously, each in its own way, like instruments blowing out one by one in a storm.

Rocket's translator implant — the one that had rendered Groot faithfully through eleven years, two wars, and one funeral — returned static and then, worse, returned *nothing*, and Rocket came up out of his seat with his ears flat. "Okay, that's — hold on. Hold on. I didn't get that one." His voice did a thing it had never done in public. "I get *all* of 'em. I'm the guy who gets 'em. Groot. Buddy. Say it again."

Groot did not say it again.

Thor, who had taken Groot as an elective on Asgard and dined out on the fact for years, had gone very still. "I understood the first word," he said slowly. "Only the first. It was not 'I.'"

"The Allspeak," Loki said, and stopped. The room turned, because Loki's voice had lost its silk. The Allspeak was not a skill; it was a birthright woven into the bones of gods, the tongue beneath tongues, and it did not fail. "The Allspeak renders all speech as meaning. It is rendering this as—" His hand had closed on the seat back. "It is declining. I did not know it *could* decline."

Vision rose, head tilted, running the acoustics with the courteous frown he wore in place of alarm. "The utterance contains three phonemes," he reported. "Three. The information encoded in it — measured by the response latency of the listener — exceeds the carrying capacity of that channel by eleven orders of magnitude. This is not compression. Compression has a floor. This is—" He paused, and chose the accurate word over the comfortable one. "This is not language *transmitting* meaning. It appears to be language *permitting* it."

High in the archival seats, the Rigellian Recorder — who had catalogued four hundred thousand tongues and considered the number embarrassing — printed **RECORDING** and then **ERROR** and then **RECORDING** and then **ERROR**, in a loop, and finally, with what would have been a sigh in anything with lungs: **THIS UNIT WILL LISTEN.**

And the gem answered.

It was not the theatre-voice, the vast entertained rumble the room had grown fond of. It was under that voice, the way bedrock is under a road — a slow harmonic that came up through the floor and the teeth and the oldest parts of the hindbrain, and the green light of the gem moved in patterns, tidal, deliberate, and Groot's rings answered it, luminescence rising through the grain of him like sap made of light.

In the mystics' rows, Doctor Strange was on his feet with his hands half-raised, not casting — *reading* — and finding, visibly, that his fingers had nowhere to hold. "It isn't a language," he said. "Stop trying to parse it as one. Languages are agreements. This is older than agreeing. It's—" He grasped for it. "It's *custody*. They aren't exchanging words. They're exchanging things held in trust."

"The first conversation," Wong said quietly, beside him. He had not stood. He had folded his hands, as one does at the door of a temple older than one's order. "Every mythology on every world we have catalogued keeps a version of it, and we filed them all as metaphor. The tree that holds the worlds. The serpent at its roots. Kamar-Taj's oldest scroll calls it *the argument that keeps the roots wet* and does not translate one line of it."

"Because it isn't ours to translate," said Agatha Harkness, and the old witch's voice had no theatre in it at all tonight. "Three centuries I've stolen words that weren't mine to speak. I know the taste of a locked tongue. Children — that one isn't locked. It's *entrusted*. There's a difference, and the difference is what's listening."

Doctor Voodoo said nothing. The loa rode him lightly, most nights, a murmur at his shoulder. Tonight, Daniel's voice in his ear had gone silent mid-sentence — the dead, listening too.

Down from the Asgardian tiers came Heimdall's voice, gold-eyed and very low. "I heard this once. A boy on the walls of Asgard, farther down the roots of Yggdrasil than I was permitted to look. Two notes of it, and my father fetched me back and did not scold me, which frightened me more." He watched the green light move. "I have waited nine thousand years to know what the tree was saying. I find I am content to wait longer."

The exchange went on. Perhaps two minutes; perhaps much longer — Vision declined, afterward, to publish his measurement, which the room noted, because Vision had done that only once before, in Chapter Thirty. Groot spoke in slow tidal phrases; the gem answered in light and bedrock; once, unmistakably, Groot gestured — a broad sweep of one arm that took in the entire theatre, every hero and monster and god of a gathered multiverse — and the gem *flickered*, and several thousand beings had the distinct, prickling experience of being briefly discussed.

"Did—" Sam Wilson leaned forward. "Did the tree just tell the dragon about *us*?"

**[Heh,]** said Ddraig — the familiar voice for just that one syllable, surfacing like a whale's back and sounding, the room would swear, *fond* — and then the bedrock took him again.

And in the villains' dark, Fin Fang Foom stood up.

The theatre had seated exactly one dragon of its own, and for forty-five chapters Foom's contribution had been scorn — scorn for the gauntlet, scorn for the servitude, scorn for a "true dragon" leashed to a lecherous mammal. He stood now with his wings half-flared and his ancient face wearing something the room had no file for, and when Titania snapped at him to sit, he said, without looking at her, in a voice like a landslide remembering being a mountain:

"Be silent. That is High Root-Speech. My kind has *dreamed* of it. We stopped teaching it to our hatchlings before your species learned fire, because—" He stopped himself. His claws closed on the seat back. "Because it carries too far."

"Carries to *whom*?" Doom asked, sharp, from two tiers down.

Foom did not answer. Foom had, visibly, decided he had said one sentence too many, and the room filed *that* — a dragon the size of a bridge choosing silence out of what could only be called manners, or fear, or both.

It was then that Groot said the last thing.

It was short. Three syllables, same as ever — *I am Groot* — but the light in his rings gathered and went *down* him as he said it, root-ward, the way meaning falls into the ground, and the gem on the screen stopped moving entirely.

Green light. Utter stillness. A held breath the size of a sky.

And the gacha wheel, which no one had touched, which its designated operator was standing forty feet away from with his hand nowhere near the plate, began to spin.

**UNSCHEDULED,** printed the screen, in small type, and the room's blood went cold, because the machine annotating its own machinery had happened exactly once before, and that night had ended with a child-shaped hole in a photograph.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

**"FORGOTTEN DRACONIC PROPHECY" — RARITY: MYTHIC.**

**PULL TRIGGERED BY UTTERANCE. THIS UNIT DID NOT DECLINE IT.**

"That's not the same as choosing it," Rocket said hoarsely, to nobody. "You all caught that, right? That's not the same—"

The theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

There was no place. That was the first wrongness. Every vision in forty-five chapters had stood them *somewhere* — a park, a battlefield, a kitchen. This one stood them in fire that predated the invention of *somewhere*: the first sky, uncounted, unbounded, and full of dragons.

Not dragons as the room knew them. Dragons before names, before factions, before the sorting of worlds into worlds — vast slow shapes of the original heat, gathered in a congregation that was also a song. The vision did not translate the song. It *subtitled* it, in fragments, the machine's white type arriving halting and reluctant, like a clerk transcribing scripture at gunpoint:

**WHEN THE SKIES ARE COUNTED, THE COUNT WILL BE BROKEN.**

**WHEN THE COUNT IS BROKEN, THE RED WILL RETURN — NOT THE FIRST RED, NOT THE LAST, BUT THE RED WHO CHOSE THE SMALL.**

**HE WILL CARRY A SOVEREIGN OF NOTHING AND A WING MADE OF OATHS. THE DRAGONS OF ALL SKIES WILL COME TO HIM — THE CROWNED, THE FALLEN, THE BOUND, THE BURIED — AND HE WILL NOT COMMAND THEM. THEY WILL SIMPLY ARRIVE, AS RIVERS ARRIVE.**

**THEY WILL GATHER AGAINST**

— and there the song *stopped*.

Not ended. Stopped. The next stanza was gone, and the room knew the shape of the goneness the instant they saw it, because they had seen it on a mountainside scar, and in a family photograph, and threading the hairline fracture that still crossed the theatre's screen like a scratch on the sky: a static-edged absence, a cut with shivering borders, a place where the song's spacing continued *around* something that had been removed — verse-shaped, exactly, the way a slot in a photograph had been child-shaped, exactly.

In the front row, Asia Argento made a small sound and pressed both hands over her heart, weeping again for something with no name on it, and Kuroka's ears went flat to her skull: "Same hole, nya," she whispered. "Same *knife*."

The congregation of first dragons, in the vision, did not sing past the gap. They sang *at* it — grief in the original key — and then, one by one, the vast shapes turned and breathed on the song itself, and buried it in fire, and agreed, in the way of mountains agreeing, to forget it on purpose.

The last surviving line hung alone in the dark before the vision closed. The machine tried three times to render it. The first two attempts printed as ▮▮▮▮. The third came through, flickering, static-edged, wearing the wrongness like a borrowed coat, and the theatre read it in the silence of a room that had read it once before, on its very first night:

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT.**

---

The lights came up on pandemonium held at arm's length.

"Ddraig." Thor was on his feet, and there was no fondness in it now, only the old-story dread. "Dragon of the roots. You knew this song. Sing us the buried verse."

Silence from the gem.

"Dragon." Doom, rising, cold and exact. "Doom does not petition. The stanza names the enemy. This assembly was *built* to be briefed. Complete the record."

The green light stirred, at last, and when Ddraig spoke it was the theatre-voice again — but slowed, and careful, and the room realized it had never once heard the Welsh Dragon Emperor choose his words before. He had always spent them like a rich man.

"**No.**"

"That's—" Carol Danvers started.

"**Ask me anything else,**" Ddraig said. "**Ask me my defeats. Ask me how the White and I were bound, which shames me. Ask me what my partner mumbles in his sleep, which shames him. I will spend all of it.**" A long exhale, felt in the floor. "**Not this. Little ones — hear the shape of what you saw. My kind did not *forget* that song. Forgetting is what happens to songs nobody guards. That song was buried, by every dragon of every sky that ever learned it, in the same age, without conferring. We did not bury it because it was false.**"

The gem's light banked low.

"**Some things are not hidden from you,**" the dragon said, quieter than the theatre had ever heard him. "**They are hidden from what listens when you speak.**"

And the green light went out — not gone, the room could feel him there still, coiled around his own silence like a hoard — but *guarded*, shut, a door closed by something that had never once, in forty-six chapters, declined to talk.

Into the ringing quiet, Groot turned from the rail. He looked up at the dark screen, and at the hairline fracture crossing it, and he reached out one long hand and rested two fingers, very gently, against the seam of the crack — the way you'd touch a wound on someone you couldn't heal.

"I am Groot," he said.

Three syllables. Every translator in the room stayed dark. And yet not one being in the theatre, from the gods' tier to the cheap seats, escaped the weight of it — a pressure of meaning arriving without any words to carry it, the way grief arrives, the way weather does.

Rocket sat down slowly. His hands were not steady, and he made no attempt to hide it, which frightened the Guardians more than the prophecy had. "First time in my life," he said, to his boots. "I got nothin'. Eleven years. I got *nothin'*."

"He said it like a promise," Drax offered, frowning, entirely certain. "I did not understand the words. But the intention was a promise. Or a warning. In my language these are the same word."

Mantis was crying, silently, antennae glowing, and could not tell anyone why.

"Thor," Steve Rogers said quietly. "You caught a word. First word."

Thor looked, for a moment, every one of his fifteen hundred years. "Aye," he said. "And I'll not repeat it in a room where the dragon just told us something *listens*." He sat. He did not touch his hammer. He put both empty hands on his knees, like a man in church.

High above, the Watcher had leaned forward, and stayed leaned, his reflection doubled in the fractured screen. He had called himself blind once in this theatre. Tonight he had watched a tree and a dragon hold a conversation his sight could not enter, about a verse his inventory did not contain, and he said nothing at all, which everyone understood to be the loudest thing he'd said in six chapters.

The console chimed, almost apologetic. The violet band retired; the Ravager banner relit in its scuffed rose-and-flame; and the scoreboard, resuming its arc-long duties as though the sky had not just moved, printed its tease for the next night's business:

**NEXT PULL PENDING: COMPATIBILITY ANALYSIS — GUARDIANS OF THE GALAXY.**

**SOME OF YOU WILL BE SURPRISED.**

"Great," Quill said weakly, into the dark. "Great. Speed-dating. Right after the doom verse. This place has *no* transitions."

Groot went back up the aisle steps, patient and all at once, and folded himself into his seat between Rocket and Drax, and the two of them, without discussion, each put a hand somewhere on the bark of him and left it there until the lights went down.
