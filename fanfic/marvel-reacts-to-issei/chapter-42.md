# Chapter Forty-Two: A Dragon's Pirate Code

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Ravagers (Yondu, Stakar, Kraglin, the captains) • Guardians of the Galaxy (Rocket, Nebula, Gamora, Quill, Drax, Groot, Mantis) • Avengers (Captain America, Falcon, Iron Man, Captain Marvel)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Smuggling for a Good Cause" — **Rare**
2. "Killing Pirates for Breaking His Code" — **Epic**

---

On the second night of the Ravager administration, the gacha machine was still insulting its new management.

The great wheel had migrated down from its high corner to a crooked console in the Ravager block — a lever, a coin slot that took no coin, and a brass plaque that tonight read: **OPERATOR OF RECORD: UDONTA, Y. — CLEARANCE: PROVISIONAL. REASON: SENTIMENTALITY DETECTED.**

"It called me *sentimental*," Yondu said, wounded, to the theatre at large. "In front of Stakar."

"In front of *everybody*," Kraglin said helpfully.

High in the upper right of the screen, the crack from arc's-end ticked once, softly, like a clock in another room. Nobody mentioned it anymore. Everybody heard it.

Stakar Ogord watched the wheel the way a man watches a rigged card game he's been ordered to play anyway. "Pull the lever, Yondu. Machine picked us to run it for a reason, and I'd rather find out the reason than get told it."

Yondu spat on his palm, gripped the lever, and grinned his ruined grin up at the screen. "Alright, you smug hunk of scrap. Show us what a dragon does for money."

He pulled.

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2.**

**"SMUGGLING FOR A GOOD CAUSE" — RARITY: RARE.**

"Ha!" barked Rocket, feet up on the seat in front of him. "*Smuggling!* Finally. Forty-one chapters of hugging and holy swords, and the loot box finally coughs up a crime."

The theatre took its breath, and swallowed them.

---

Night, in the vision, over a black border sea in the underworld — a sky with no stars, lit from below by the fires of a nation eating itself.

The captions laid it out with the machine's usual cold economy. A devil principality gone rotten: an old-blood tyrant purging his territory of everything impure — reincarnated devils, half-bloods, humans who had seen too much. Border wards sealed. Watchtowers paid by the head.

And running the blockade, lights doused, a flotilla of the ugliest ships the Ravagers had ever approved of on sight: smuggler craft, patched and unlicensed, holds crammed not with cargo but with *people*. Hundreds. Children asleep on coiled rope.

On the lead ship's deck, leaning on the rail like he'd been born on it, stood Issei Hyoudou — and beneath his feet, unmistakable, the deck itself was *alive*, timbers flexing like muscle, a familiar dragon-prowed silhouette grown to full running size.

"That's his boat!" Kraglin was on his feet, pointing. "That's the little boat! Ryuuteimaru! Look how big he got!"

"He is a good ship," Drax agreed solemnly. "He has chosen a good captain. This is known."

The smuggler captain came up the deck to Issei — a scarred low-class devil with brands on his knuckles and a ledger where his conscience should be. The money was already paid; the captions said Issei had emptied a high-class devil's stipend without haggling, and Mephisto's chuckle drifted up from the villains' dark like smoke off a ledger. What the vision chose to show them was the *handshake*.

"Rules are rules, kid," the smuggler captain said. "My fleet, my sea, my routes. You don't like how I run 'em, swim."

"Your fleet, your sea, your rules," Issei agreed easily. "Except one." He didn't raise his voice. The green gem on the back of his left hand turned, lazily, like an eye finding something. "Nobody in those holds gets touched. Not sold, not shorted, not scared for fun. They walk off your ships the same as they walked on. Swear that, and I'm your crew till the last one's ashore — I'll haul rope, I'll take your orders, I'll fight your fights."

"And if I don't swear it?"

Issei smiled. It was his stupid, sunny, teenage smile, and somehow that made it worse. "Then you're not smugglers, you're a problem, and I'm real good at problems."

The captain looked at the gauntlet for a long moment. Then he spat on his palm and shook.

"Kid drives a Ravager bargain," Stakar said quietly, in the theatre. "Pay full, swear terms, spit and shake. Where in nine galaxies did a schoolboy learn contract law for pirates?"

The run played out in long, held-breath minutes — patrol wards sweeping the water, the flotilla threading a reef of sensor-spells with inches to spare — and when the tyrant's border cutters finally caught their wake and closed in, the theatre heard the sound half the room had learned to lean forward at:

**[BOOST.]**

**[BOOST.]**

**[BOOST.]**

But Issei didn't fly out to meet them. He knelt on the living deck, put both hands flat on the timbers of the oldest, slowest, most overloaded ship in the fleet — the one carrying the sick and the small — and the theatre heard the other word, the one that had always mattered more:

**[TRANSFER.]**

Doubled and redoubled dragon-power poured out of him into *engines*. Into patched hulls and dying ward-sails and a smuggler fleet that had never in its miserable underpaid existence moved like *that* — the whole flotilla lit up crimson at the seams and went across the border sea like a thrown knife, cutters falling away astern, and on the lead deck the smuggler crews were screaming, laughing, pounding the rails, and Issei Hyoudou was on his knees with his nose bleeding from the output, laughing with them.

"**Understand,**" Ddraig's voice rolled over the vision, dry as old gold, "**he could have sunk the cutters in one pass. He asked me, and I told him so. He said the fleet gets everyone out, or nothing counts. So the fleet it was. My partner does not think like a weapon. On my bad days I consider this a flaw.**"

The last frame held on the far shore at dawn: hundreds of refugees wading up onto free sand, and a seventeen-year-old boy asleep against a dragon-prowed hull, being covered with a stolen coat by a smuggler who would have denied it under torture.

The vision released them.

---

The Ravager block erupted into what was, technically, a parliamentary debate.

"That's a Ravager!" Kraglin was standing on his seat. "That's — cap'n, tell me that ain't a Ravager! Runs a blockade, pays his crew, honors terms, steals from a *lord*—"

"He didn't steal nothin', that's the problem," growled Martinex from the captains' row. "He *paid*. Emptied his accounts. A Ravager turning zero profit on a run that clean is a disgrace to the colors."

"He turned profit." Yondu hadn't moved from the lever. He was watching the frozen dawn on the screen with an expression his crew couldn't file. "Three hundred head walked off them boats *free*, and every one of 'em's his forever now, and he didn't even do it for that. That's the richest damn haul on the board, boys. We just been countin' wrong our whole lives." He scratched at his fin, sour at his own sincerity. "Sentimental. Yeah. Machine had my number."

"The math checks out, actually," Rocket said, and something in his voice made the Guardians look over. The raccoon wasn't joking. He was sitting very still, tail wrapped around his own feet. "Transfer, not Boost. He's got the biggest gun in every room he walks into and his first move is handing out ammo. You know how many crews I've run with?" He bared his teeth at nobody. "You know how many guys with big guns share?"

"I am Groot," said Groot, gently.

"Yeah," Rocket said. "Exactly. Him and you. That's the list."

Steve Rogers had been quiet through all of it, elbows on knees. "Enemy blockade, civilian extraction, force multiplication through allied assets, and he embedded under local command instead of taking over." He shook his head slowly. "We ran ops like that into occupied France. The guys who insisted on doing it *that* way — support the resistance, don't replace it — those were the good ones. That's not pirate doctrine. That's not even Avengers doctrine. That's better."

"It's both, Cap," Sam Wilson said. "That's the scary part. It compiles on both operating systems."

The machine chimed. The brass plaque reprinted: **OPERATOR ASSESSMENT: PENDING. SECOND EXHIBIT REQUIRED.**

"Second exhibit," Gamora repeated, flat. She had learned the machine's grammar faster than most. "It showed us the oath first. Now it shows us the price of breaking one."

Yondu looked at the lever for a second longer than he needed to. Then he pulled it.

**"KILLING PIRATES FOR BREAKING HIS CODE" — RARITY: EPIC.**

---

The same sea. A different fleet, a different month — the captions were scrupulous about that, as if the machine wanted no confusion about who had stayed bought and who hadn't. A second run, a second crew, the same handshake on the same deck: *nobody in those holds gets touched.* Spit, and shake.

And then a rendezvous that wasn't on the charts. Imperial cutters riding at anchor in a hidden cove — waiting, not hunting. Lanterns exchanged. A bounty ledger passing from hand to hand. And the smuggler captain's crew moving down into their own holds with slaver collars swinging from their fists, because the tyrant paid by the head, and the heads were *already on board*, and why sail all that dangerous way when the cargo could be sold right here?

The theatre went cold row by row, but nowhere colder than the Ravager block, where several dozen former slaves and sold children sat in absolute silence and watched a betrayal they knew from the inside.

A little girl screamed in the hold. A collar clicked.

And the sea stood up.

Ryuuteimaru came out of deep water below the anchored fleet like the ocean had grown a grudge, and on his prow, already armored, stood the boy who had hauled rope for these men for nine days and taken their orders and eaten their terrible food. The vision put the theatre close enough to see through the visor. They would rather it hadn't. There was nothing sunny left.

"You swore," Issei said. Just that. Not a war cry. An *audit*.

The smuggler captain went for charm first — kid, be reasonable, look at the margins — and then, when the crimson helm just kept looking at him, went for the little girl with a knife, because men like him always know exactly one hostage-shaped trick.

He did not finish the reach.

The vision did not flinch and did not gloat. It showed the work the way the theatre had learned this boy did the work when the line was crossed: fast, close, and final. Ascalon's edge took the captain while the knife was still rising. The crewmen who drew on the holds — who put hands on collars, who chose, visibly *chose*, in the lantern-light — met Boost-compounded force with nothing held back, and the imperial slaver crews on the cutters fared no better and lasted no longer. It was over in under a minute, and the sea in the cove was burning in patches.

But the theatre, primed by forty-one chapters, saw the other thing too — the thing the machine had made them literate enough to catch. The deckhand who had refused, who had stood back from the holds with his hands open: Issei walked past him. The cook, on her knees, who had hidden two children behind the galley stove: Issei pulled her to her feet, and *bowed*, armored, absurd, sincere. The captions printed the tally with the machine's terrible neutrality: **THOSE WHO KEPT FAITH: SPARED, PAID, AND SEEN HOME. THOSE WHO BROKE IT: NONE SURVIVING.**

The last thing the vision gave them was the boy sitting on the burned deck afterward with the freed kids, helmet off, letting a six-year-old wear the gauntlet, looking tired in a way that had nothing to do with his body, and Ddraig's voice, low, for his partner alone:

"**You are not a killer, partner. But you keep a graveyard for oath-breakers, and I have never once had to talk you into filling it. Interesting boy.**"

The theatre came back to itself.

---

"That," said Yondu Udonta, into the ringing quiet, "is a Ravager captain."

"That is *not*—" Martinex started.

"That's the *code*, Marty!" Yondu came off the console, fin blazing, arm out at the frozen screen. "The one damn law we got! Ravagers don't deal in kids! Everything else is negotiable — cargo's negotiable, loyalty's negotiable, *I'm* negotiable — but that one law holds or the colors mean nothin'! I broke it once. Cost me my fleet, my flame, twenty years of Stakar's back turned to me — and this boy," his voice cracked, and he let it, in front of everyone, "this boy enforces it *for free*. On his own crew. First offense. No appeal." He turned to Stakar. "Tell me he don't fly."

Stakar Ogord looked at the burning cove on the screen for a long time. "He'd have made a good Ravager," he said at last. "That's not the question that worries me. The question that worries me is whether any of *us* would've passed nine days under his flag." Nobody in the captains' row answered that. It was the loudest thing in the theatre.

"He wouldn't have joined," Quill said quietly. "That's the thing. I keep trying to make him fit the jacket and he doesn't, because Ravagers steal *for* somebody — for the crew, for the take. He steals people *back*. It's not the same job. It just uses the same sea."

"The code's better than ours," Rocket said. He'd meant it to come out flip. It didn't. "Ours is a floor. One thing we won't do. His is a *deal* — keep faith with the helpless and he's your crew, break it and you're dead. It's clean. You always know where you stand with a guy like that." A beat. "I've never once known where I stand. I'd have worked for him in a heartbeat, and it would've scared the fur off me."

"He executed eleven beings without trial," Carol Danvers said, from the Avengers rows — not an attack; a filing. "I want that in the record next to everything else. Sovereign-tier actor, self-issued rules of engagement, capital enforcement."

"They were armed, mid-crime, holding collars over children, and he offered terms in advance to every one of them," Steve answered, and his voice was heavy with how much he didn't enjoy the arithmetic. "I've signed off on worse with less warning. God help me, the paperwork's cleaner than mine."

And then Nebula stood up.

She had watched both pulls without one word, and the Guardians had felt her silence sitting among them like an unexploded shell. She didn't address the room. She addressed the machine, directly, the way one weapon addresses another.

"Slavers," she said. "Not oath-breaking pirates. Not tyrants. *Slavers.* The ones who take people and sell them and go home and sleep." Her black eyes didn't blink. "Does he kill them?"

The screen did not spin the wheel. It answered her at once, plainly, in white — and beneath the words, one silent frame from a market that had burned six arcs' worth of nights ago flickered like a receipt:

**WITHOUT HESITATION. WITHOUT EXCEPTION. WITHOUT REGRET.**

**IT IS THE OLDEST ENTRY IN HIS CODE.**

Nebula stood there a moment longer. Everyone near her had seen her take hits from things that cracked moons and never move. Now something in her machined face shifted about a millimeter, and the theatre understood it had watched a fortress accept a supply drop.

"Good," she said, and sat down.

Gamora's hand moved — slowly, telegraphing, the way you do with her sister — and rested on Nebula's forearm, on the metal one. Nebula did not shake it off. In the Ravager rows, someone began, very softly, to whistle the old fleet hymn, and the whistling spread through the leathers and the bad attitudes until even Martinex gave it up and joined.

The plaque on the console reprinted itself one last time.

**OPERATOR ASSESSMENT: COMPLETE. SUBJECT MEETS RAVAGER ADMISSION STANDARDS. RAVAGERS DO NOT MEET SUBJECT'S.**

**CLAIM DENIED. CUSTODY SHARED.**

"Cold," said Kraglin, with enormous respect.

Yondu patted the machine's console like a man patting a mean dog he'd decided to keep. Then the coin slot lit, unprompted, and printed a slip of paper nobody had asked for — a chit, gilt-edged, that floated across the theatre and landed in Rocket's lap. On it, in small mocking letters: **SIMULATION VOUCHER: STEAL ONE ITEM FROM THE SUBJECT'S ARCHIVE. REDEEMABLE: NEXT SESSION. GOOD LUCK, VERMIN.**

Rocket read it twice. His grin came up like a sunrise with a criminal record.

"Oh no," said Quill.

"Oh *yes*," said Rocket.

Above them all, in the upper right of the screen, the crack ticked once more in the dark — a hand-span longer than yesterday, patient as interest accruing — and the lights went down on the sound of pirates whistling a dead fleet's hymn for a boy none of them had met.
