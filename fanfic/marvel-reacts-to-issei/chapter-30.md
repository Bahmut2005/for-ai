# Chapter Thirty: Family-Class Jackpot

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Cosmic Families (Fantastic Four, Inhumans) • Villains (Doom, Mephisto, Magneto, Loki, Kang, Thanos) • The Eight Fiancées • Cosmic Observers (Uatu, Heimdall)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Montage of Ordinary Family Moments" — **Family-Class**
2. "Erased Child Frame" — **Secret**

---

The theatre had learned, over nine chapters of the warm-gold banner, that Family-Class nights smelled like food.

No one had ever caught the machine doing it. There were no kitchens in the impossible architecture, no vents, no source — but on the nights the doorbell-chime track lit up, the air in the tiered dark acquired miso and grilled fish and the ghost of cheap birthday-cake frosting, and several thousand heroes, monsters, gods, and killers had stopped pretending they didn't lean into it. Ben Grimm had a standing theory that the machine was softening them up for something. Ben Grimm was, as usual on structural matters, correct.

Tonight the smell arrived early, and it arrived *everywhere*, and the front rows noticed first, because the front rows had been rearranged since Chapter Twenty-One and were now occupied by eight women who could level the seating if the seating had been levelable.

"Breakfast," said Koneko Toujou, flatly, nose lifting a centimeter. "Ours."

"Ara," murmured Akeno Himejima, hand to her cheek, "it's even got the burnt bit Xenovia always fights the crows for."

"I do not fight crows." Xenovia Quarta paused. "I have fought one crow. It was a rematch."

Down the row, Rias Gremory did not join the bit. She was watching the gacha wheel descend with the particular stillness she'd worn all arc — the stillness of a high-class devil who had spent nine chapters watching a cold machine hand her private life to a coliseum, and had slowly, grudgingly, been forced to admit that the coliseum was handling it with more grace than she'd budgeted for. They had laughed at the laundry war, yes. But they had also gone quiet at Asia's prayers, and the Punisher had nodded once — *once* — at the injury ledger, and somewhere around Chapter Twenty-Seven the betting board on *Who Would He Adopt?* had turned from mockery into something closer to a waiting list.

The wheel settled. The warm-gold track lit end to end, every segment at once, which it had never done, and the ceiling put on the color of late Sunday afternoon.

**FINAL PULL OF THE BANNER,** the screen printed. **ARC FINALE.**

"Here we go," said Johnny Storm, rubbing his hands. "Jackpot round. Please be embarrassing. Please be *so* embarrassing—"

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2.**

The wheel spun once, lazily, a formality, and stopped on gold.

**"MONTAGE OF ORDINARY FAMILY MOMENTS" — RARITY: FAMILY-CLASS.**

"Ordinary," repeated Medusa, from the Inhuman block, her hair coiling into a skeptical question mark. "Twenty-nine chapters, and it has never once shown us anything ordinary."

"That's why it's the jackpot," said Sue Storm quietly, and the theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

Morning. The Hyoudou residence — the absurd one, the six-floors-down-and-who-knows-how-many-up one, the house that a devil clan had rebuilt around two bewildered human parents — and a kitchen table long enough to seat a peace conference, which, functionally, it did.

The vision was not one memory. It was hundreds, laid one over another like a year of mornings pressed into a single golden hour, and it moved the way memory moves — not in order, but in weight.

Issei Hyoudou, hair flattened on one side, refereeing the last slice of tamagoyaki between Xenovia and Koneko with the gravity of a man defusing ordnance. Rossweisse doing the grocery arithmetic aloud, in Norse, because swearing about yen in Norse didn't count. Miki Hyoudou moving through the chaos with a teapot, entirely unbothered, pausing to press a second helping on Asia because in this house Asia's plate being full was a law of physics. Gorou Hyoudou behind a newspaper he had not read one line of in three years, because over the top of it he was watching his son — his mediocre, ridiculous, murdered-and-returned son — laugh with his mouth full, and Gorou's eyes were doing the thing that fathers pretend is the newspaper's fault.

"Oh no," said Ben Grimm, thickly, forty seconds in. "It's gonna be one of *these*."

It was one of these.

A birthday — Kunou's, the fox girl's, gold-eared and incandescent, her mother Yasaka watching from the engawa as Issei carried out a cake he had decorated himself, which is to say, decorated the way a Dragon Blaster decorates a mountainside. The frosting read *KUNOU* and then surrendered. The girl looked at that ruined, lopsided, painstaking cake the way other children look at fireworks.

A treaty summit in the living room, formal seating, Ravel Phenex presiding with her terrible ledger open to the chore annex: whereas the party of the first part (the Red Dragon Emperor) did solemnly commit to bathroom duty on alternating weeks, and whereas his prior performance had been assessed at *catastrophic-to-adequate, trending adequate* — the room got the giggle it needed, and Reed Richards whispered to Sue that their chore chart could learn from the arbitration clauses.

A school run. This one the theatre leaned into, because they had met these children in Chapter Twenty-Seven: the warded kids, the gathered strays of a dozen crises — Kunou with her charm-stitched satchel; the solemn black-haired little girl who was, the room now knew, an infinite dragon god wearing a child shape because she liked how it fit; Irina's two adopted ones from a world that no longer needed them, each with Rossweisse's protective sigils inked inside their collars like a mother's name tag. Issei walked them the human way, on foot, one on his shoulders, past the convenience store, and the vision let the audience feel the wards the way he felt them — a lattice of watchfulness laid over an ordinary street, invisible, absolute, the whole household's power braided into a promise that these children would get to be *bored* at school today.

"He walks them," Crystal said softly. "He could fold space. He walks them."

"The walking *is* the ward," said Karnak, and for once no one asked him to find the flaw, because his voice made it clear he had looked and, in this one structure, hadn't.

And a barbecue — the montage's long final movement, a backyard summer evening that seemed to contain every named soul of nine chapters. Gorou at the grill defending his tongs from a Welsh Dragon Emperor's vessel with a fly-swatter. Kiba and Irina running a beanbag toss the children were shamelessly rigging. Akeno and Miki conspiring over marinade. Rias barefoot on the grass, crown nowhere, title nowhere, laughing at something Kuroka said until she had to hold the fence, while Koneko lay across Issei's back where he sat cross-legged in the grass, not to be carried anywhere, only because that was where she went when the world was safe.

The vision held there. Smoke and lantern light and fourteen conversations, and underneath it, audible to the theatre the way his dying thoughts had been audible in Chapter One, the slow tide of Issei Hyoudou's heart: *this. This is the thing. Not the throne, not the titles, not even — okay, mostly not even the harem-king part. This. Everyone fed, everyone loud, everyone HERE. This is what all of it is for.*

The montage began, gently, to close — the memories stacking into a final image, the way a year folds into a photograph. The whole sprawling household assembling on the lawn for a group picture at golden hour, Gorou fussing with a camera on a tripod, bodies sorting themselves into rows with the ease of long practice, Issei in the center being strangled affectionately from two directions.

In the theatre, in the front row, eight women watched their own life with their hands quietly finding each other's along the seat backs.

"I remember that evening," Asia whispered, shining. "That was the summer after — that was a good evening. We couldn't get everyone to look at the camera at the same time. It took eleven tries."

The screen, as if in fond agreement, began flicking through them. Try one: Kunou sneezing. Try two: Xenovia blinking. Try three—

---

The theatre would argue, afterward, about how long the frame lasted. Sam Wilson said half a second. Vision said four hundred milliseconds and declined to repeat the measurement. Karnak said it did not last any length of time at all, that duration was the wrong axis for what it did, and then he asked, politely, to change the subject, which Karnak had never done in thirty chapters.

Try three was the same lawn, the same golden hour, the same rows of the same family.

And standing in the front row of the photograph, between Asia and Koneko, with Issei's hand resting on their shoulder — a child.

Small. Nine, ten, maybe. The eye took in brown hair with a crimson cast, or crimson hair with a brown cast, or — the eye could not finish. The child was *wrong* the way a word repeated too many times is wrong: every part of the image around them was warm film-grain gold, and they were interference, a shape cut out of the photograph and the cut-out then filmed, edges shivering with a static that wasn't static, flickering between *there* and a lawn where the family's spacing left a gap exactly that size, as if the picture had been composed around someone and then told to forget why.

And where every other soul in thirty chapters had arrived in the theatre's collective mind with a name — *Issei Hyoudou, second-year* — *Rias Gremory, heiress* — *Kunou, daughter of Yasaka* — where the machine had never once failed to introduce so much as a passing crow—

—nothing arrived.

Not an unknown name. Not a withheld name. The theatre's minds reached for the introduction the way a foot reaches for a stair that isn't there, and several thousand beings felt, simultaneously, the specific vertigo of a hole where a fact should be.

The frame ended. Try four: Kunou sneezing again, eleven tries, laughter, the montage sealing itself shut in warm gold — and then the screen froze, rewound itself with a sound like tape dragged over a magnet, and stamped, in letters that did not pulse, over the locked image of a lawn with a static-edged gap in the front row:

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

**"ERASED CHILD FRAME" — RARITY: SECRET.**

**SOURCE: ▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮.**

**THIS MEMORY DOES NOT EXIST.**

---

The silence that followed was not the silence of grief, which the theatre knew well by now, or of awe, which it had learned in Chapter Twenty. It was the silence of a room checking its own pockets.

"Asia." Rias's voice, front row, very level, the level of a voice being held level by main strength. "The eleventh-try evening. Who is that?"

"I don't—" Asia Argento was on her feet without knowing she'd stood. Her hands were pressed over her heart, and her face wore an expression the theatre had never seen on it: not fear, not confusion — *loss*, arriving without luggage, grief with no name on the tag. "I don't know. Rias, I was *there*, I remember every try, there was no— there was never—" Her eyes were spilling over. She looked down at her own tears in bewilderment, as if they belonged to someone else. "Why am I— why does it *hurt?*"

"Look at the spacing," said Rossweisse. The Valkyrie's voice had gone to its runework register, the one she used for wards, and she was pale to the lips. "Look at where we're all standing in every other try. We're standing *around* something. In all eleven. I drew the household wards myself, I know our formations blind, and that photograph has a slot in it, and the slot is child-sized, and I have no memory of what it was for."

Koneko hadn't spoken. Koneko was gripping the seat back in front of her, and the seat back — theatre-matter, the substance that had shrugged off the Hulk in Chapter One — was creaking.

It was Kuroka who said the thing, and she said it with every trace of the lounging cat-lazy purr gone from her voice, ears flat to her skull, golden eyes fixed on the frozen frame:

"Senjutsu reads the flow of life, nya. Everything alive leaves a current. Everything *dead* leaves a wake." Her tail had bottled to twice its size. "That frame doesn't have either. It has a hole shaped like a life. Something didn't die there. Something got *unhappened*."

"Machine." Rias had turned from the screen to face the impossible ceiling directly, and now her voice was not level at all; it was the voice that had once told the theatre's cold system exactly what she thought of it airing her proposal night, the voice of the woman who had spent eight Pawns on a dying pervert because of what she saw in him. "You have narrated every horror in this boy's life with captions. You *itemize.* So itemize. Who is the child in my family's photograph?"

The screen did not answer.

The theatre had seen the machine be cruel, coy, pedantic, and once — memorably — sarcastic. It had never seen the machine be *silent*. The cursor at the bottom of the frozen frame blinked, and blinked, like something with its mouth open.

Then, letter by letter, slower than it had ever printed:

**RECORD NOT FOUND.**

And beneath that, unbidden, in a smaller type the room had to strain for:

**THIS UNIT DID NOT SELECT THIS PULL.**

---

The warmth died out of the air like a tide going all the way out.

"Okay," said Johnny Storm, into it, with none of his usual wattage. "Okay, the loot box that kidnapped the multiverse just said *that wasn't me.* Anybody else want to go home? I've wanted to go home for thirty chapters, but *now*—"

"Quiet," said Sue — but her arm had gone around Franklin, fast and instinctive, because her son was staring at the frozen frame with an expression no ten-year-old should have owned, and Franklin Richards, who dreamed universes the way other children dreamed of dogs, had gone the color of paper.

"Franklin?" Reed knelt. "What do you see?"

"The picture's not broken," Franklin said. His voice was very small and very certain. "Everybody thinks the picture's broken. It's not. The picture *remembers*. It's the only thing that still does." He pressed his face into Sue's side and would not say anything else, and Reed Richards — who had an equation for everything, who had spent nine chapters charmed by chore treaties — looked up at the static-edged gap on the screen and, for the first time in the theatre's memory, did not reach for a notebook. He reached for Valeria's hand.

Across the aisle, in the Inhuman block, Black Bolt had risen. He could say nothing, so his standing said it. Medusa read her king's face and translated, quietly: "He asks the room to note that in Attilan, when the genetic records show a gap, it means the record was attacked. Gaps do not occur. Gaps are *made*."

"The frame's edges." Heimdall's voice rolled down from the Asgardian rows, gold eyes fixed and grim, and half the theatre finished his sentence in their own heads before he spoke it, because they had heard him say it ten chapters ago about a scar in the sky over a mountain. "I have seen that shimmer before. Near the roots of Yggdrasil, where things have been *cut away*. The boy's great cannon bruised the world and left that mark by accident." He paused. "Nothing about this mark is an accident. This is the same knife, held by a hand."

And in the villains' dark, where the room had grown used to Doom's verdicts and Mephisto's markets, it was Kang the Conqueror who came out of his seat like a man who has found his own obituary in the morning paper.

"No." Flat. Final. Wrong in the mouth of a man who dealt in certainties across forty centuries. "No, this is not — listen to me. All of you, *listen*. There are mechanisms — bureaucracies — beyond every timeline, whose function is pruning. I have been their prisoner, their emperor, and their paperwork. I know what a pruned branch looks like from every side of the blade, and here is the single law of pruning, the one law nothing violates: *a pruned branch does not photograph itself.* When a future is cut, the cut takes the evidence. The grief. The gap in the family photo. The child-sized slot in the wards. It takes *everything*, that is what pruning IS—" He stopped. His hands, the theatre noticed, were not steady. "Something was erased, and the erasure *left a receipt.* Do you understand? Either the blade is failing." He sat down very slowly. "Or something on the far side of the cut is *pushing back through*."

"Kang of the many centuries." Loki's voice, from two rows over, silk over a wire. "You're afraid."

"I am *inventorying*," said Kang, which fooled no one.

Mephisto had not moved through any of it. The demon sat with his fingers steepled and his eyes on the front row — not on the screen, on the *wives*, on Asia Argento weeping for someone she could not name — and when he finally spoke, it was almost to himself, and it was the worst thing anyone had heard him say in thirty chapters because it was gentle.

"I deal in souls," he said. "Souls are my ledger, my currency, and my law, and the law says a soul may be bought, damned, traded, or destroyed — but a destroyed soul *balances*. The books always balance." He nodded, minutely, at Asia. "That girl is grieving a debt entry that isn't in any book I can read. Somewhere there is a soul that never got to exist, and the interest on it is compounding in a mother's heart anyway." He unsteepled his fingers. "I want no part of whatever holds *that* account. I wish to note, formally, for the room: there are things below even my basement. I believe we have just heard one of them moving furniture."

Magneto said nothing at all. But Charles Xavier, beside him, turned his head — because he had felt it, the way he felt all of it — the old man looking at a family photograph with a child-shaped hole in it, and thinking, with a grief fifty years deep, of a house fire, of a lost daughter, of every parent who has ever stood in front of an absence the world refuses to confirm. *Whoever did this,* Erik Lehnsherr thought, with a clarity that made Charles's temples ache, *whatever did this — when the boy finds out, he will need no help from any of us. But he shall have mine.*

---

Highest of all, in the tier that belonged to him alone, Uatu the Watcher had not moved for four hundred milliseconds and then had moved more than he had in thirty chapters.

He was standing. Watchers do not stand; watching has no posture. He stood, vast and pale, and his voice, when it came, had lost the archival calm that had carried it through murders and wars and god-tier ascensions alike.

"I am going to say this once, and I require the room to hear it precisely, because precision is all I have left tonight." The frozen frame reflected in his enormous eyes, twice. "I watch. It is my nature, my sentence, and my sum. I have seen every timeline that is. I have seen every timeline that was, and every timeline that may yet be, and — hear this — I have seen the *dark inventory*: the timelines that were unmade, for I watched them until the instant of their unmaking, and my memory holds what reality releases. That is the terrible privilege of my kind. Nothing is erased so completely that a Watcher did not watch it."

He raised one hand and pointed — the first time he had ever pointed — at the static-edged child.

"I have never seen that child. Not in what is. Not in what was. Not in what might be. Not in the inventory of the unmade." A pause, and the pause had a fall in it. "There is no vantage from which that child was ever visible to me. And yet there the child stands, in a photograph, with a father's hand upon their shoulder. Which means the erasure I am looking at was performed in a place *I cannot watch*." His hand lowered. "I did not know there was such a place. I have been unsettled in this theatre before. This is the first time I have been *blind* in it."

Far below, the front row had drawn together into a knot — eight women, arms around Asia, around each other, staring up at the gap in their own golden hour. And on the screen, frozen mid-laugh in try three, Issei Hyoudou's hand rested on the shoulder of a child that flickered, and the theatre noticed, at last, the detail that would keep the room awake long after the lights went down. In every other frame, in every other try, in three hundred stacked memories of the montage, Issei's face had been open, loud, entirely legible — a face that had never once played poker.

In this frame, and only in this frame, he wasn't looking at the camera.

He was looking down at the child. And he was smiling the way his father smiled from behind the newspaper.

"He knew them," Irina whispered. Her voice broke on it. "Look at him. Whoever that is — wherever they took them *from* — Issei knew them, and loved them, and he doesn't remember either, and nobody ever got to *mourn*—"

The screen went dark. Not dimmed — dark, all at once, as if it, too, needed a moment.

Then, small and white, center screen:

**ARC THREE COMPLETE: FAMILY, WIVES AND NEST LAW.**

**YOU HAVE SEEN WHAT THE DRAGON GUARDS. YOU HAVE NOT SEEN EVERYTHING THE DRAGON HAS LOST.**

**IT IS NOT YET TIME FOR YOU TO KNOW WHAT COUNTS WHAT IS TAKEN.**

And beneath it, for exactly one frame — flickering now, static-edged, wearing the same wrongness as the child, as if the sentence itself had been somewhere it shouldn't:

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT.**

The gacha wheel rose toward the ceiling in silence. The warm-gold Family-Class track went out segment by segment, like windows in a house at bedtime, and where it had been, a new band waited unlit — steel-grey, martial, stamped ARC FOUR: HEROES, KINGS AND MONSTERS.

Nobody reached for the smell of the barbecue as it faded. It felt, now, like taking something that belonged to a grave no one was allowed to find.

In the front row, Koneko Toujou finally let go of the ruined seat back. "We're getting them back," she said, to no one, to everyone, in the flattest, smallest, most absolute voice in the theatre. "Whoever they are. Whatever took them. We get them *back*."

**[RECORD NOT FOUND,]** printed the screen, softly, one last time.

"**Then we will make the record ourselves,**" said Ddraig's voice out of the dark — and for the first time in thirty chapters, the dragon sounded neither ancient nor amused, but like a grandfather standing over an empty crib. "**Partner's nest law, little one. Nothing taken from the nest stays taken. I have seen empires learn that. Whatever is out there beyond the Watcher's eyes... it has made an enemy it cannot count.**"

The lights went down on a theatre that had come, tonight, to watch a family — and sat now in the dark, every faction, every aisle, mourning a stranger none of them could remember, which is to say: doing, at last, the one thing the erasure had been designed to make impossible.

## Theatre Ledger

- **New Pulls This Chapter:** *Montage of Ordinary Family Moments* — Family-Class (the banner's finale jackpot: a year of breakfasts, Kunou's birthday, the Ravel-arbitrated chore treaty, walking the warded children to school on foot, and the great barbecue — closing on the eleven-try family photograph at golden hour); *Erased Child Frame* — Secret (a pull the machine itself disclaimed: **"THIS UNIT DID NOT SELECT THIS PULL."**).
- **Secrets Revealed:** Photo try three contains a child, roughly nine or ten, standing between Asia and Koneko with Issei's hand on their shoulder — flickering, static-edged, "edited out of reality." For the first time in thirty chapters the theatre's name-delivery failed: no name arrived, not withheld but *absent*. None of the eight fiancées recognize the child, yet Asia grieves involuntarily; Rossweisse identifies a child-sized "slot" in the family's formations across all eleven tries; Kuroka's senjutsu reads "a hole shaped like a life" — not death, *unhappening*. The frame's edges carry the same excision signature Heimdall and Uatu flagged on the Dragon Blaster scar in Chapter Twenty — but deliberate, "the same knife, held by a hand." In the frame, and only there, Issei looks not at the camera but down at the child, smiling like his father. The machine answered Rias's direct demand with **RECORD NOT FOUND** — its first-ever refusal/failure.
- **Alliance / Opinion Shifts:** *Arc-close stance on the family:* unanimous across the aisle — Issei's household is no longer filed as leverage, weakness, or spectacle but as his moral structure and load-bearing wall; even the villains' gallery formally retires "use the family against him" (Mephisto declares the family's grief-account beyond any ledger he can read and wants no part of its holder; Magneto privately pledges his aid to whoever avenges the erasure). The Fantastic Four and Inhumans, the theatre's resident families, shift from charmed analysis to visceral kinship — and to alarm: Black Bolt stands to testify that "gaps are *made*"; Franklin Richards says the photo "remembers — it's the only thing that still does" and will say no more. Kang drops all conqueror poise: pruned branches do not photograph themselves — either the blade is failing or something beyond the cut is pushing back through. The eight fiancées close ranks around a grief with no name; Koneko vows retrieval; Ddraig invokes nest law: "nothing taken from the nest stays taken."
- **Unresolved Hooks:** **THE ERASED CHILD — the arc's ominous jackpot and the theatre's first true mystery.** Who is the child in the eleventh-try photograph, why does no record of them exist anywhere — including the Watcher's "dark inventory" of unmade timelines — and what performed an erasure in a place Uatu *cannot watch* (his first admitted blindness)? Who or what "counts what is taken," per the screen's arc-close line — and why did the prophecy line itself flicker with the child's static? Why did the machine disclaim the pull — is something else feeding frames into the theatre? Do not resolve; seed only. *Carried forward:* the greyed-out crimson Queen still waits; the excision-signature thread now spans Chapters 20 and 30; next banner lit: steel-grey **ARC FOUR: HEROES, KINGS AND MONSTERS.**
