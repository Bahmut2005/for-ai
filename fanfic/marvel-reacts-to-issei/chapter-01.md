---

One moment, Tony Stark was in his workshop, elbow-deep in a repulsor housing, telling FRIDAY that no, he did not want to hear his three o'clock reminders.

The next moment, there was no workshop.

There was a theatre.

It was the wrongness of the scale that hit him first, before the panic, before the suit-summoning reflex that found nothing to summon. The room was too big. Not stadium-big, not Chitauri-portal big — big in a way his eyes kept trying to file under *architecture* and failing, because the tiered seats rose up and up past any reasonable ceiling into a dark that wasn't darkness so much as a polite refusal to have anything above it. The seats were red velvet. That detail almost offended him. Something had folded him out of his own reality without so much as a light show, and it had upholstery.

And it was *full*.

Steve Rogers was three seats away, already on his feet, shield arm coming up around a shield that had made the trip with him. Natasha was a row down, perfectly still, eyes moving. Thor stood in an aisle with Mjolnir in his fist and thunder in his face. Banner was breathing through his nose in careful, counted breaths. Farther out — and Tony's stomach performed a slow, sinking roll as he kept looking — the crowd simply did not stop. Wolverine, claws already out. Storm with her eyes gone white. Charles Xavier, hands folded, expression carved from stone. Magneto standing beside him like the last twenty years of enmity had been postponed pending new information. A block of seats full of leather jackets and bad attitudes that had to be the Defenders — Luke Cage, Jessica Jones, Danny Rand, and a man in red horns who was tilting his head, listening to a room that Tony suspected had far too many heartbeats in it.

Blade. The Ghost Rider, actually on fire, actually seated, the velvet unburnt beneath him. A pale figure on a silver board hovering above the mezzanine like gravity was a rumor he'd chosen not to believe. A blue-skinned Ravager crew swearing in six languages. Loki. Hela. Victor von Doom, standing alone in an aisle as though the aisle had been built for him. The Green Goblin's laughter, high and delighted, from somewhere in the dark. And up in the highest tier, vast and motionless, a bald titanic figure whose only expression was a faint, disturbed frown: the Watcher, who by every law of his existence should have been watching this from outside it.

Uatu had not been *summoned* to anything in a very long time. His frown deepened.

The violence started about four seconds later, because of course it did.

Hulk happened. Banner's counted breathing lost the count somewhere around *there are villains in this room*, and eleven feet of green rage came up out of the seats and put both fists into the floor of the theatre with a sound like a continent clearing its throat. The floor did not crack. It did not shake. It did not acknowledge him in any way whatsoever, and the silence after the blow was so total that Hulk actually looked at his own hands, betrayed.

Mjolnir hit the far wall trailing lightning that would have unzipped a mountain range. The wall accepted the lightning the way a lake accepts rain. Doom's gauntlets poured emerald hellfire at the ceiling in a spell that had once cracked open a Celestial's armor; the fire simply stopped existing at a certain altitude, without drama, like it had been quietly asked to leave. Nightcrawler bamfed — and reappeared two seats to his own left, sulfur-scented and wild-eyed, because there was no *elsewhere* to go. Magneto reached out for the metal bones of the structure and found, to his genuine unease, that the theatre was not made of anything. Doctor Strange sketched a sling-ring portal in burning orange, and the ring closed on empty air like a mouth deciding not to speak.

"Stop," Strange said, and his voice carried, because he made it carry. He was standing in the central aisle, cloak restless around his shoulders, and his face had the particular pale concentration of a man reading a diagnosis he didn't like. "All of you. Stop. You're wasting your strength and my patience. This structure isn't warded against us." He flexed his scarred fingers, feeling the shape of the room the way another man might feel weather coming. "It doesn't need to be. We are not inside a building. We are inside a *decision*."

"That's very poetic," Jessica Jones called out. "What does it mean?"

"It means the exits don't exist because leaving doesn't exist," Strange said. "Someone rewrote the premises."

"Someone," rumbled Thor, "will answer for it."

"Aye," said Yondu Udonta from the Ravager block, whistle already between his teeth, arrow hanging dead and useless in the air beside his ear, "and I'm gonna be real interested to watch you make 'em, big fella, seein' as my girl won't even fly."

That was when the screen turned on.

It descended without descending — one moment the front of the theatre was a wall of red curtain, the next there was a screen the size of a sky, and every conversation, every threat, every half-formed alliance in the room died at once, because the screen spoke. Not loudly. It didn't have to be loud. The voice was flat and clean and belonged to nothing with lungs, and it printed itself in white letters even as it sounded:

**SUBJECT SELECTED: ISSEI HYOUDOU.**

A pause. The letters hung there.

"Who?" said Peter Parker, from somewhere in the middle rows, voicing the entire assembled might of a multiverse.

The screen did not explain. Instead, something new appeared beneath the words: a great slow wheel of light, sectioned in colors, ticking around and around with a sound like a roulette of destinies. Golden letters assembled themselves above it, one at a time, savoring it.

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2.**

The wheel spun. Rocket Raccoon, who understood exactly one thing about this situation, said "oh, you have *got* to be kidding me, it's a loot box," and then the wheel stopped, and the screen flooded gold, and stamped across the gold in letters that pulsed like a heartbeat:

**"RAYNARE'S DATE AND THE SPEAR THROUGH THE CHEST" — RARITY: SSR.**

"That's the top rarity," Kraglin said helpfully, into the silence. "Super-super-rare. Means it's the good stuff."

"The *good* stuff," Frank Castle repeated, very quietly, from the shadows of the back rows. He was looking at the word *spear*. He had already understood something about the shape of this evening that most of the room had not.

And then the theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

It was not like watching a film. That was the first thing everyone would say afterward, the ones who could talk about it at all. The screen reached out and folded them into the vision the way the theatre had folded them out of their worlds — unskippable, undimmable, no way to look aside because *aside* had been repossessed along with the exits — and suddenly a few thousand heroes, monsters, gods, and killers were standing invisibly inside an ordinary Japanese town on an ordinary spring afternoon, watching a boy walk home from school.

He was seventeen, maybe. Scrawny in the way of teenagers who eat constantly and burn it all off doing nothing. Brown hair, brown eyes, a school uniform worn with the blazer open and the collar loose, a face that had never once played poker and never would. His name arrived in their minds the way names did here, without introduction: *Issei Hyoudou. Second-year, Kuoh Academy.*

He was, at the moment of introduction, being beaten with a kendo shinai by several furious girls for peeping into their club changing room, and he was crying, and his two equally bruised friends were crying, and all three of them were loudly agreeing that it had absolutely been worth it.

"Oh no," said Peter Parker.

"Oh, he's *that* guy," said Miles Morales.

"Every school has that guy," Gwen Stacy said, arms crossed somewhere in the shared dark. "Most of them grow out of it. Some of them get restraining orders."

The vision didn't argue. It just kept showing him, with a patience that began, minute by minute, to feel deliberate. Issei Hyoudou declaiming to the sunset, fists clenched, that his dream — his *destiny* — was to become a Harem King, while his friends Matsuda and Motohama wept with envy at a fantasy none of them had the slightest chance of achieving. Issei Hyoudou getting his scores back and hiding them in his bag. Issei Hyoudou, mediocre at literally everything measurable, loathed on principle by the entire female student body, walking home across a bridge at dusk with his hands in his pockets.

And Issei Hyoudou stopping, and turning, because a beautiful girl in another school's uniform had just called his name — his actual name — and was standing there with her hands clasped and her long black hair catching the last of the light, asking him, with a shy smile, if he would go out with her.

"No," said Jessica Jones flatly, cop-brained and cold, before the girl had finished her second sentence. "Nope. Look at her eyes."

"Her heartbeat's wrong," Matt Murdock said, very softly. He couldn't have explained how he heard a heartbeat inside a vision. He heard it anyway. "It's *calm*. She just asked a boy out and her heart's beating like she's reading a shopping list."

But the boy — and this was the part that would stay lodged under the ribs of half the room — the boy didn't see it, because the boy had never in his life been chosen for anything, and you could watch that fact arrive in his face in real time. Disbelief first. Then a frantic look over his shoulder for the person she must actually be talking to. Then, when there was no one, a joy so raw and so stupid and so *young* that it was hard to look at. He spent the following days in a delirium of preparation the vision refused to compress: agonizing over an outfit, showing up an hour early, buying her a cheap pink scrunchie with most of his allowance because she'd paused in front of it for two seconds.

The date itself was heartbreakingly ordinary. A café. A cinema. Yuuma Amano laughed at his jokes. She held his sleeve. He walked like a boy who had decided the universe was, against all prior evidence, fundamentally kind.

"Kid's not even trying anything," Luke Cage murmured, brow furrowed. "All that Harem King noise, and look at him. He bought her a hair tie. He's terrified to hold her hand."

Sunset. A park, emptying out. A fountain, and the sound of water, and the light going long and orange and then failing, the way it does. Yuuma stopped walking. She turned to face him in front of the fountain, hands behind her back, and asked, sweetly, if he would do something for her, to commemorate their first date.

"Sure!" said Issei Hyoudou, seventeen years old, delighted. "Anything! What is it?"

She leaned in, past his burning ear, and the entire theatre heard her whisper as though her lips were against each of their own ears individually:

"*Would you die for me?*"

The temperature of a few thousand watching hearts dropped as one.

He laughed. That was the horror of it — he *laughed*, asked her to repeat it, sure he'd misheard, and while he was laughing she was already changing. Her clothes ripped away into a leather obscenity, her sweet face aging into something amused and infinitely bored, and out of her back tore two vast wings, black as an oil spill, shedding dark feathers into the fountain spray. She rose against the last of the light and looked down at him the way a woman looks at a mess she has been asked to clean up.

"Fallen," Doctor Strange said sharply, into the dark of the theatre. "Those wings — that's not a mutation, that's not cosmic. That's a *fallen angel*, but the resonance is wrong, that's not any celestial framework I've catalogued, that's not our—" He stopped. He said, in a much quieter voice, to no one: "That's not our anything. This isn't our cosmology at all."

Light condensed in Raynare's palm. It stretched, and hummed, and became a spear — a spear made of light itself, pink-white, spitting like a live wire.

"Nothing personal," she told the boy gaping up at her. "Blame God for putting a Sacred Gear in you."

And she threw it through his chest.

There was no cut away. There was no mercy of editing. The spear went in below the sternum and out the back, and the vision held, steady and close, on everything that a spear of burning light does to a seventeen-year-old body. Issei Hyoudou looked down at it. He touched it, confused, the way you'd touch a stain on a new shirt. His legs stopped answering. The spear dissolved back into nothing — light doesn't leave a blade to bleed around, it leaves a hole — and he folded down onto the gravel by the fountain, in the dark, alone, because she was already gone. Bored. A chore completed.

He did not die fast. He lay there in the spreading warmth of his own blood and his thoughts stayed audible to the theatre the whole way down, and they were not profound, and that was the worst thing about them. He thought that it hurt. He thought that dying like this, without a single girlfriend, was so pathetic he wanted to cry, and then he did cry. He thought about his mom and dad eating dinner without him and not knowing. His vision filled up with red — the sunset, he thought, or the blood in his eyes — and his very last thought, drifting, absurd, was that if he had to die looking at something, the red hair of the pretty third-year from school, Rias Gremory, was a nice red, and he wished—

The vision ended.

The lights of the theatre came up, dim and pitiless, on every hero and every monster at once.

Peter Parker was on his feet without knowing he'd stood, both hands laced behind his neck, walking a tight little circle in the row because his body needed to do *something*. He had held dying people. He knew the exact confused politeness of a scared kid going into shock, the way they apologize, the way they get *small*, and the screen had just played it back to him in high definition. "He didn't — she didn't even land. She threw it from the air. He couldn't have — even if he'd run, he—" He stopped. Miles was crying, silently, furiously, and not bothering to hide it. Gwen had her hood up.

Steve Rogers had gone very still, jaw tight, the stillness of a man adding a name to a list he kept somewhere behind his sternum. Natasha Romanoff was expressionless, which those who knew her understood was not the same as unmoved; she had clocked the tradecraft of it — the target development, the pretext, the isolation to a controlled site at a low-witness hour — and identified it, precisely, as a professional hit on a child, and filed the shooter accordingly.

Frank Castle said nothing at all. He had gone cold and quiet and economical the way he did before work, and anyone glancing at his face found it as informative as a closed door. Wolverine, three sections over, was much the same — claws in, now, arms folded, eyes narrow. He'd smelled the blood in the vision. He'd have sworn he had. "Kid never had a chance," was all he said, and it came out like gravel going down a chute.

Elsewhere, the temperature was different, and the difference itself became a map of the room.

Hela had watched with the mild appreciation of a connoisseur at someone else's gallery. "A clean kill," she allowed. "Wasteful theatre around it, but clean." Thor rounded on his sister with the thunderclouds building, and she raised an elegant eyebrow at him: "Oh, don't. You've killed ten thousand and mourned none of them. He's one mortal. They end. It's what they're *for*."

The Silver Surfer, hovering, said quietly, "I have watched worlds of such boys end in a single hour," and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible flat perspective of the cosmos — and yet he did not look away from the screen, and had not, at any point.

The Green Goblin laughed his high delighted laugh at the spear, at the fountain, at *would you die for me*, and applauded like it was theatre because to him it was. Bullseye whistled at the throw itself — the rotation, the placement — with a technician's sincere admiration, and several people near him moved seats.

But Loki did not laugh. Loki, who knew more than most about wearing a lovely face to lead someone somewhere fatal, watched the boy laugh trustingly up at his murderer and found his own mouth had gone into a thin, complicated line, and was very glad the theatre's lighting was poor. And Doctor Doom had not moved at all through the entire death, arms folded, masked face tilted, because Doom was not watching a boy die; Doom was watching the *light spear*, and the words *Sacred Gear*, and the mechanics of a magic system his libraries had no entry for, and Doom did not like discovering that his libraries had gaps. "Strange," he said across the theatre, not bothering to raise his voice, the first words he had spoken since arrival. "You felt it also."

"I felt it," Strange said grimly. "Angelology as *infrastructure*. Weaponized divinity, standardized, mass-produced. Whoever built that cosmology built it like a government builds an arsenal. And that phrase — 'blame God for putting a Sacred Gear in you.' She wasn't being colorful. She was citing *policy*."

Up in the highest tier, the Watcher leaned forward by perhaps a centimeter. He had witnessed every death in his universe and the deaths of universes entire, and he was not moved by one boy in a park. What moved him — what genuinely, structurally disturbed him — was simpler and far worse: he had never seen that boy before. There was no branch, no timeline, no distant probability in all his ancient inventory that contained Issei Hyoudou. This story was not in his library. And he had been brought here, bound here, to *learn it*.

The screen chimed.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

The wheel spun again, ticking down through its colors, and landed on silver.

**"BOOSTED GEAR FIRST ACTIVATION" — RARITY: SR.**

"Wait," said Sam Wilson, "we're just — we're going again? He's still *dead*, can we—"

The theatre went again.

---

Night, now, in the vision. The park, the fountain, the boy cooling on the gravel with his blood gone black in the moonlight — and something in the pocket of his ruined blazer began to glow.

It was a slip of paper. A flyer, the kind handed out in shopping arcades — *your wish granted!* — with a strange circular sigil printed on it, which some costumed girl had pushed into his hands days ago and which he'd kept because a cute girl had given it to him. The sigil lifted off the paper in lines of red light, widened, and became a circle on the ground beside the body, ornate and turning, and out of the circle stepped a young woman.

She was Issei's age, perhaps a year older. She wore the Kuoh Academy uniform. Her hair fell past her waist in a heavy crimson sheet — that red, the exact red the dying boy had reached for with his last thought — and her expression, as she looked down at the corpse of a boy she barely knew, was not grief and was not pity. It was *interest*. Deep, dawning, acquisitive interest, like a jeweler turning over a filthy stone and catching, deep inside it, an impossible fire.

"So," said Rias Gremory softly, to the dead boy, "you're the one who called me. It seems you're dying — well. Already dead, really." She knelt, heedless of the blood, and studied him, and whatever she saw made her breath actually catch. When she spoke again there was something almost gleeful under the aristocratic calm. "I'll take you. Your life, from now on, belongs to me. Live for my sake."

She produced, from nowhere, a handful of chess pieces.

Red Pawns. Eight of them.

She placed one on his ruined chest. It sank in and began to glow. Her brows rose. She placed a second. A third. The vision felt her growing astonishment as each piece drowned in him like a stone in a well that had no bottom — four, five, six, seven — until she was holding a single remaining Pawn and looking at it, and at him, with an expression balanced exactly between alarm and delight.

"All of them," she murmured. "You need *all* of them?" And she laughed, once, quietly, a laugh with edges on it, and pressed the eighth and final Pawn into the dead boy's chest. "Then all of them it is."

Crimson light swallowed the park. The hole beneath his sternum closed. And in the darkness inside Issei Hyoudou, in a place deeper than his heart, something the size of a mountain range opened one slitted green eye.

The vision jumped — days, the system supplied blandly, *subject resumed ordinary life, memories fogged, attributed events to a dream* — and found Issei again at dusk, feeling strange, feeling *strong* after sundown in a way he couldn't name, standing in that same park because his feet had carried him back to the place he didn't remember dying. And when a second fallen angel found him there and conjured a second spear of light to finish the sloppy job, Issei Hyoudou threw up his left arm in pure animal terror and *screamed* — and his scream was answered.

Red light detonated around his forearm. When it faded, his left hand and wrist were encased in a gauntlet — segmented crimson dragon-scale plate, golden spikes, and set into its back a round green gem like a reptile's eye, glowing from within. A voice came out of it, or out of him, or out of somewhere underneath the world: vast, gravelly, ancient, and unmistakably *entertained*.

**[BOOST.]**

Power doubled. Everyone in the theatre felt it double, because the vision made them feel it — the sensation of every cell in a body suddenly counting for two.

Ten seconds passed.

**[BOOST.]**

It doubled again.

The scrawny dead-last of Kuoh Academy, sobbing, snot-nosed, with no technique whatsoever, punched a soldier of Heaven's fallen out of the sky with one blow and stood over his own smoking fist in absolute incomprehension, and the ancient voice rumbled up out of the gem with something like a chuckle, unhurried, proprietary, speaking to its host for the first time:

**[So you've finally woken me, partner. What a strange one you are. No matter. I am Ddraig — and you and I are going to get along fine.]**

The system's cold text overlaid the image, clinical as a spec sheet:

**SACRED GEAR: BOOSTED GEAR. CLASSIFICATION: LONGINUS. RESIDENT: DDRAIG, THE WELSH DRAGON EMPEROR. FUNCTION: DOUBLES THE WIELDER'S POWER EVERY TEN SECONDS. THEORETICAL LIMIT: NONE.**

The vision released them.

This time the theatre did not go quiet. This time it *erupted*.

"Run it again," Tony Stark was saying, on his feet, hands already sketching math on a holographic interface that didn't exist, drawing in the air out of sheer habit. "Doubling. Every ten seconds. That's not additive, people, that's two to the *n*. Sixty seconds, that's sixty-four times baseline. Ten minutes and — okay, ten minutes and the number stops meaning anything, ten minutes is ten to the eighteenth. That scrawny kid is a *compound-interest bomb*." He turned around, looking for Banner and finding him. "Tell me I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong," Bruce said. He looked slightly ill. "The limit's not the gear. The system said no theoretical cap — so the limit is the *container*. His body. Which means the question isn't how strong the gauntlet is. It's how much doubling that kid can survive holding."

"The gem's an interface," Shuri said, leaning forward from the Wakandan seats, eyes bright with the specific hunger of a scientist confronted with someone else's impossible tech. "Did you see the latency? The activation ran on *emotion*. Terror-triggered. There's a mind on the other end of that hardware."

"There's a *dragon* on the other end of that hardware," said Thor, and his voice had changed.

People turned. The God of Thunder was not looking at the mortals arguing exponents. He was looking at the frozen last frame on the screen — the green eye of the gem — and his face wore an expression very few of them had seen on it: the sober, wary respect of a warrior culture's oldest stories waking up. "I have fought dragons," Thor said. "Asgard has slain them, bargained with them, buried heroes beneath their skeletons. And when that voice spoke, I felt it in my teeth. That is no beast. That is an *elder power* — something from the roots of its world — and it is *sealed in a dead schoolboy's arm*." He shook his head slowly. "Whoever imprisoned such a thing in a trinket for mortals to carry was either a god of terrible strength or a fool of terrible arrogance."

"In my experience," Loki murmured, "those are the same person." But his eyes, too, had not left the gem, and there was a covetous, calculating flicker in them that Thor knew of old and did not like. A dragon of the old kind, bound, portable, and currently wasted on an idiot. Loki filed that away with great care.

Down among the Midnight Suns, Blade turned a stake over and over in his fingers. "Y'all are missing the transaction," he said flatly. "Watch the girl again. Red hair. She didn't save him. She *bought* him. 'Your life belongs to me. Live for my sake.' That's not a rescue, that's a bill of sale, and the kid never got to read the contract because he was dead when he signed."

"He is correct," said a smooth, warm, appalling voice from a dark corner of the villains' tier, and heads snapped around, because Mephisto had been so quiet until now that many had managed to forget he was there. The demon lord was smiling the way other beings breathe. "And oh, it is *elegant*. Chess pieces. They've standardized reincarnation into a *game system* — rated, slotted, transferable. A Pawn's worth of soul, a Knight's worth, a Rook's." He steepled his fingers, and his smile widened into something with far too much delight in it. "But do attend to the arithmetic, little heroes, since arithmetic is what excites you. The pretty princess came expecting to spend one piece. Perhaps two. That is what a peasant costs. She spent *all eight* — her entire allotment, her whole hand of Pawns, on one lecherous dead schoolboy — and she *laughed* when she did it." His eyes gleamed like banked coals. "I have made markets in souls for a very long time. When a buyer empties her purse without haggling, it is because she has seen what the seller has not: that the goods are priceless. That girl knows something about that boy. And now, so do I — he is worth more than she paid."

"Which raises the better question," said Doctor Strange, and though he spoke quietly the whole theatre bent toward him, because he was staring not at the screen but at the ceiling, at the dark above the dark, at whatever had built this place. "Not what the boy is worth. What *we're* doing here." He turned in a slow circle, taking in the tiers — Avengers and X-Men, devils of their own acquaintance and gods of their own pantheons, killers and children and cosmic constants, everyone, *everyone*, the entire strategic depth of an entire multiverse gathered into one indestructible room. "Think about the guest list. It didn't summon Earth. It didn't summon the Avengers. It summoned *all of us* — every hero, every monster, every power our reality contains — and sat us down in front of one ordinary boy from a universe even the Watcher has never seen." He let that land. Above them all, Uatu's silence confirmed it more loudly than words could have. "You don't assemble an audience like this for entertainment. You assemble it for a *briefing*. Somebody — something — believes that every being in our multiverse needs to know who Issei Hyoudou is."

"Or," said Natasha Romanoff, softly, "needs to know him *before* something. You don't brief an army on a stranger unless the stranger's coming."

The silence that followed had a new texture to it.

And into that silence, the screen flickered once — almost gently, almost *fondly* — and printed a single line of white text, unannounced, unexplained, in a smaller font than before, as if it were talking to itself:

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT.**

Then the words were gone, so quickly that half the room would later argue about whether they had really been there at all. The screen dimmed to a patient, waiting gray. The wheel of the gacha reassembled itself in a corner, ticking softly, idly, like a heart at rest.

**NEXT PULL PENDING,** it said.

In the highest tier, the Watcher settled back into his seat, folded his great hands, and — for the first time in longer than most universes had existed — waited to find out what happened next.

**Secrets Revealed:**
- The subject, Issei Hyoudou, comes from a cosmology entirely outside the Marvel multiverse — even the Watcher has no record of him or his world.
- His world runs on an industrialized supernatural order: fallen angels acting on policy, "Sacred Gears" implanted by that world's God, and Devils who reincarnate the dead via a standardized chess-piece system ("Evil Pieces").
- Issei's Sacred Gear, the Boosted Gear, is Longinus-class: it houses Ddraig, the Welsh Dragon Emperor, and doubles its wielder's power every ten seconds with no theoretical cap — only the limit of his body.
- Rias Gremory spent all eight of her Pawn pieces to resurrect him. Per Mephisto, that price is wildly abnormal and means she perceived something priceless in him.
- The screen's unexplained final line: "HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT."

**Alliance / Opinion Shifts:**
- Spider-family and street-level heroes: fierce protective grief over Issei's murder; his perversion noted, his kindness and harmlessness noted harder.
- Frank Castle and Wolverine: cold, quiet; Raynare has been privately marked.
- Doom and Strange: open enemies now trading professional analysis — a working truce of curiosity has formed around the unknown magic system.
- Loki: covetous interest in a bound, portable elder dragon "wasted on an idiot."
- Thor: sober, old-story respect for Ddraig's presence; unease at whoever did the sealing.
- Mephisto: delighted; treating Rias's expenditure as insider information about Issei's hidden value.
- Hela, Silver Surfer, and the cosmic tier: unmoved by the death itself — but the Surfer never looked away.

**Unresolved Hooks:**
- Why was this multiverse — every faction, hero and villain alike — assembled to watch this specific stranger? Natasha's read: you brief an army on someone who is coming.
- What did Rias Gremory sense in a dead boy that was worth her entire hand of Pawns?
- The contradiction planted and unresolved: the school's most pathetic pervert is also, somehow, its kindest heart — and something ancient in his left arm has already chosen him. Ddraig called him "partner." Dragons choose. Why him?
