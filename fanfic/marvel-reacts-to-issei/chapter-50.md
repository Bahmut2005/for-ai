# Chapter Fifty: The Captain's Vote

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Ravagers • Guardians of the Galaxy • Avengers

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Ravager Vote on Issei" — **Secret**
2. "Destroyed Trafficking Fleet" — **Epic**

---

On the tenth night of the Ravager arc, the machine did not offer Yondu Udonta the lever.

For nine nights it had. That had been the whole flavor of the thing — the gacha wheel surrendering its crank to a rotating crew of blue-skinned pirates, insulting their intelligence in polite white letters while they argued about whether you could hotwire fate. They had pulled a dragon's pirate code out of it, and a heist simulation that had cost Rocket his dignity in front of two galaxies, and a treasure planet that turned out to be a jail, and a funeral that nobody in the theatre had managed to joke about, last night, not even once. The Ravager block had come into this arc loud. They were quiet now, in the way of men who had been shown a mirror with better posture.

High in the corner of the screen, the crack sat where it had sat for ten chapters — hairline, static-edged, patient. It had not grown. Several beings checked it nightly the way you check a smoke detector. It ticked, very occasionally, like a house settling, and everyone pretended not to flinch.

Tonight the lever was gone, and the wheel spun itself.

**ARC FINALE,** the screen printed. **THE RAVAGER ROLLS: VERDICT.**

**FOR NINE NIGHTS YOU HAVE OPERATED THE MACHINE. TONIGHT THE MACHINE OPERATES YOU.**

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2.**

The wheel ticked down through its colors and landed on a color it had almost never used — the deep, lightless violet the theatre had learned to sit up for.

**"RAVAGER VOTE ON ISSEI" — RARITY: SECRET.**

"Uh," said Peter Quill. "That's — hang on. Secrets are supposed to be about *him*."

**THIS ONE IS,** said the screen, and then, beneath it, in the tone of a subpoena: **THE SUBJECT'S FILE IS NINETY PERCENT COMPLETE FOR THIS ARC'S PURPOSES. ONE FIELD REMAINS EMPTY. THE FIELD IS YOURS.**

**PROPOSITION: ISSEI HYOUDOU ENTERS RAVAGER SPACE. FLYING NO FLAG. ASKING FOR NOTHING. WHAT IS HE?**

Three vertical bands of colored fire kindled across the bottom of the screen, and under each one a word assembled itself, letter by letter, savoring it the way the machine savored all its cruelties.

**WELCOMED.**

**WATCHED.**

**SHOT ON SIGHT.**

**ALL SWORN RAVAGERS AND RAVAGER-RAISED WILL CAST. CAPTAINS' BALLOTS WILL BE READ ALOUD. THE MACHINE DOES NOT ACCEPT ABSTENTIONS. YOU ARE PIRATES. PICK.**

A coin of light appeared in front of every leather jacket in the block — hundreds of them, hanging in the air at chest height, turning slowly. One materialized in front of Quill, who stared at it. "Ravager-*raised*," he read off the screen, and something crossed his face that he covered with a cough. "Okay. Okay, wow, tonight has layers."

"It's a vote," said Sam Wilson, from the Avengers rows, in the careful voice of a man identifying an unexploded thing. "It's making the pirates *vote*. On a kid."

"It's making them go on record," Steve Rogers said quietly. He had leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his eyes were moving across the Ravager block the way they moved across a room before a hard conversation. "There's a difference. Fifty chapters of opinions in the dark. Now it wants signatures."

The Ravager block detonated into caucus.

"He killed pirates," a scarred captain was saying, three rows deep, jabbing a finger at the dark screen as if Chapter Forty-Two were still playing on it. "You all saw it same as me. Crew broke their word about civilians and he put 'em *down*. You want that walking through the Quadrant asking nothing?"

"He killed pirates who broke code," Kraglin said, with unusual heat. "That ain't a threat to Ravagers, that's a *compliment* to Ravagers. That's a boy who read the rules and took 'em serious. More'n Taserface ever did."

"The rules," rumbled Charlie-27, massive arms folded, "are that nobody flies through our space for free."

"The rules," said Aleta Ogord, acid-sweet, "also say we don't deal in kids, and half of you voted to make an exception the year the money was good, so let's not get *scriptural*."

"He'd be the single largest liability in the Quadrant," Martinex said, crystalline and precise. "Every bounty office in six empires would post him inside a week. Anywhere he docks becomes a war."

"Anywhere he docks becomes *defended*," Kraglin shot back. "You saw the vault planet. You saw what he does when somethin' can't run."

Rocket, who held no ballot and had opinions anyway, was standing on his seat. "I wanna state for the record that I ran three hundred and eleven simulations against this guy's house and the *house* beat me, and I am voting welcome, and I don't even get a vote, that's how welcome I'm voting."

"I am Groot," said Groot, solemnly.

"He says his vote's welcome too," Rocket translated. "He and the dragon got history. Don't ask."

Nebula had not joined the caucus. She sat at the edge of the Guardians' rows with her arms crossed, watching the three bands of fire, and when Gamora glanced at her she said, without being asked: "Watched. Everyone should be watched." A pause, machinery ticking in her jaw. "But he'd pass. That's the difference between him and everyone I grew up with. Watch him as long as you want. He'd never notice, because he'd never be doing anything worth catching."

Drax stood. Drax had been waiting, possibly for days, to stand. "When I fought him," he announced, to the entire theatre, "he could have humiliated me. Instead he spoke of my family without flinching from what happened to them. He fights like a man who has buried people." He sat down. Then he stood up again. "Welcome. Obviously. I have finished speaking."

Up in the Avengers rows, Tony Stark had the look of a man watching someone else's shareholder meeting. "I want everyone to appreciate what's happening here," he said. "The most legally flexible demographic in two galaxies is holding a *citizenship hearing*. We couldn't get through one Accords meeting without a civil war, and the space pirates have a functioning electoral process. I need to sit with that."

"They're not voting on whether he's dangerous," Carol Danvers said. Her eyes hadn't left the fires. "That got settled in Chapter Forty. Sovereign-tier, filed. They're voting on whether he's *theirs*. That's the only question his power level doesn't answer."

The coins began to fly. Ravagers cast the way Ravagers did everything — loudly, with commentary, several of them trying to cast twice — each coin of light streaking into one of the three fires, which flared and swallowed and kept no visible count. Whistles and jeers tracked the obvious ones. A long, low *ooooh* tracked the surprises.

The three fires burned. The tally line appeared:

**BALLOTS CAST: 100%. CAPTAINS' READINGS COMMENCE. TALLY IN—**

The wheel moved.

It should not have moved. The vote was mid-ceremony, the tally line half-printed, and the wheel spun anyway, hard, unscheduled, the way it had only ever done when the machine decided the room was about to conclude something without all the facts.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

**"DESTROYED TRAFFICKING FLEET" — RARITY: EPIC.**

"Oh, you *dirty* cheat," Rocket breathed, delighted. "It's stuffing the ballot box."

**THE MACHINE DOES NOT INFLUENCE ELECTIONS,** said the screen. **THE MACHINE COMPLETES DISCLOSURE.**

And the theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

Deep space, in the vision — not their deep space; the stars were arranged by a different hand — and strung across it like a filthy necklace, a fleet.

Thirty-one hulls. The captions supplied the anatomy with the machine's usual clinical economy: a slaver combine operating across three supernatural territories, grabbing from war zones and refugee columns and the villages of species without treaties. Cargo manifests scrolled past faster than reading speed, which was a mercy, because the theatre caught the column headers anyway. *Age. Condition. Projected yield.*

Every Ravager in the block had gone very still. There is one thing outlaw cultures agree on across every galaxy that grows them, and the fleet on the screen was it.

The vision found Issei Hyoudou standing on nothing, in empty space, a mile off the flagship's bow. Crimson armor, wings folded, small against the necklace of hulls. The captions timestamped it dryly — *subject age seventeen; acting alone; no authorization, no backup, no plan on file* — and the theatre heard him ask, quietly, inside the helm:

"Ddraig. How many people in those holds?"

"**Eleven thousand,**" said the dragon, low. "**Give or take the ones who didn't survive the week. Partner. Your heartbeat is doing the thing it does. I'll ask once: arrest, or war?**"

The boy was silent for a moment. When he answered, the theatre had heard the voice before — twice. Once over Asia Argento. Once over a vault that turned out to be a prison.

"Both. Holds first."

**[BOOST.]**

What followed took eleven minutes, and the vision refused to compress a single one.

He hit the flagship first — not its bridge, its *collar grid*, the network node that wired eleven thousand slave collars to one master switch, and the theatre watched Penetrate go through four decks of shielding like an opinion through a committee and kill the switch before any hand aboard could reach it. Then the fleet's engines, ship by ship, wings of crimson crossing the dark in Knight-speed drumrolls, each drive section surgically deleted, each hull left whole, lit, breathable — and drifting, anchored, *harvestable*. Slavers who surrendered got exactly what the pirates of Chapter Forty-Two had gotten before they broke their word: cable-ties of hardened light and a place to sit. Slavers who turned their guns inward, onto the holds, onto the merchandise, to burn the evidence—

The vision did not cut away from what happened to those. It didn't linger either. It simply let the theatre understand that the count of them was twenty-six, that each had been a choice, and that he had made every one of the twenty-six without hesitation and had not enjoyed a single one.

"He does kill slavers," Nebula said, into the dark, to no one. "I asked, eight nights ago." Something in her voice had come loose. "The answer is still yes."

The last minutes were the ones that broke the block. Issei Hyoudou walking the holds with his helm retracted so that the people chained there could see a face — seventeen years old, exhausted, wet-eyed, saying *you're okay, you're okay, we're going home* in a language the captions translated but the tone didn't need. Transfer pouring warmth into bodies too cold to shiver. The manifests — every ledger of ownership, every bill of sale, every column headed *projected yield* — hauled into open space in one mass and burned in dragonfire, so that no record survived saying any of those eleven thousand had ever been anyone's property. And the salvage rights to thirty-one intact hulls, a fortune that made the Collector visibly recalculate, signed over entire — to the cargo. To the eleven thousand, who arrived at the nearest free port not as refugees but as a shipping fleet's owners.

"**For the record, partner,**" Ddraig said, as the boy fell asleep mid-sentence on the flight home, "**you forgot to keep even one ship. Again.**"

"Mm. Didn' forget."

The vision released them.

---

Nobody spoke first for a long moment, because the Ravager block was having a problem, and the problem was that pirates do not cry in public and several hundred of them needed a minute to reestablish this.

**TALLY SUSPENDED PENDING CAPTAINS' READINGS,** said the screen, remorseless. **CAPTAIN UDONTA.**

Yondu Udonta stood up slow, fin catching the light of the three fires. He turned his coin over in his fingers once, twice, and when he spoke he pitched it to carry, the way he did when he wanted a crew to remember something.

"I spent twenty years tellin' myself the worst thing I ever did was fly kids to somebody who wanted 'em for somethin' I didn't check on." He let that sit. The theatre knew the story by now; the theatre had watched him and a crimson dragon talk about it, six nights ago, in a vision neither of them would confirm was hypothetical. "Boy on that screen just spent eleven minutes bein' the man I coulda been if I'd checked. He can dock anywhere I'm captain. He can eat my food an' scratch my ship an' marry whoever the hell else he wants, I lost count." He flipped the coin into the leftmost fire, and the fire went up like a cheer. "*Welcome.* An' I whistle at the next idiot who makes me explain it."

**CAPTAIN OGORD.**

Stakar Ogord did not stand for a moment. When he did, he was looking at Yondu, not the screen, and something passed between the two men that had a hundred years of exile and one funeral in it.

"Welcome's a feeling," Stakar said. "I got the feeling. I ain't voting it." He held up his coin. "A hundred years of code says you watch *everybody* — that's not an insult, that's the job, and the boy would tell you the same. You saw him. He watches himself harder than we ever could. He counts his own kills and hates the arithmetic." The coin went into the middle fire, clean and unapologetic. "*Watched.* And it'd be the easiest watch any Ravager ever stood, and I'd bid for the shift."

**CAPTAIN... TASERFACE,** said the machine, and even the machine's flat white letters managed skepticism.

"It's an intimidating name," Taserface began, standing, and got no further, because he tossed his coin toward the rightmost fire and said "*shoot on sight, he's a threat to our whole way of—*"

The theatre shouted him down.

Not the Ravagers. The *theatre*. It started in the Ravager block — a wall of boos, Kraglin's whole seat cushion, someone's boot — but it spread, rows on rows, Rocket howling insults with both hands cupped, Drax booing with the grave thoroughness of a man completing an assigned task, half the Avengers on their feet, Hercules booing *musically*, and from somewhere in the villains' dark even Mephisto's voice, warm and appalling: "Sir, I traffic in souls and *I* find you embarrassing." Yondu's whistle split the noise once, sharp — the arrow rose, glowing, and hung in the air an inch from Taserface's nose with the patience of a raised eyebrow — and Taserface sat down, and the rightmost fire, receiving his lone coin, produced a small, damp, apologetic *flup*.

**TALLY,** said the screen.

**SHOT ON SIGHT: ONE VOTE. THE MACHINE HAS LOGGED THE VOTER'S NAME AND FEELS NOTHING BUT IS CAPABLE OF IRONY.**

**WATCHED: ELEVEN PERCENT — CONCENTRATED AMONG CAPTAINS AND OFFICERS OF LONG SERVICE, WHO ARE PAID TO VOTE THAT WAY.**

**WELCOMED: ALL THE REST.**

**VERDICT OF THE RAVAGER FLEETS: THE SUBJECT, ENTERING OUTLAW SPACE, FLYING NO FLAG, ASKING FOR NOTHING — IS KIN.**

The Ravager block came up out of its seats roaring, and the leftmost fire climbed the screen in a sheet of gold, and Peter Quill — who would never tell anyone which fire his own coin had gone into, and did not have to, because everyone had watched him cast it the second Yondu finished talking — stood in the middle of it with his jaw working, muttering something about how the weird dragon kid got adopted in ten days and it took *him* twenty-six years.

"Fifty chapters," Steve Rogers said, into the noise, mostly to himself — but Sam heard it, and Natasha. "Night one, that room up there watched a boy die in a park and half of us filed him under victim and the other half under punchline." He watched the gold fire settle. "Tonight the hardest crowd in two galaxies just voted him family, and the only argument was whether loving him required keeping an eye on him." He shook his head slowly. "That's not a threat assessment anymore. Hasn't been for a while. That's a *reputation*."

**SESSION 50 OF 100,** printed the screen, and the whole theatre went quiet at the number, because the machine had never once acknowledged its own arithmetic. **INTERMISSION ACKNOWLEDGED. THE FILE IS HALF READ.**

**FIRST HALF: WHO HE IS. YOU HAVE ANSWERED ADEQUATELY.**

**SECOND HALF: WHAT HE IS FOR.**

And beneath it, one frame, the old line — steady, patient, no longer feeling like a threat and not yet feeling like a comfort:

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT.**

Up in its corner, softly, the crack ticked once — a single new hairline branch, fine as a frost-flower, reaching a centimeter further toward the center of the screen. Ddraig's rumble came out of the dark, low, almost amused, entirely unreassuring:

"**Halfway. Hm. In my experience, partner, the second half of a siege is the one where you learn what the walls were for.**"

The lights went down on the gold fire still burning, and the banner that assembled itself in the dark was not addressed to the pirates at all.

**NEXT: ARC SIX — MARVEL JUDGES THE DRAGON.**

**BRING YOUR OWN GAVEL.**
