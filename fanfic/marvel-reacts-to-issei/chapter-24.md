# Chapter Twenty-Four: Asia's Husband

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Moralists (Steve Rogers, Charles Xavier) • The faithful (Nightcrawler, Daredevil) • Logan • Cosmic tier (Silver Surfer, the Watcher) • Mephisto (briefly)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Asia's Healing and Devotion" — **Family-Class**
2. "Issei's Gentlest Moment" — **Family-Class**

---

The theatre had a new neighbourhood now, and the whole room had learned to orient by it.

Three chapters ago the gacha machine had committed its first true act of malice — or mercy; the vote was still out — and summoned Issei Hyoudou's eight fiancées bodily into the audience, mid-viewing, furious, and extremely real. A long crescent of seats near the front had reshaped itself around them, warmer-lit than the rest of the hall, and the audience had taken to calling it *the nest rows* — quietly, and never within earshot of Rias Gremory.

Tonight the ceiling glowed the colour of a kitchen window at dusk. The warm-gold Family-Class band on the wheel was lit before the wheel even descended, and the chime, when it came, was the small doorbell sound that Sam Wilson had been right not to trust.

The token dropped. The banner unfurled in gold soft enough to read by.

**ASIA'S HEALING AND DEVOTION — FAMILY-CLASS**

In the nest rows, a small blonde girl made a sound like a kettle deciding not to whistle and attempted to disappear behind Koneko Toujou, who was roughly two-thirds her size and did not move.

"It's your banner, Asia," Koneko said, deadpan, patting her knee once. "Sit up. Let them see what he's actually like."

"That's what I'm afraid of," Asia Argento whispered.

Half the theatre had turned to look at her — carefully, the way you look at someone the Ghost Rider once called soul-clean, the girl whose show-trial had put a grey banner in the machine and a crack in two Catholics. It was one thing to have watched her story.

It was another thing entirely to watch it sitting next to her.

The screen lit.

---

It was not one memory. That was the first surprise. The Family-Class banner, it turned out, did not deal in incidents. It dealt in *patterns* — and it laid this one out the way you lay out a year of photographs on a table.

A boy comes home hurt. A girl is waiting.

That was the whole grammar of it, conjugated a hundred ways. Issei limping through the Hyoudou front door with his uniform scorched, mid-excuse, and Asia already crossing the hall with her hands lifting, twin silver rings kindling green. Issei face-down on training-ground dirt with something wrong about the angle of his shoulder, and Asia arriving at a dead run that she would deny was a run, because running implied panic and panic implied doubt and she had none. Issei unconscious after a Rating Game, laid out on infirmary sheets, and Asia beside him on a stool at three in the morning, her healing light long since finished its work, staying anyway. Her head bowed. Her hands folded — folded the way children are taught to fold them for prayer, the way the tribunal had watched her fold them, the fold she had never once unlearned.

The vision let the theatre hear what she was doing, and the theatre went very quiet.

She was praying. Over a Devil. In direct, cheerful, unrepentant violation of every doctrine that had ever thrown her away — a defrocked holy maiden asking Heaven, politely, nightly, to look after the specific Devil in front of her. And each time she prayed, a small spike of pain went through her skull — the vision rendered it as a brief flinch, a hand pressed to her temple, breath held and released — because that was the tax her new nature levied on her old love, and she paid it like a toll she considered fair.

**SHE HEALS HIM AFTER EVERY BATTLE,** the screen annotated, in its warm domestic font. **SHE HAS NEVER ONCE ASKED WHETHER HE DESERVED THE WOUND.**

"Of course she hasn't," Matt Murdock said, low. He was sitting forward, elbows on knees, in the pew-posture the theatre knew well by now. "That's her whole doctrine. One line long. She told the tribunal to their faces." He recited it from memory, because it had been living in his chest for sixteen chapters: "*I don't know how to see someone bleeding and decide they deserve it.*"

On screen, the year of photographs kept turning. And slowly, watching it, the room began to understand what it was actually being shown — because interleaved between the healings, the vision kept cutting to *where the wounds came from*. It was footage they already owned. The cold walk to the church, chapter eight. Fenrir's teeth. The respected, bone-deep loss to Sairaorg Bael. The Knight armour crumpling like foil; the Rook standing into a god-killing spear; the boy on his hands and knees on an edited mountain. Every scar the theatre had spent two arcs cataloguing as *doctrine*, as *ledger*, as *threat assessment* — the machine now filed each one a second time, under its other name.

A thing a girl with green light in her hands had sat up all night undoing.

"We measured him in output," said Charles Xavier quietly.

The Professor had been a background presence for most of two arcs — listening, mostly, in the way of a man who spent his life hearing more than anyone said. Now he had wheeled forward to the aisle, and the theatre's acoustics did what they always did for the softest voice in the room, which was carry it everywhere.

"Twenty-three chapters," Xavier said. "We have classified that boy as an asset, a calamity, a constant, a variable no plan may omit — I believe those were the words. And in all our arithmetic, no one asked the only diagnostic question I have ever found reliable." He nodded at the screen, at the girl on the stool, at the folded hands. "*Who is allowed to see him weak?* You can learn everything about a mind from where it goes when it can no longer perform. His goes home. It goes to *her*, and it lets her see the damage, all of it, without armour and without shame. I have known gods who could not do that." A pause. "I have known X-Men who could not."

"Soldiers, too," Steve Rogers said.

He hadn't meant to say it aloud, from the look of him — but the theatre was in a listening mood, and so he stood by it, arms folded.

"I've written a lot of letters," Steve said. "The kind you write when somebody doesn't come home. And the thing you learn writing them is that the war isn't the whole story of a fighter. The whole story is what they were fighting *from*. Some people go into the fire because they've got nothing else —" his jaw tightened a fraction, an old self-recognition, "— and some go in because they have *everything* else, and know exactly what it costs to lose it. First kind burns up. Second kind is who you build a line around." He nodded at the screen. "Twenty chapters, I couldn't place which one he was. Now I know. She's what's behind him. That's the answer."

In the nest rows, Asia Argento had gone the colour of a sunrise and was being visibly, physically prevented from apologizing to the entire theatre by Xenovia's hand on her shoulder.

---

It was at approximately this point that Mephisto could no longer help himself.

He had been suffering, in his box in the villains' dark, for some minutes — the steepled fingers drumming, the smile reinflating from its chapter-twenty puncture, when the market had moved out of his range and he had said so in front of *Loki*. And now the theatre was serving sincerity, in a warm font, and every soul in the room was drinking it, and there is nothing a broker of damnation finds more professionally offensive than an asset appreciating for reasons he cannot securitize.

"Oh, *enchanting*," Mephisto said, rising, spreading his hands to the room with his very best benedictory sneer. "Truly. The dragon and his little defrocked saint. Do go on weeping, all of you — but let us at least be *honest* at these prices. What are we watching? A traumatized child imprinting on the first creature who didn't discard her, and a hormonal boy playing house with a girl too grateful to refuse him. Devotion?" He laughed, and the laugh was silk over a filing cabinet. "Devotion is merely a debt neither party has learned to invoice. Give me five minutes and a contract and I could show you what it's *actually* wor—"

The theatre turned off his voice.

Not muffled it. Turned it *off*, cleanly, mid-syllable, the way one closes a door on weather. Mephisto's mouth continued moving for a full second and a half of perfect silence — the most articulate silence of his long career — and then the screen, without interrupting the gentle year of photographs playing across its main expanse, dropped a small, tidy, bureaucratic panel of red text into the corner, accompanied by the sound of a ruler striking a lectern:

**THEATRE DISAPPROVAL.**

**VIOLATION: CONTEMPT OF NEST. SECTION ONE: SINCERITY.**

**PENALTY: ASSESSED.**

And Mephisto's box seat — the private, velvet, self-important box he had occupied since chapter one — folded itself up around him like a hand closing around a coin, and deposited him, with a soft *pop* of displaced pomposity, in the front row.

In the nest rows.

Directly between Koneko Toujou and Rossweisse, both of whom turned and looked at him.

The theatre did not restore his voice for the remainder of the viewing. It did, however, hang one final line of red text over his new seat, in the manner of a museum placard:

**THE HOUSE PROTECTS THIS PULL.**

The laughter that went around the room was general, bipartisan, and long — Deadpool had to be thumped on the back twice — but it thinned out quickly into something more thoughtful, because the sharper minds in the audience had caught what the joke was resting on. Doom's mask tilted one degree. Loki's smile arrived and then departed without unpacking.

"Note it," Doom said, to no one and therefore to everyone. "Twenty-four chapters, and the theatre has enforced nothing. Not grief, not spoilers, not Wilson's commentary, which Doom has personally petitioned against. It has let us mock gods, murders, and each other." The mask turned toward the warm gold screen. "*This* it protects. A girl healing a boy. Whatever this room is preparing us for — and it is preparing us; Doom has said so since the fifth viewing — the machine has just shown us the shape of what it considers load-bearing. Attend."

From the cosmic tiers, unbidden, the Watcher said softly: "Attend."

Uatu agreeing with Doom, unprompted, was sufficiently against nature that the entire room elected to move on.

---

The wheel turned again while the room was still smiling — the machine had learned its own comedic timing somewhere around chapter twelve — and the second token dropped with the doorbell chime, gold on gold:

**ISSEI'S GENTLEST MOMENT — FAMILY-CLASS**

"Oh no," said Asia, with feeling.

"Oh *yes*," said Akeno Himejima, resettling luxuriously. "I know which one this is. Ufufu. Watch closely, everyone; you're about to lose whatever composure you've got left."

The screen lit on darkness first. A hallway of the Hyoudou house, deep in the night. And a sound the theatre knew intimately by now, because the theatre had been present, in a sense, at its source: a girl crying in her sleep, small and strangled, the way you cry when the dream is a white stone chamber and five mountains in vestments and doors closing with you on the wrong side.

The vision found Issei Hyoudou standing outside her door in a T-shirt and track pants, one hand raised to knock, not knocking.

The theatre watched him decide something. Then he went downstairs instead.

What followed was, by the standards of a room that had recently watched him bruise causality with a chest cannon, the least remarkable footage the machine had ever screened. A sixteen-year-old boy in a dim kitchen, cooking. Rice. Miso. Rolled egg, on the third attempt, the first two attempts being eaten as evidence. The vision lingered on his hands — hands the audience had seen sheathed in a dragon's gauntlet, hands that had transferred mountain-erasing power to allies — fumbling a chopstick, salvaging the egg, laying out a tray with the excessive geometric care of someone doing a thing that matters more than he knows how to say.

When Asia came downstairs at dawn, hollow-eyed and pretending not to have been crying, breakfast was waiting. So was he, asleep sitting up at the table, hair ridiculous.

She stopped in the doorway. On her face, the theatre watched the nightmare lose.

"He does this," the on-screen Asia said softly — and the vision, in the manner of Family-Class pulls, let her narration drift over the scene like steam off the soup. "Every time. He never says, *I heard you crying.* He never makes me explain. There's just… food. And him. He guards me like — like everyone saw, in the frightening chapters. But that isn't the real protecting. The real protecting is this. He makes the world so ordinary that the bad dreams can't find anywhere to stand."

On screen, Issei jolted awake, saw her, and immediately began narrating the menu at unnecessary volume with his chest puffed out, ruining his own tenderness so thoroughly and so deliberately that half the theatre understood, in one stroke, that ruining it was the point — that the boy knew exactly how to make kindness weigh nothing, so that a girl who had been taught all mercy comes with a tribunal attached could pick it up without flinching.

And then, when the meal was done, the vision showed the last thing.

The two of them, side by side at the window, morning light coming up over the roofs of a sleeping human city. Asia folding her hands. And Issei — a Devil, eight Pawns deep, dragon-marked, oath-sworn against Heaven itself in this very girl's name — folding *his*.

"Partner," rumbled the gauntlet, materializing in faint outline, and there was no alarm in Ddraig's old voice, only a checking-in. "**You know what it costs you.**"

"Yeah," Issei said. "Headache. Big one. It's fine."

"**You could simply sit with her.**"

"She'd know the difference." He shut his eyes. Beside him, Asia had already begun, lips moving, one hand drifting to her temple as the toll came due — and his jaw tightened at the same instant, the same tax hitting the same nerve, two exiles paying it together. "Nobody should have to kneel alone, Ddraig. Not her. Not ever again. If Heaven's got a problem with a Devil in the room, it can send somebody down to explain, and I'll boost until *they* kneel."

A pause.

"**...That,**" said the Welsh Dragon Emperor, who had carried two thousand years of hosts down two thousand roads of ruin, and whose voice had gone suspiciously gravelled, "**is the strongest thing I have ever felt you do.**"

The screen held the image — the dragon and the saint, heads bowed, hurting on purpose, together — and then dimmed, gently, the way you leave a room where something sacred is happening and no one needs you.

---

For a while, nobody managed anything.

Then, from the left flank, Kurt Wagner stood — and the theatre braced, because the last time Nightcrawler had stood for Asia Argento he had been shaking, gripping the seat-back, watching an institution devour its gentlest member. He was not shaking now. He was smiling, and there were tears standing in his golden eyes, and his tail had curled into an almost jaunty question mark.

"Matthew," he said. "I have your answer."

"Kurt—"

"No, no, you asked me — sixteen chapters ago, you asked me, and I could not answer then, so I will answer now, while I have the nerve." He gestured at the darkened screen with both three-fingered hands. "You asked whether the institution's cruelty indicts the faith or betrays it. And there is the verdict, mein Freund. The tribunal took everything institutional away from her. The habit, the walls, the name, the sanction — every fence, gone. And what remained? What was left when they had stripped away every single thing that men control?" His voice cracked, cheerfully, like a bell that didn't mind. "*The grace remained.* The prayer remained. The mercy remained — it is stronger, it has taught itself to a *Devil*, it is teaching itself to this entire room right now. The fence was never load-bearing, Matthew. The grace was. They kept the fence and lost her, and she walked out of that church carrying everything in it that was true."

Daredevil was quiet for a long moment. The theatre, which translated everything for him, had given him that dawn kitchen in heartbeats and breath — two hearts settling out of nightmare-rhythm into something slow and safe — and whatever he had heard in it was still moving through his face.

"Two exiles saying grace over breakfast," he said finally. "Both of them getting hurt for it. Both of them doing it anyway." He laughed, once, and this time there was no rust in it at all. "You know what that is, Kurt? Structurally? That's a church. Congregation of two. Better attendance record than mine." He tipped his head back against the seat. "Yeah. Okay. Some days the ledger's not so ugly. Write this one down."

"Way ahead of you," said Kamala Khan thickly, from three rows over, writing it down.

And then there was Logan.

He hadn't said a word all viewing. He'd sat where he always sat, low in the seat, arms crossed, the posture of a man who had come to the theatre against his will and stayed twenty-four chapters for reasons he declined to examine. The room had mostly stopped expecting commentary from him — his last significant entry in anyone's ledger had been the private mark he and Frank Castle had put next to the name Raynare, back when this whole story had been a murdered kid and a debt.

Now he uncrossed his arms.

"I've known a lot of berserkers," Logan said. Just that, at first. His voice came out at gravel-pitch, unhurried, and the theatre gave it room. "Am one. Buried most of the rest. And there's a thing nobody tells you about the animal — everybody figures the hard part's the rage. It ain't. Rage is easy. Rage is *simple*." He nodded once at the dark screen. "The hard part is what that kid just did. Comin' back from the cold place with your hands still fit for somethin' soft. Most of us can't. You go to where he went — chapter eight, that walk up the hill, I *know* that walk — and mostly you don't come all the way back from it. Somethin' stays out there. And the people who love you get whatever's left, and learn to flinch."

He looked at the nest rows. At Asia, specifically, who looked back with her hands folded.

"Kid walks it both directions," Logan said. "Full trip, every time. Cold enough to scare a room fulla gods, and then he goes home and makes the eggs three times 'cause the first two weren't good enough for her." He settled back down, arms recrossing, conversation visibly ending. "That ain't a power-up. Fifty years I've been lookin' for that trick. Respect."

From Logan, the theatre understood, this was a papal blessing.

---

High above them, the cosmic tier had been conducting its own quieter reckoning.

The Silver Surfer had drifted forward again, board angled toward the dark screen — but where chapter twenty had bent him toward it like a compass finding a pole, tonight he simply *looked*, chrome face full of something older than assessment.

"I burned my capacity for this," the Surfer said, "as the price of power. A world, a woman, a small life of gardens — traded, that they might survive without me. It is the oldest arithmetic I know: the strength to protect a thing is purchased with the thing itself. Every herald learns it. Most gods assume it." His gaze went to the nest rows, to the small blonde girl. "The boy refuses the arithmetic. He grows monstrous in strength and *keeps the gardens.* Holds both, pays both tolls, kneels beside her with a headache rather than above her with a halo." The board turned slowly. "If the universe permits him this — if it does not eventually present him the bill I paid — then the universe is kinder than I have found it. And if it does present the bill…" He was silent a moment. "I find I would cross a galaxy to help him refuse it."

And the Watcher — who had once, over this same girl, closed his eyes for the length of one full human breath and called his own stirring of care *perhaps not permitted* — looked down at the braided thing he had first perceived sixteen chapters ago: her exile and his path, twisted into a single cord.

"It held," Uatu said, mostly to himself, in the tone of a scholar confirming a proof he had not dared to want. "I have watched ten thousand worlds teach me that the shape ends in the fire — the institution devours the gentle one, the avenger goes cold, the cord burns through. I catalogued it each time. This one *held.* The mercy survived the tribunal; the rage came home from the hill; the cord is not a fuse." His vast head inclined toward the screen where the warm gold was fading. "The theatre protects it. I begin to understand why. When the things they cannot stand before finally come—" and every ledger-keeper in the room heard the old prophecy shift its weight in its sleep, "—they will not be stopped by power. Power, those things have already eaten. They will be stopped, if they are stopped, by whatever it is that boy is protecting when he makes breakfast."

No one asked him to elaborate. Watchers never elaborated at the worst possible moment; it was practically the job description.

The lights came up. In the nest rows, Asia Argento sat very straight and very pink while an entire multiverse of heroes failed to pretend it wasn't looking at her — and Steve Rogers, moving with the deliberateness of a man performing ceremony, crossed the aisle, stood before her, and inclined his head.

"Ma'am," Captain America said. "Thank you for taking care of him. We've all gotten pretty invested."

Asia Argento burst into tears, apologized for bursting into tears, apologized for apologizing, and was gathered up by six fiancées at once while the seventh, Koneko, silently handed Mephisto — still voiceless, still trapped in the nest rows, wearing the expression of a demon experiencing secondhand sincerity poisoning at point-blank range — a handkerchief, purely to watch him suffer.

Above the rising warmth of the room, the wheel ascended, its gold band still lit. And as it went, a new token glinted in the queue behind the glass — and the screen flashed one frame of preview, in a crisp, mercantile font that had several billionaires in the audience instinctively reaching for lawyers:

**NEXT: THE LEDGER OF RAVEL PHENEX. ITEMIZED. HE WILL WISH IT WERE NOT.**

"...Why do I feel personally targeted," said Tony Stark, "by a font."

Nobody answered. The lights went down on a theatre that had spent two arcs learning what the dragon could do, and had just spent an evening learning — in the machine's own warm, protected, load-bearing gold — what the dragon was *for*.
