# Chapter Twelve: Defeat Without Surrender

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Villains (Magneto, Doctor Doom, Mephisto) • Antiheroes (Punisher, Wolverine, Elektra, Moon Knight, Ghost Rider, Blade)

---

The theatre had moods, and by now everyone could read them.

After the training chapter — ten days of a boy grinding himself down to the bone to be worthy of a fight he had no business surviving — the room had settled into something almost like optimism. The Avengers' rows were still trading notes on his improvement curve. Shang-Chi had used the word *coachable* again. Even the cynics had begun, privately, to sketch the shape of the story they assumed they were being told: the weakling works, the weakling rises, the weakling wins. It was the oldest arc there was. Half the people in the room were living proof of it.

Then the lights went down to the color of an overcast morning, and the wheel descended, and the room remembered that this theatre had never once promised them a story they'd like.

The wheel ticked through its colors with what felt, tonight, like reluctance. It slowed. It stopped on a modest, unglamorous blue.

**"SECOND RATING GAME LOSS" — RARITY: RARE.**

"Loss," read Frank Castle, from the dark of the back rows, flat as a report.

"*Second* loss," said Wolverine, arms folded. He glanced, without meaning to, toward the villains' tier — toward the aisle where Doom stood, because Doom did not sit, and the theatre had long since stopped expecting him to. Everyone remembered the first loss. The montage chapter — the boy getting folded in half by a devil named Sairaorg Bael, a devil who had been born with nothing and built himself out of nothing but repetition and refusal. Doom had watched that beating the way other men watch coronations.

"They rank it *Rare*," Magneto observed from two rows above Doom, hands folded over the arm of his seat, helmet off, silver hair catching the screenlight. He had been quiet for several chapters. He was the sort of man whose silence accumulated weight. "His victories are Super Rare. His humiliations are Rare. Note what this machine considers common currency in the boy's life, and what it considers precious."

The screen took a breath, and took them.

---

The vision opened on a sky that wasn't a sky — a vast dimensional pocket, the system explained in its bloodless overlay, a replica of a ruined city block sealed inside a bubble where young devils could break each other without breaking the world. **RATING GAME: EXHIBITION FORMAT. GREMORY PEERAGE VERSUS BAEL PEERAGE. YOUTH ASSESSMENT MATCH, OBSERVED BY THE DEVIL NOBILITY.**

*Assessment,* the theatre understood, meant *audience.* Tiered seats in a floating gallery, filled with the old aristocracy of Hell — bored, beautiful, cruel in the specific way of people who have never once been billed for anything. They had come to watch the Gremory heiress's strange little peerage get measured. Mostly they had come to watch the famous eight-Pawn boy, the one with the dragon, the reincarnated *human*, and to enjoy confirming what they already believed about him.

The theatre watched the match unfold with the sound of its own breathing.

It was not a slaughter. That was the first thing the tacticians noticed — Rias played it well, traps and sacrifices, Akeno's lightning turning a whole avenue to glass, Koneko holding a chokepoint two full minutes past what her body should have allowed. But Sairaorg Bael's peerage fought like people who had been drilled by a man who had never been allowed a single shortcut, and piece by piece, call by call, the cold announcer's voice dismantled the board.

**"Rias Gremory's Rook, retired."**

**"Rias Gremory's Queen, retired."**

**"Rias Gremory's Bishop, retired."**

Until, in the cratered plaza at the city's dead center, under the eyes of the floating gallery, there was one Pawn left standing between Sairaorg Bael and the Gremory king.

Issei Hyoudou was already bleeding. He'd been bleeding for twenty minutes. The crimson armor of his Scale Mail — the theatre had cheered that armor, one chapter ago — was crazed with fractures, one horn snapped, the green gem in the chest plate flickering like a lamp in wind. Inside the helmet, the theatre could hear him breathing, and the breathing had a wet edge that made several medics in the audience sit forward.

**[Partner,]** said the deep voice out of the gauntlet, quietly, for the boy alone. **[The armor will not hold. Your body has taken more doosts than it can bank. I can give you ten seconds of everything, or thirty of something. Choose.]**

"Thirty," Issei said. "No — wait." He laughed, and the laugh cost him. "Ten. Let's be rude about it."

**[BOOST. BOOST. BOOST. BOOST.]**

Across the plaza, Sairaorg Bael rolled his neck and smiled — and it was not a cruel smile, which somehow made the whole thing worse. He was a big young man with lion-colored eyes, bare-knuckled, wearing no armor at all, wrapped in a faint standing heat-shimmer of fighting spirit that the system flagged, clinically, as **TOUKI: LIFE-FORCE REFINED INTO ARMOR AND WEAPON BY TRAINING ALONE. NO SACRED GEAR. NO INHERITED POWER. NO CLAN ABILITY.**

"You're still here," Sairaorg said. "Everyone in that gallery said you'd be the first one retired. I told them you'd be the last. Don't make a liar of me, Red Dragon Emperor."

"Wasn't planning to," said Issei Hyoudou, and charged.

What followed lasted four minutes, and the theatre would argue for a long time afterward about what to call it, because *fight* was not quite honest. It was a demonstration with one participant who refused to learn the lesson. Issei hit Sairaorg with everything the previous eleven chapters had taught the audience to respect — boosted blows that had cracked a mountain, dragon-shot that had erased a hillside, the transferred, compounded, doubled-and-redoubled arithmetic that Tony Stark had once called a compound-interest bomb. Sairaorg took some of it on his crossed forearms, slipped more of it with footwork so economical it looked lazy, and answered with fists.

Just fists. That was the obscenity of it. No light spears, no demonic flame, no ancestral annihilation. Knuckles, hips, breath, and fifty thousand hours of practice, delivered with a precision that made Danny Rand's hands close slowly on the seat in front of him.

The first exchange broke the Scale Mail's left gauntlet. The second caved the chest plate and the countdown in Issei's ears — the armor's time limit, already gutted by a long match — began to shriek. The third put him through a wall.

**[BALANCE BREAKER: TERMINATED,]** the system said, and the crimson armor blew apart into embers, and there was just a boy again, in a torn school tracksuit, folding out of the rubble of a fake building in front of the assembled nobility of Hell.

The gallery laughed. Not all of it. Enough of it.

"Stay down," someone called, silvery and amused. "You've been paid for, little Pawn. Damaged goods depreciate."

The theatre — the real theatre, the Marvel one — went very quiet, because Issei Hyoudou put his palms flat on the rubble and pushed.

It took him eleven seconds to stand. The vision did not cut away, because this theatre never cut away, and so a few thousand heroes and monsters stood inside those eleven seconds with him and felt what they cost. The scraped-out hollow where the boosts had been. The two fingers that wouldn't close. The tremor in the thighs, the specific weight of a body that has spent everything and is now writing checks against tomorrow. And underneath all of it, humiliatingly audible, the boy's actual thoughts, which were not noble, and were therefore unbearable: *Everyone's watching. Rias is watching. I'm going to lose. I'm going to lose in front of everyone.*

And then, arriving like a hand on the back of the neck: *Okay. Then I'll lose standing up.*

"Yield," Sairaorg said. He hadn't moved. His voice had changed — the showman's edge gone off it. "You have nothing left. There's no shame in the call. I've made it myself."

"Can't." Issei spat red and got his guard up — a wreckage of a guard, elbows dropping, one eye swelling shut. "Sorry. My King hasn't called it. And you're—" he grinned, split-lipped, entirely sincere, "—you're really strong. If I quit now, I won't get to remember how this feels. I'm gonna need it."

Something crossed Sairaorg Bael's face that the gallery above was too far away to see, and the theatre was not.

"Then forgive me," he said, "for taking you seriously."

The touki came up like a furnace door opening, and he crossed the plaza in a blink, and he did take him seriously — that was the mercy and the horror of it both. Three punches, thrown with full respect, none of them cruel, every one of them absolute. The vision held on Issei Hyoudou refusing to fall for two of them. On the third, unconsciousness took him mid-step — *forward*, the room noted; his last step was forward — and the announcer's voice said, without any inflection at all:

**"Rias Gremory's Pawn, retired. Winner: the Bael peerage."**

The last thing the vision showed was the gallery of devil nobles applauding Sairaorg with polite, dismissive grace — and Sairaorg himself ignoring them entirely, standing over the broken boy being gathered up by the retrieval circle, staring down at him with an expression like a man rereading a letter he'd misjudged.

The theatre surfaced.

---

For a moment, nobody managed anything.

Then the antiheroes' section, which had watched the entire beating in a silence like a held knife, all started talking at once.

"That," said Elektra Natchios, first and coldest, "was the stupidest thing this screen has shown us. And it has shown us his browsing habits." She sat with her ankles crossed, immaculate, unmoved. "He had nothing. He knew he had nothing. The exits were honorable — a formal yield, a tactical retirement, live to train. Sentiment kept him standing and sentiment got him destroyed in front of his enemies. In my profession we bury sentiment early, or it buries us."

"In your profession you died," said Wolverine, not unkindly. "Twice, ain't it?"

"And came back sharper."

"Kid doesn't come back, that's the point." Logan leaned forward, forearms on knees, and his voice had gravel and something else in it. "Look — I get up 'cause I can. Bones knit, meat closes, getting up costs me nothin', it's a parlor trick. That kid's got no factor. Every one of those eleven seconds came outta his actual hide, and he spent 'em anyway, knowing the price, knowing he'd still lose." He shook his head slowly. "I've watched a lotta hard men fold for less. Don't ask me to call it stupid. It's the only kind of brave I still respect."

"It's both," said Frank Castle.

Heads turned. Frank rarely spent words, and the room had learned to attend when he did. He was looking at the frozen last frame — the boy face-down, the last step forward — with the expression of a man doing grim arithmetic.

"Getting up when you can't win is how Marines die," he said. "Seen it. Wrote the letters home after. Any officer worth a damn would've ordered that kid down after the armor broke." A pause, long enough that people thought he was finished. "And every man I'd actually want next to me in a hole would've disobeyed that order. That's the whole problem with the species. The thing that gets 'em killed and the thing that makes 'em worth anything — it's the same thing. Kid's just got more of it than his body can afford. Yet."

"*Yet,*" repeated Moon Knight, from three seats over, with the fractured brightness of a man conducting an internal referendum. "Khonshu's watched every battlefield since Egypt had a river. You know what he says about the ones who won't stay down? Nothing. He goes *quiet.* He's quiet right now." Marc Spector's fingers drummed the armrest. "The god of vengeance has no file for a kid who takes a beating like a sacrament. It's not vengeance. It's not even pride. What do you even call it?"

"Faith," said the Ghost Rider, and the flame of his skull burned low and blue, which those who knew him understood was as close as the Rider came to reverence. "Not in God. In the people watchin' him. He said it himself. *My King hasn't called it.*"

"He said the *other* thing himself, too," Blade said quietly, turning a stake he wasn't going to use. "Rewind it. 'I won't get to remember how this feels. I'm gonna need it.'" The daywalker's jaw worked. "That's not a loser talking. That's a man taking inventory in the middle of a burning house. He was *studying* the beating while he took it. Y'all keep arguing noble or stupid. Check the third option. Patient."

---

And then the villains' tier spoke, and the rest of the theatre — as had become custom when that particular cold aisle stirred — shut up to listen.

"It is neither noble nor patient," said Magneto. "It is a tragedy. I have seen this exact boy before."

He stood. Erik Lehnsherr did not raise his voice; he had never needed to. The screenlight made something bleak of his face.

"I was smaller than him when I learned the arithmetic this theatre keeps flinching from. I watched men of magnificent conviction — rabbis, fathers, boys who stepped in front of their mothers — stand up exactly like that, with exactly those eyes, in front of power that did not respect the standing. Do you know what their courage purchased? Nothing. A number in a ledger. The powerful wrote the ledger and the brave went into it, and the world *applauded politely from the gallery*, precisely as you just saw." His hand had closed, slowly, on the rail. "Conviction without power is not a virtue. It is bait. It teaches the boy that the standing itself is worth something, and one day he will stand up in front of a thing that will not stop at three respectful punches, and every person who cheered his beautiful refusal will attend his beautiful funeral. I do not despise him. Understand me. I *mourn* him in advance. I have buried that boy a hundred times, and I made myself into what I am so that I would never have to bury him again."

The silence after that had grief in it, and not only his.

"Sentimental," said Doctor Doom.

The word fell into the theatre like a gauntlet onto marble. Magneto turned, slow and regal, unintimidated — one of perhaps four beings in the room who could hear that voice go cold without adjusting their posture.

"You disagree, Victor? You? Latveria's whole doctrine is power as the first virtue."

"Doom's doctrine is precise, and you have quoted it imprecisely." Doom had not moved from his aisle. He addressed the room without conceding that the room deserved it. "Power is the first *instrument.* It is not the first virtue. Doom has held power that gods envied and lost it to accidents of fortune — stripped of armor, of throne, of country, cast down among enemies more than once. On those occasions precisely one possession remained, and it was the only one that mattered." A pause, measured. "Doom did not kneel. Doom has *never* knelt — not to Mephisto with my mother's soul on the table, not to conquerors, not to gods, not to fate. Take everything from a man and you learn, at last, what he is made of, because what remains is what he *is.* Tonight the machine took everything from that boy in front of the aristocracy of his world — his armor, his strength, his dignity, his victory — and what remained *stood up.*" The mask tilted a degree. "You mourn him, Lehnsherr, because you believe the ledger is the final document. Doom informs you that it is not. The nobles in that gallery will forget the match. They will not forget the standing. *Doom* will not forget the standing. A man who will not kneel possesses the only crown that cannot be taken, and it is the crown every tyrant secretly checks for, in the mirror, at night."

"Pretty words," Magneto said, "carved on a hundred thousand graves."

"On graves," said Doom, "and on the gates of every prison that was eventually torn down. You survived the ledger, Lehnsherr. Ask yourself with what."

Magneto did not answer. Which the whole theatre understood to be an answer.

And it was into that taut, ringing quiet that the wheel — with tact nobody believed it possessed — began to spin again.

**"SAIRAORG'S RESPECT" — RARITY: RARE.**

---

The vision opened small: an infirmary in the Gremory estate, past midnight. Issei Hyoudou, mummified in bandages, one arm in a sling, asleep sitting up because lying down hurt too much — and the door opening on the last visitor anyone expected.

Sairaorg Bael had come alone, without retinue, without announcement, carrying — the theatre needed a moment to accept this — a basket of fruit.

What followed was not a dramatic scene, and that was its power. Two young men, one bandaged, one bruised across the knuckles, talking awkwardly at first and then not awkwardly at all. And under the talk, the gacha did what it did for context: it opened Sairaorg's life like a book laid gently on a table.

Born the heir of the Great King house of Bael, the highest blood in Hell — and born *without* the one thing that blood existed to carry. No Power of Destruction. Nothing. The clan's verdict arriving before he could walk: *defective.* The theatre watched a small boy and his gentle mother exiled to a frontier estate to spare the family embarrassment; watched cousins with inherited annihilation in their palms sneer at the heir who had none; watched the mother fall into a sleep the healers could not lift, with her son's small hand in hers and a promise being made over it. And then the theatre watched the promise being *kept*, in the only currency he had — years of it, dawn after dawn after dawn, fists against stone, fists against waterfalls, fists against the flat assertion of an entire civilization that a devil is his bloodline and nothing else. No Sacred Gear. No dragon. No eight Pawns' worth of hidden treasure. Push-ups. Ten thousand days of them, alone, until his life force itself learned to stand up around him like armor.

"He is the counter-argument," breathed Magneto, in the theatre dark, before he could stop himself.

In the infirmary, Sairaorg set down the fruit basket and said what he had come to say, plainly, because he did everything plainly.

"Every devil I have ever fought looked at me and saw the Bael name, or the missing power, or the freak who trains. In the plaza today, at the end — you looked at me and saw a *wall you intended to climb.* Do you know how long I have waited for someone to look at me like that?" He held out his hand — the same hand, the theatre noted, that had ended the match. "My dream is to take the seat of a Maou someday, so that every child born with nothing can point at me and know that nothing is a starting line and not a sentence. When I stand for that seat, the whole of Hell will say it cannot be done. I want men around me who don't know how to hear that. I fought you twice, Issei Hyoudou. You lost twice. And both times, the only thing on that battlefield I couldn't beat was your refusal to make it easy." A grin cracked through the formality, boyish and huge. "So. Rival. Next time, make me use the *other* arm too."

And Issei Hyoudou — bandaged, beaten twice by this man, holding the fruit basket in his one good arm — laughed until his ribs made him stop, and shook the offered hand, and said, "Next time I'm gonna win, so you'd better bring the whole peerage, and also more fruit."

**[He means it,]** rumbled Ddraig, to both of them, with an old warmth in the vastness. **[Heaven help you, Bael. He always means it.]**

The final image lingered: the two of them talking until the candles guttered — the boy born with a dragon and no talent, and the boy born with the highest blood in Hell and no power at all — and the system's white text writing itself, small and unbidden, beneath them:

**LOYALTY LOG: SAIRAORG BAEL — EARNED. METHOD: NONE. METHOD: HIMSELF.**

The vision released them.

---

"Well," said Mephisto, into the hush, and for once the warm, appalling voice had a thread of genuine irritation in it. "*That* is inconvenient."

Faces turned toward the dark corner of the villains' tier. The demon lord was steepling his fingers, smiling, but the smile had effort in it tonight.

"Do attend, colleagues, because our little ledger requires revision. I deal in leverage. Everything that walks can be purchased; one merely locates the price — power, love, terror, redemption, a mother's soul." A gracious nod toward Doom's aisle, which did not acknowledge it. "But observe tonight's transaction. The Bael boy's loyalty was not bought with victory — our subject *lost.* Not with power — he *has* none to spare. Not with cunning, flattery, or fear. The price of Sairaorg Bael was *witnessed sincerity*, and the boy paid it by being beaten honestly in public." The smile thinned. "I have no counterfeit for that. None of us do. You cannot fake a currency that is only minted by losing. Every ally this boy collects will be collected the same way, and every one of them will be *unbuyable*, because they did not join him for what he offers. They joined him for what he is." He sat back. "I dislike this stock more with every chapter, friends. It only rises."

Up and down the villains' tier, the recalibration moved like weather. The Green Goblin had stopped laughing somewhere in the second pull and not restarted. Bullseye, connoisseur of professional excellence, was frowning at the frozen handshake as at a trick shot he couldn't reverse-engineer. They had filed the boy under *weak*, then under *lucky*, then under *growing but immature*, and every file kept reopening itself. A thing that cannot be bought, frightened, or made to kneel is not a large threat, said the tier's collective silence. But it is not a *dismissible* one. And they had all, in their separate centuries, been undone by exactly one thing: the file they closed too early.

"Lehnsherr." Doom's voice, pitched to carry exactly as far as he wished. "Your counter-argument. The powerless devil. He did not choose between conviction and power, as your tragedy requires. He kept the conviction and *built* the power, fist over fist, out of nothing, against the ledger of his whole world — and tonight your buried boy shook his hand. Doom merely observes."

Magneto was quiet for a long moment, watching the handshake still glowing on the screen — the exiled son and the murdered boy, the two kinds of nothing, holding on.

"Then let us hope," he said finally, and it came out older and lower than before, "that his world gives him the time to build it. Mine did not give my boys the time." He resumed his seat and said nothing else for the rest of the night, and no one, not even the Goblin, was fool enough to disturb him.

The lights began their end-of-chapter dim. The wheel reassembled in its corner, ticking, idle, patient.

And those seated nearest the villains' aisle would later swear to the rest: as the screen faded on the boy who had lost twice and somehow left the field owning something no victory confers, Victor von Doom looked up at him, and behind the mask — slow, private, unmistakable in the eyes — Doom *smiled.*

Several observers agreed it was somehow worse than the bleeding.

**NEXT PULL PENDING,** said the screen.
