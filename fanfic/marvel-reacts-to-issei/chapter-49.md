# Chapter Forty-Nine: The Ravager Funeral

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Ravagers • Asgardians • Avengers • Cosmic Pirates

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Funeral for Fallen Outlaws" — **Rare**
2. "Issei's Eulogy" — **Rare**

---

Nine nights into the Ravager arc, the theatre had learned the rhythm of its temporary operators, and the rhythm was noise.

The machine had handed its controls to the Ravager section back at the top of the arc — a decision the screen itself had annotated with the label **COMMON SENSE FAILURE** — and every night since had sounded like a dockside bar with a dress code of leather and grudges. They had watched Issei smuggle refugees and execute the pirates who broke their word. They had watched Rocket's heist simulation die four hundred deaths. They had watched Yondu talk fatherhood with a boy from another cosmology and come away quieter than anyone had ever seen him. They had watched Nebula's compatibility score, and Drax's lesson, and through all of it the crack in the screen's upper quadrant had gone on ticking, soft and mineral and patient, like a clock owned by something that was not in a hurry.

Tonight Kraglin Obfonteri had the lever, because Kraglin usually ended up with the lever, the way the first mate ends up with everything nobody else wants to hold.

"Alright, you beautiful hunk of insultin' junk," he said, and pulled.

The wheel spun down through its colors and stopped on the plain silver band the room had learned to read as *Rare* — the breather tier, the low-stakes tier, the tier that had given them Yondu's dental records and a compatibility quiz.

Several people actually relaxed.

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2,** printed the screen.

**"FUNERAL FOR FALLEN OUTLAWS" — RARITY: RARE.**

"Aw, hell," said Yondu Udonta, very quietly, before the vision even took them. He was a pirate. He could read a manifest. He knew what that cargo was going to be.

And then the theatre breathed in, and swallowed them all.

---

Space, in the vision. Not the loud kind. The kind at the edge of a system nobody names in songs — a brown dwarf sulking on the horizon, a debris field still warm, and hanging in the middle distance the broken halves of a ship the theatre recognized, because the theatre had met her crew eight nights ago.

The *Molly Weathervane*. The smuggler that had run refugees out of a dying empire with Issei Hyoudou standing on her hull like a crimson figurehead. Her captain had been a woman with a tin jaw and a laugh like a cargo winch; her crew had been forty-one accumulated warrants wearing coats. They had kept the code. That was the whole of their virtue, the vision's captions noted, with the machine's usual refusal to flatter: **THEY ROBBED THE INSURED. THEY LIED TO EVERYONE. THEY DID NOT HARM CIVILIANS. ONCE.**

The captions laid out the arithmetic of their last hour with terrible economy. A convoy of colony ships — three thousand souls, farmers and children and one bewildered brass band — caught at the jump point by a slaver fleet that had been promised easy freight. The *Weathervane* had been close enough to run. She had jump fuel. She had no contract, no client, no percentage.

She turned around anyway.

The vision did not glamorize the fight, and the theatre understood by now that this was the machine's highest form of respect. Forty-one outlaws held a jump corridor for eleven minutes against odds that needed no dramatizing. They bought the colony ships their spool-up time with hull plate and bad language and, at the end, with the *Weathervane* herself, driven like a nail into the slaver flagship's spine. The colony ships jumped. The brass band, the captions noted, made it to the new world, and plays every founding day.

Eleven of the forty-one got to lifepods.

Thirty did not.

The vision cut — days later, a rust-red moon with a view of that same brown dwarf, and a landing field filling up with the worst-looking honor guard in two cosmologies. Smugglers. Wreckers. Bounty men. Crews that shot at each other on principle, parked wing to wing with their guns cold. And walking among them, in a black suit that did not fit him because he had borrowed it from his own father's closet, a Japanese teenager with brown hair and an aura the locals' instruments refused to file, carrying — the theatre leaned in — thirty small lanterns in a crate.

"He came," said Kraglin, hoarse. "They's outlaws an' he *came*."

"Course he came," said Yondu. He didn't say anything else. His hand had come up, without his permission, to rest on his own chest, over the place where a fin used to be and a heart still was.

What followed, the captions named plainly: **RITE OF THE FLEETS. VARIANT: LOCAL. PATTERN: UNIVERSAL.**

Because it seemed — and the vision let the theatre feel the truth of it settle — that every fleet in every sky eventually finds its way to the same funeral. There were no graves. Spacers don't dig. There was fire, and there were colors. As the brown dwarf set, each crew fired its dead into the dark and lit the sky behind them — banners of dyed plasma poured from engine housings, streamers of flare-light in the colors of each ship, thirty lanterns released from a boy's hands one at a time, rising until they were indistinguishable from the founding stars of new constellations. The whole horizon became ribbons of red and gold and slaver-bane blue, arches of light over a rusty moon, a cathedral improvised out of exhaust and grief.

In the theatre, in the operators' box, Stakar Ogord stood up without a word and took off his hat.

The room heard it then — a soft rustle spreading through the Ravager section like wind through a junkyard, ninety-odd hardened felons coming to their feet, hats and hoods and helmets coming off, because the shape of those lights was *their* shape, the rite was *their* rite with different fuel, and you stand for the colors, that was the whole of the law, you *stand for the colors*.

"Every fleet finds its way to the same sky," Stakar said, to no one, to everyone. His voice was gravel poured over something older. "Hundred thousand light-years and a whole other cosmology, and they still send 'em up in fire so the dark has to look at 'em." He watched the ribbons arch and fade. "Ain't no God of pirates. So we make the sky do it."

Three tiers up, the Asgardian block had gone equally still, and Thor Odinson was looking at the lantern-strewn dark with his jaw set and his eyes bright and eight hundred years of funeral boats behind his face.

"We burn ours on the water," he said quietly. "The ship, the sword, the fire, the long push from shore. I stood so for my mother. For my father the sky itself did it." He nodded slowly at the screen. "I did not think to see the same shape rise out of a smuggler's engine wash. It is the same prayer, spoken with worse grammar."

"It's the only prayer," said the Valkyrie, from beside him, arms folded, voice flat with professional knowledge. "I carried dead to Valhalla for centuries. You want to know the dirty secret of the halls? We never asked how they *lived*. We asked how they *died*. One good death bought the whole ledger." She tipped her chin at the thirty rising lanterns. "By Odin's own accounting, every one of those thieves just outranked half the honored dead of Asgard. Sit with that."

"I don't accept that math," Frank Castle said, from the dark of the back rows, and the theatre's temperature dropped a degree, because Castle had been silent all arc. "Dying good doesn't unhurt anybody they hurt. The ledger doesn't wash. It just stops."

"Nobody said it washes," said Sam Wilson — evenly, without heat, a counselor's voice that had run more grief groups than the room knew. "Funerals aren't for the ledger, Frank. They're for the living. Look at the kid's face."

The vision obliged. It went close on Issei Hyoudou, seventeen, releasing the last lantern with both hands, the way you'd release a bird, and the room could see that he was crying without any attempt at dignity whatsoever, tears cutting tracks down his face while outlaws twice his age and ten times his body count stood dry-eyed around him — and that not one of those outlaws thought less of him for it. Several of them stood closer to him instead, the way people stand near a fire in the cold.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2,** said the screen, gently, for it.

**"ISSEI'S EULOGY" — RARITY: RARE.**

"Rare," muttered Rocket Raccoon, from the operator's rail, in the tone of a man building a wall out of whatever was handy. "Two rares. Whole night of discount sentiment. Machine's gone soft. Somebody pull the — someone else can log this one, I got, there's a — coolant thing," and nobody looked at him, which was its own kind of mercy, and he did not go anywhere at all.

---

On the rust-red moon, somebody had built a speaker's stand out of two cargo crates, and the tin-jawed captain's first mate — captain now, by the worst promotion there is — asked the boy from the black suit to say words, because the *Weathervane* had liked him, and outlaws don't trust their own mouths at funerals.

Issei Hyoudou stood on the crates in his father's suit and was visibly, catastrophically bad at this.

"I'm not good at speeches," he began, which the theatre already knew, and heard him plow forward anyway, because plowing forward was the entire architecture of his soul. "And I'm not going to lie to you, because they'd know. They were liars. Professionals. They'd know."

A ripple of terrible laughter went through the mourners, the kind that keeps a crowd from breaking.

"The people we're sending up tonight did bad things," Issei said. "Real ones. I'm not going to stand here and pretend they were secretly heroes all along, because they weren't, and if I said they were, Captain Marrow would climb out of that sky and take my wallet." He had to stop for a second. The vision let him. "Where I come from, I'm a Devil. That's not — it's a job description, kind of, it's a long story — but the point is, everyone I love, almost everyone, is someone that somebody else gave up on. My best friend was born something people fear. One of the people most precious to me spent years as a criminal, a real one, wanted posters and everything, and she is the best big sister anyone ever had. I have fought people to the absolute edge of both our lives who eat dinner at my house now. So I know this one thing. I know it the way I know my own arm."

He held up his left arm — bare, no gauntlet, just a boy's arm in a borrowed sleeve — and the theatre, who knew what lived in it, held its breath anyway.

"Nobody is finished," Issei said. "Nobody. Not the worst person you ever met, not the worst person you ever *were*. Anybody can turn around. It doesn't erase the road behind you — you don't get that, nobody gets that — but the road ahead is yours until the second it isn't, and these thirty people spent their last eleven minutes proving it. They turned the ship around. That was the choice. Everything they'd done before made it unlikely, and they did it anyway, and three thousand people get to grow old because thirty thieves decided the last thing they'd ever be was *good*." His voice cracked, and he let it, because he had never once in his life protected his dignity when there was something better to protect. "So that's what I'll remember. Not the warrants. The turn. I hope — wherever they are, whatever sky gets them — I hope somebody's telling them it counted. It counted. It counted."

He got down off the crates. The new captain put her hand on his shoulder. Behind them the last ribbons of color came apart into the founding dark.

The vision released the theatre and did not turn the lights up, and for once no one complained, because no one's face was ready.

---

In the Avengers' rows, Scott Lang was crying with the frank, unembarrassed competence of a man who had done his crying in prison and learned it held no terrors. "That's it," he said thickly, to Hope, to Steve, to anyone. "That's the whole thing. That's what you want somebody to say over you. I got a *second chance* and half the guys I did time with never — 'nobody is finished.' Somebody put it on a wall."

Steve Rogers said nothing for a long moment. Then: "I've buried soldiers who lived their whole lives clean and I've buried men who only found their courage at the end. I never once figured out how to weigh one against the other." He looked at the dark where the lanterns had been. "He doesn't weigh. That's the trick of him. He just counts the turn."

"The turn is the only datum that predicts anything," said Nebula, flat and precise, from the Guardians' block — and the room went carefully quiet, because Nebula did not volunteer. Her black eyes stayed fixed on the screen. "Every system I was built by measured origin. Where you came from. What you were made as. His system measures *heading*." A pause, machined and deliberate. "I have changed my heading. His compatibility engine noticed before most of the people in this room did. That is all. That is my eulogy for the thieves."

Stakar Ogord still had his hat in his hands. He was looking, the theatre gradually noticed, not at the screen but down the operators' rail — at Yondu, who had not moved in a quarter of an hour, who was still standing with his palm flat on his own chest.

"Hundred ships," Stakar said at last, rough. "When I go, hundred ships'll send up the colors, on account of that's the rite, and I earned the rite." He worked his jaw. "I took your colors, Yondu. You broke the code and I took 'em, and I'd take 'em again — and yet I just watched a boy in a bad suit stand over thirty men worse'n you and say *nobody's finished*." He put his hat back on, which for Stakar Ogord was the raising of a flag. "So maybe there's an exile someplace that ends. That's all I'm sayin'. Maybe the arrow flies home."

Yondu didn't look at him. Yondu's voice came out low and stripped, no whistle in it at all.

"Kid woulda come to mine," he said. "That's what's got me. All them stars in that boy's world, all them wives an' dragons an' wars — some blue idjit with a bad ledger goes down swingin' one time, an' that kid woulda put on his daddy's suit an' *come*." He blinked hard at the dark. "Ain't nobody taught him to do that. That's just what's in the tank."

Nobody had anything to add to that, so the theatre, in its wisdom, argued about something else, and the gods and the captains and the killers talked ledgers and lanterns for a long while — and no one noticed, or everyone pretended not to notice, that the operator's rail had been short one raccoon since the eulogy's third sentence.

Behind the machine housing, in the dark between two conduit banks where no sightline reached, Rocket Raccoon sat with his knees up and his face pressed into his forearms, shaking, making no sound at all, because he had learned a long time ago on a table in a lab that sound brings attention. Thirty thieves and a sky full of colors. *Nobody is finished. Not the worst person you ever were.* He'd have shot anybody who said it to his face. He cried like something breaking open, alone, where nobody could see.

A shadow fell over him, fifteen feet tall and patient.

"I am Groot," said Groot, very softly.

"I know, buddy," Rocket managed, not lifting his head, reaching up blind so a wooden hand could close around his whole arm. "I know. Shut up. I know."

Above the theatre, unremarked amid the arguments, the crack in the screen's upper quadrant had gone silent. Through the whole rite, through the whole eulogy — those keeping count would confirm it later, and Heimdall, who missed nothing, would not contradict them — the crack had not ticked once. As if whatever was testing the walls had stopped, briefly, to look at the lights.

Then the screen chimed, and the wheel reassembled, and printed the next night's freight in letters that got the whole Ravager section shouting at once:

**NEXT PULL PENDING: RAVAGER VOTE ON ISSEI HYOUDOU. WELCOME. WATCH. SHOOT ON SIGHT. THE FLEET WILL DECIDE.**

"Oh, now it's a democracy," said Kraglin, wiping his face with his sleeve, and pulled the lever to bank the night.
