## THEATRE LEDGER — SESSION 88

**ENTRY 1: THE QUESTION THAT NEVER ENDS**

Peter Parker had what could only be described as a *situation*.

The situation was that he'd spent the last three sessions watching Issei handle complex problems with a kind of calm certainty that Peter had spent his entire superhero career trying to achieve. And now Peter's brain, which never knew when to shut up, was full of questions.

So many questions.

"Okay but like," Peter said, following Issei around the observation chamber like an exceptionally persistent puppy, "how do you handle the responsibility thing? Because I do, like, *all the time*—Uncle Ben, May, all the people in Queens depending on me—and I feel like I'm failing constantly. But you have to be responsible for like, an entire *world* sometimes, and you just... don't seem to be having a meltdown about it?"

Issei stopped walking and turned to face Peter with the expression of someone who'd learned that the best way to handle an anxious spider-themed hero was to actually answer the questions rather than deflect.

"I do have meltdowns," Issei said honestly. "I just do them in private. Or I do them with people who love me. I don't do them in front of the world because the world's fear becomes worse than the actual problem."

"But like," Peter pressed on, because Peter never stopped pressing on, "don't you ever feel like you're going to screw it up? Like, one day you're going to make the wrong call and thousands of people die because of your choice?"

"Yes," Issei said. "All the time. But here's the thing—making no choice also kills people. Hesitation also kills people. The only difference is I can't blame myself for inaction the same way I blame myself for action. So I act. And I accept that sometimes action has consequences I didn't predict."

Miles Morales leaned back in his chair, listening quietly. He was processing this at a deeper level than Peter's surface-anxiety questions.

"How do you keep loving people after you fail them?" Peter continued. "Like, I've lost people. People I was supposed to protect. How do you look at Rias and the others knowing you might fail them?"

"The same way they look at me," Issei replied. "Understanding that failure is part of love. That if you're only willing to love someone when you can guarantee their safety, you don't actually love them. You love the idea of being good enough. But actual love means accepting that you can't guarantee anything except that you'll keep trying."

Kamala Khan was taking notes now, her own anxieties about being a leader suddenly finding voice through Issei's responses.

"Here's another one," Peter said, and he was genuinely trying to understand, not just asking for the sake of asking. "Like, money. I never have money. I'm constantly choosing between fixing my apartment or buying web fluid or helping Aunt May with bills. But you have an entire nation probably depending on you economically. How do you make those choices?"

"Honestly? My wives handle most of it," Issei said with a slight smile. "Ravel keeps a ledger of spending. Akeno negotiates with various factions. Rossweisse manages international finance. I'm a dragon, not an accountant. I recognize the gap in my skill set and I let people with better skills handle it."

"That's..." Peter paused. "That's actually smart. We don't have enough of that in the hero community. Everyone thinks they have to do everything."

"They don't," Issei confirmed. "And the moment you accept that you can't do everything is the moment you become actually effective. You stop wasting energy on things you're bad at and you focus on things only you can do."

Deadpool appeared in the observation chamber like he'd materialized from nowhere, which was probably accurate. He had the energy of someone who'd been listening from the ventilation system.

"Question for you," Deadpool said, pulling up a chair. "Do you ever want to quit? Like, genuinely give up and just go somewhere peaceful and let the rest of the world figure itself out?"

Issei looked at Deadpool with genuine curiosity. "Do you?"

"Every day," Deadpool admitted. "But I can't because apparently I'm good at this, and people need me, and my brain chemistry is a disaster, so I'm stuck. I want to know if you feel the same or if you're actually well-adjusted."

"I want to quit sometimes," Issei said honestly. "But wanting to quit isn't the same as being able to quit. My family depends on me. My country depends on me. The people I've sworn to protect depend on me. So I don't quit. I take breaks. I rest. I let other people carry weight sometimes. But I don't quit."

"That's not well-adjusted," Deadpool observed. "That's just responsibility so deeply ingrained that it's become your fundamental operating system."

"Probably," Issei agreed. "But it's the operating system I chose, and at this point it's pretty core to who I am."

---

**ENTRY 2: THE SPIRAL OF QUESTIONS**

Peter had one more question, and this one was different. This one had weight.

"How do you know if you're doing it right?" he asked quietly. "Like, all the choices, all the sacrifice—how do you know if it actually matters? How do you know you're not just... wasting yourself?"

The theatre went very quiet.

Issei took a long time with this answer. "You measure it in small things. In the person who gets to go home to their family because you were there. In the kid who survives because you made a choice. In the country that gets to exist because you fought to protect it. You don't measure it in grand cosmic victories. You measure it in the fact that people who matter to you are alive and safe."

"That's not quantifiable though," Peter protested. "How do you know it's working?"

"You ask them," Issei said simply. "You ask Rias if her world is better with you in it. You ask your family if you being there mattered. You ask the people you've protected. And you listen to their answers."

Miles spoke up for the first time: "That means you're vulnerable to them telling you no."

"Yes," Issei confirmed. "That's the actual risk. Not dying in battle. Not losing your power. The risk that the people you're protecting could tell you that you failed, and you'd have to accept that."

Kamala raised her hand like she was in class. "I have this problem where I assume I'm failing everyone, even when logically I know I'm not. How do you deal with that?"

"Talk to people," Issei said. "Actually talk to them. Not assume their judgment—ask them directly. Most people are kinder than you think. And if they're not, then you've learned something important about them too."

Deadpool was grinning behind his mask. "This is beautiful. This is like a therapy session, but it's actually working because he's not pretending to have all the answers. He's just saying, 'Yeah, it's hard, and you keep going anyway.' That's better advice than anything the actual therapists say."

"I'm literally just honest," Issei said. "I'm not trying to make you feel better. I'm answering the questions."

"Exactly," Deadpool said. "Honesty is the therapy. That's the secret nobody tells you."

---

**ENTRY 3: THE WEIGHT OF KEEPING GOING**

By the end of the session, Peter had asked approximately seventy-three questions. (Deadpool counted. He was planning to make it a record.) Miles had learned that uncertainty wasn't the same as weakness. Kamala had started a list of people to ask if she was actually failing them. And Deadpool had decided that Issei was officially his second-favorite person in the Marvel multiverse, right after himself.

"One more thing," Peter said as the session was winding down, and Issei could see the genuine vulnerability in the question. "Do the people you love forgive you for the times you fail them?"

Issei looked at Peter directly. "Not immediately. Not always easily. Sometimes it takes time. But yeah, if the people matter to you and you matter to them, they forgive. They might set new boundaries. They might ask you to be better next time. But they forgive."

"How do you know that's not just luck?" Peter asked. "What if you fail someone and they don't forgive you?"

"Then you learn something about that relationship," Issei said. "And you accept that sometimes people can't forgive, and that's their right. Your job is to keep trying to be someone worth forgiving. Their job is to decide if you are."

Peter sat with that for a long moment. Then: "You've thought about this a lot."

"I've had to," Issei agreed. "I've made mistakes that hurt people I love. And I've had to figure out how to live in a world where some of those people decided to forgive me and some decided they couldn't. And I've had to accept both outcomes as valid."

Miles nodded slowly. "That's probably the most real advice I've heard from an adult in a long time."

"It's not advice," Issei corrected gently. "It's just... what happens when you keep living and pay attention."

---

**ENTRY 4: THE UNEXPECTED LESSONS**

As Peter was finally satisfied with his question quota and left the chamber with Miles and Kamala, Deadpool lingered.

"You know what I like about you?" Deadpool said. "You don't pretend anything is simple. Everything's complicated and hard and uncertain, and you just... keep doing it anyway."

"What else is there?" Issei asked.

"A lot of things," Deadpool said cheerfully. "Quitting, running away, giving up, becoming a villain because the world's too hard. But you don't do those. You just keep choosing the hard path because it's the path that lets people you care about live."

"Sounds like you're describing yourself too," Issei observed.

"Yeah," Deadpool agreed. "Except I'm also insane and I make it funny because the alternative is crying about the futility of everything. You just lean into the difficulty and do it anyway. Respect."

After Deadpool left, Issei found himself alone in the observation chamber, surrounded by the questions he'd answered and the young heroes who'd asked them. He thought about Peter's anxiety, Miles's uncertainty, Kamala's doubt. He thought about how the same questions that haunted them haunted him too.

The difference, he realized, wasn't that he had answers. It was that he'd stopped waiting for certainty and started acting despite uncertainty.

The young heroes would learn that too. Eventually.
