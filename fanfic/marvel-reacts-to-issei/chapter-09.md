# Chapter Nine: The First Time He Terrified Heaven

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Asgardians (Thor, Loki, Odin) — Heliopolitans (Horus) — Olympians (Hercules, Ares) — Cosmic Authorities (Uatu the Watcher, the Living Tribunal)

**Gacha Pulls:**
- "Ddraig's Roar at Heaven" — **Mythic**
- "Issei's Oath Against God" — **Epic**

---

The theatre had never been quiet after a chapter ended. Not truly. There was always the low tide of a thousand factions muttering, arguing, grieving, laughing at one another's grief. But after the last chapter — after they had watched a gentle girl be cast out by her own Church for the crime of healing a Devil, after they had watched the boy's face do that *thing* it did, that slow tectonic shift from goofy to glacial — the noise had a different texture. It was the sound of a room recalibrating.

"He's still just a boy," Daredevil said, to no one in particular, in the way a man says a thing he is trying to keep believing.

"Aye," said Thor, from three rows up and one pantheon over. "So was I, once. It didn't take."

The great screen, which had gone dark and patient as a closed eye, brightened. The gacha wheel assembled itself out of golden light — the room had learned to hate and crave that wheel in equal measure — and began to spin. Its segments blurred: bronze, silver, gold, and then colors that had no business existing, colors the theatre only deployed when it wanted the gods in the room to sit up.

The wheel slowed. Clicked. Stopped on a segment that burned like the inside of a star.

**[ PULL: "DDRAIG'S ROAR AT HEAVEN" — RARITY: MYTHIC ]**

A hush went through the sections of the theatre where the divine sat. Mythic. The theatre had thrown that word around exactly twice before, and both times the chapter that followed had rearranged somebody's understanding of the boy on screen.

Before anyone could finish drawing breath, the wheel spun again — the theatre in a generous mood, or a cruel one; with the theatre it was so often the same mood — and stopped a second time.

**[ PULL: "ISSEI'S OATH AGAINST GOD" — RARITY: EPIC ]**

"Oh," said Loki, very softly, leaning forward in his seat with the expression of a man who has just been handed someone else's diary. "Oh, *now* we're getting somewhere."

Odin, who attended perhaps one chapter in five and slept — or pretended to sleep — through half of those, opened his single eye.

---

The screen bloomed into the aftermath of the last chapter's horror.

Night, in that provincial Japanese town. The ruined church on the hill still smoldering in the story's recent memory. And Issei Hyoudou — seventeen years old, dead once already, a Devil for less time than most of the theatre's occupants took to choose breakfast — standing under a sky he had every reason to hate.

The theatre had watched him fight before. They had watched him lose before, over and over, and drag himself up each time with that stubborn, undignified, weirdly magnificent refusal to stay down. They knew the shape of his anger now, after Asia. They thought they did.

They had not seen this.

He was not screaming yet. That was the first wrong note, the thing that made the veteran warriors in the room go still. The boy stood with his fists at his sides and his head tipped back, staring up past the clouds, past the moon, at the throne he could not see and had never been allowed to see, and he was *breathing*. Slow. Deliberate. Like a man counting himself down from something.

"His heart rate," Daredevil murmured, out of habit, though of course he could hear nothing from a screen. But he could read the throat, the jaw, the stance. "That's not panic. That's someone deciding."

On the screen, the red gauntlet on Issei's left arm gleamed. The green gem at its heart pulsed once — twice — like something waking up and choosing to be awake.

**"Partner,"** came Ddraig's voice, that vast rumble the theatre had come to know, the sound of a mountain that had opinions. **"You should think carefully about what you say next. Words spoken in rage travel further than you know. Some of them travel all the way up."**

"Good," said Issei Hyoudou.

And then he told Heaven what he thought of it.

It was not eloquent. That was the second wrong note, the thing that made it land harder instead of softer. There was no rhetoric in it, no theology, none of the lawyerly precision that Daredevil's Church had taught him to expect from disputes with the divine. It was a teenager's fury, raw and specific and *personal*. He shouted Asia's name at the sky. He shouted what they had done to her — a girl who had never asked for anything, who had healed everyone put in front of her without once asking what they were, who had prayed every single day to a Heaven that let her be thrown away like a broken tool. He shouted that if that was what God's justice looked like, then God's justice could come down here and explain itself to his face.

"If You're up there —" his voice cracked, and he did not care, and the not-caring was somehow the most frightening part — "if You were *ever* up there, and You let that happen to her — then You'd better hope I never get strong enough to climb up and ask You why. Because I will. I swear it. On my life, on my death, on whatever I am now — if Heaven ever reaches for someone I love again, I will tear the gates off it with my own hands."

Silence, on screen. Silence, in the theatre.

A newly-turned low-class Devil. Eight pawns' worth of resurrection and a body that still lost fistfights to mid-tier fallen angels. Making an open oath of war against the single most powerful authority in his entire cosmology.

And then the gem on the gauntlet flared, and Ddraig did not tell him to stop.

Ddraig *roared*.

---

The theatre's sound system — if that was even the right word for whatever cosmic mechanism carried the story to ten thousand ears — had reproduced explosions, divine hymns, the death-screams of stray devils, the crack of Kiba's swords. It had never once made the building itself respond.

The roar came up through the floor.

It was not loud the way noise is loud. It was loud the way an earthquake is loud, the way the sea deciding to come inland is loud — a pressure, a declaration written directly into the bones of everyone present, translated by some ancient grammar older than language: *I AM HERE. I STAND WITH HIM. COME AND LEARN WHAT THAT COSTS.*

On screen, the sky over the little town seemed to flinch. Birds went up from every tree for miles. Somewhere, canonically, in a realm the camera did not show, things with six wings and twelve wings and eyes all the way around stopped what they were doing and turned toward a point on the map of Japan that had, until that night, mattered to precisely no one.

In the theatre, drink cups rattled in their holders. Kamala Khan grabbed the armrest. Ghost Rider's flame guttered sideways as if in a wind. And in the pantheon sections — the sections where sat beings who *remembered* dragons, who had fought things of that lineage or fled from them or been them — the reaction was immediate and involuntary.

Heimdall stood up.

Fandral, who had been halfway through a joke, did not finish it.

And Thor Odinson threw back his head and *laughed*.

It was not mockery. Everyone within forty rows could hear that it was not mockery. It was the huge, helpless, delighted laughter of a man watching something he had crossed nine realms hoping to see. He pounded the armrest — the theatre's indestructible construction was, for once, a mercy — and wiped his eyes and pointed Mjolnir at the screen like a toast.

"THERE it is!" he bellowed. "There it is! Did you see him? Did you *hear* him? Not one chance in ten thousand of surviving the fight he just picked, and he picked it with his whole chest! Asgard has raised warriors for a hundred thousand years and I tell you we produce one of *those* a generation if we're lucky!" He turned, beaming, to the rows around him, entirely unbothered by the fact that half of them were staring at him. "You don't understand. You're hearing a boy yell at the sky. I'm hearing an *oath*. In the old way. Witnessed by a dragon. There are laws older than your law, Murdock —" this hurled cheerfully down toward the street-level heroes — "and one of them says that courage is not measured against your enemy's strength. It is measured against your own fear. And that boy just spent every coin of fear he had and asked Heaven if it took credit."

"He's going to die," someone called from the Hell's Kitchen rows.

"Probably!" Thor agreed, radiant. "Gloriously!"

Two seats over, Loki had not laughed. Loki had gone very still, chin on laced fingers, green eyes tracking the screen with the particular hunger he reserved for watching someone else do something he wished he'd thought of.

"You're all watching the wrong thing," he said, quietly, and because it was Loki and because he so rarely spoke without an angle, people listened. "Yes, yes, the courage, very stirring, Thor will be composing drinking songs by intermission. Look *past* it. Ask what the oath *does*."

"It gets him killed," Ares grunted from the Olympian block.

"It gets him *seen*," Loki corrected. "That is a boy at the absolute bottom of his world's hierarchy. Yesterday, no throne in his cosmology knew his name. Devils' nobility saw a resource. Fallen angels saw a target. Heaven saw nothing at all — he was beneath the resolution of their attention. And in thirty seconds of shouting, he has forced every power in his world to open a file on him." Loki's mouth curled, slow and appreciative. "Defiance as theater. An announcement dressed as a tantrum. Whether he intended it — and he didn't, that's the maddening part, he *meant every word*, which is why it works — he has just made himself a piece on the board instead of a square. I have spent centuries engineering moments like that. He did it by *accident*, out of love, and a dragon co-signed it." He sat back. "I hate him a little. Note it down."

"Noted," said Thor, still grinning.

Behind them both, Odin All-Father watched the screen with his one eye, and said nothing for a long moment. When he finally spoke, it was almost to himself, and only Loki was listening closely enough to catch it.

"The dragon did not roar for the boy's oath," Odin said. "The dragon roared for his own. Two vows went up to that Heaven tonight, not one. Whoever sits that throne heard both." He closed his eye again. "I remember when things last swore against *my* hall in that voice. I did not sleep well for a century."

---

In the Heliopolitan section, Horus the Avenger had risen to his feet somewhere during the roar and had not sat back down.

He was not a god given to theatre — ironic, the Asgardians liked to say, for a being with a falcon's head and a sun for an inheritance — and when he spoke his voice carried the dry heat of the desert at noon.

"You are treating this as comedy," he said, and it was aimed at Thor without rancor, one warrior-god to another. "Or as spectacle. It is neither. It is *liturgy*." He gestured at the screen, where Issei stood panting under the reeling sky, the gauntlet's glow slowly banking. "My people understood something yours dress up in mead and boasting. To challenge the throne of your own Heaven is not rebellion. It is a rite. I challenged my uncle for the throne of the gods across eighty years of trial because the order of the world was *wrong* and no one seated within that order would right it. The contest was not blasphemy. The contest was the only prayer left." His talon-tipped hand closed slowly. "That boy just prayed the only prayer left to him. His Heaven took a healer who loved it and threw her to the dogs. What is left to say to such a Heaven except *come down and be judged*?"

"Careful, bird," Ares called across the aisle, lounging with his boots up on the seat in front of him, which belonged to a horrified junior functionary of the Shi'ar delegation. "Keep talking like that and mortals start getting ideas. I've had mortals swear against Olympus. You know what we called them?"

"Ash," Hercules said.

"Ash," Ares agreed, satisfied.

"*Some* of them," Hercules said, and there was a note in it that made his half-brother's smirk falter. The Lion of Olympus was not laughing, which was itself remarkable; Hercules laughed at nearly everything, it was how he stayed sane across three thousand years. He was leaning forward, elbows on his knees, watching the boy on the screen with an old, complicated expression. "Some of them we called ash. And some of them we sat beside at the feast afterward, because they turned out to be right, and we were too proud to admit it until they'd bled enough to make the point unignorable." He rubbed his jaw. "I fought my own pantheon's decrees. I hauled Cerberus into the daylight because a king with a throne's authority told me to and the gods allowed it, and half of Olympus called it hubris and the other half called it labor. I have *been* the mortal shouting upward, cousins. It is not arrogance. From inside, it never feels like arrogance." He nodded at the screen, slow and deliberate, a gesture with the weight of a laurel placed on a stranger's brow. "It feels like the only honest thing left in the world. Zeus can scoff. I'll buy the boy a drink. If he lives."

"He won't," Ares said.

"Odds?" said Hercules.

"Don't," said Athena's empty seat, in the sense that everyone glanced at it and remembered she'd have said it.

---

It was into this — the laughter, the liturgy, the wager taking shape between Olympians — that the temperature of the theatre changed.

There were two beings in the room whom nobody sat near. One stood in his customary place upon a high dais that had simply been there since the first chapter, a vast pale figure with a swollen brow, watching, as he had watched everything, always. He rarely reacted. That was the entire horror of him: a being for whom the destruction of galaxies and the first steps of a child carried the same grave, bottomless attention.

Uatu the Watcher had reacted.

It was small. A shift of the head. A fractional narrowing of attention, the way a telescope tightens focus. But the beings in the room who knew how to read him — and Odin was one, and Loki was another, and the thing wearing the Rider's skull was a third — saw it and understood that somewhere in an infinite catalogue, an entry had just been rewritten.

"You felt that," Loki said. Not a question. He had gotten braver about addressing the dais as the chapters wore on.

"I observed it," the Watcher said, which was as close as he ever came to conversation. And then, because the theatre itself seemed to lean on him, because ten thousand faces had turned toward the dais wanting the thing named, he named it.

"Power," Uatu said, "is common. I have watched a billion beings grow strong. Strength changes what a being *can* do. It does not change what a being *will* do. The boy's strength tonight is negligible. It will not remain so, but that is not what you all felt." His gaze did not leave the screen. "What you felt was a category shifting. There exist beings who accumulate power and spend their eternities defending it. There exist beings who accumulate power in order to ascend a hierarchy. And there exists a third kind — rarest of the three — that looks at the summit of its hierarchy and declares the summit itself illegitimate. Such beings do not seek a better seat at the table. They put the table itself on notice." A pause, and in the pause, the whole theatre heard it coming. "Every truly dangerous being I have ever watched belonged to the third kind. And the dragon knows it. That roar was not encouragement. It was *endorsement*. Ddraig Goch has met gods. Ddraig Goch has *killed* the servants of gods. He does not lend his voice to bluffs."

And then, for one instant — no longer than a blink, and half the theatre would spend the rest of the night arguing about whether it had happened at all — the space above the screen was occupied.

Three faces. One in shadow. A figure so vast that the theatre, which had comfortably seated Celestials, seemed briefly like a diorama in its palm. The Living Tribunal did not speak. The Living Tribunal did not need to speak. Its arrival at any event was itself the entire message: *this has been noticed at the level where noticing matters.*

It regarded the frozen image of a seventeen-year-old boy shaking his fist at the sky.

It inclined its hooded heads — the slightest angle, less a bow than a bookmark.

It was gone.

"Okay," said Kamala Khan, in a very small voice, into the ringing silence. "Okay, so that happened. That's — did anyone else — the giant three-faced cosmic supreme court just *bookmarked* Issei. The hopeless pervert. *Our* hopeless pervert. I don't know whether to be proud or start digging a bunker."

"Both," said Ghost Rider. It was the most words he had contributed in two chapters.

---

The screen was winding down toward the chapter's end. On it, the fury had finally run out of the boy the way fury does — all at once, leaving him hollow and swaying — and he had dropped to his knees in the grass, and the theatre watched the terrifying thing become, again, just Issei: scraped knuckles, tear-tracks he'd deny to his grave, whispering Asia's name like an apology.

**"You meant it,"** Ddraig said, quiet now, the mountain speaking only for its partner. **"Every word. I have carried hosts who swore grander oaths with emptier hearts. Heaven heard you tonight, partner. So did everything else that listens to Heaven's frequency. Your life just became more dangerous and more interesting in the same breath."** A pause, and then, with something that eight chapters ago no one would have believed the great Red Dragon Emperor capable of — something gruff and warm and almost paternal: **"Good. I was getting bored. Now stand up. If you are going to threaten God, you will not do it kneeling."**

And the boy laughed — wet, cracked, real — and stood up.

That was when the theatre's own machinery moved.

Everyone had long since learned to ignore the ambient status displays that drifted at the edges of the great screen: viewer counts that counted no one, cryptic progress bars, the gacha history scrolling in gold. But now a panel that no one had ever seen before unfolded across the bottom of the screen with a sound like a vault door acknowledging a correct combination, and the theatre displayed, in tall unhurried letters:

**[ SUBJECT: HYOUDOU ISSEI ]**
**[ CURRENT POWER CLASSIFICATION: NEGLIGIBLE ]**
**[ POTENTIAL THREAT INDEX: RECALCULATING... ]**

The word *RECALCULATING* pulsed three times.

Then a meter appeared beside it — and everyone in the room understood instinctively that the meter had existed since chapter one, that it had been tracking all along, silently, and that the theatre was only *showing* it to them now, which was somehow far worse. The meter's fill sat at a thin, almost pitiable sliver near the bottom.

While ten thousand beings watched, it began to rise.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. No surge, no klaxon. Just a slow, patient, tick — tick — tick upward, the sound of interest compounding, and beneath it a single line of small print that the cosmically literate in the audience read first and reacted to worst:

**[ NOTE: INDEX WEIGHTED FOR INTENT. STRENGTH PENDING. INTENT: CONFIRMED. ]**

**[ TRAJECTORY: UNBOUNDED. ]**

"Unbounded," Loki repeated, tasting it.

"Unbounded," Thor agreed, and this time when he laughed there was an edge under the delight, the sound of a warrior god privately moving a name from one list to another.

On the dais, the Watcher watched, as he had always watched. But those nearest swore afterward that as the lights came down for intermission, the meter still faintly ticking in the dark like a clock in an empty house, Uatu's attention never once returned to the rest of the room.

It stayed on the boy.

- The Living Tribunal's bookmark: appearances by that being are never without eventual consequence.
