# Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Children He Chose

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Young heroes (Kamala Khan, Miles Morales, Sam Alexander) • Cosmic guardians (Nova Corps) • X-Men

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Adoption of Irina's Children from Another World" — **Family-Class**
2. "Yasaka's Daughter Kunou" — **Family-Class**

---

Seven chapters into the Family-Class banner, the theatre had developed a survival instinct about the colour gold.

Storm-grey meant grief; they had learned that in Arc One. Tournament-hush meant power; that was Arc Two. But the warm, buttery, unforgivably *cozy* gold of the Family-Class banner had turned out to mean something far more dangerous than either: it meant that eight extremely real women were seated in the theatre's newest box — the one the room had privately named the Bridal Gallery ever since the wheel had misfired in chapter twenty-one and summoned them all — and that whatever played next would be *about* them, and that they would have *notes.*

Tonight the gold came up early, and it came up soft. The doorbell chime sounded. And across the front of the mezzanine, where in previous chapters the machine had manifested chessboards and injury ledgers, something new assembled itself out of light: a long, cheerful board, the kind found in pubs and betting shops, with a header in the cozy font Sam Wilson had never stopped distrusting.

**WHO WOULD HE ADOPT?** it said. **LIVE ODDS.**

Below the header: a list of names. Marvel names. With numbers next to them.

"No," said Sam Wilson.

"Oh, it's a *market*," said Rocket, from the Ravager rows, standing up on his seat with the expression of a raccoon who has found God. "It's giving us a *market.*"

"Sit down," said Gamora.

"It has ODDS, Gamora—"

The board flickered as if clearing its throat, and the wheel descended through the dark on its golden track, rings turning with a sound like a house settling in the evening. In the Bridal Gallery, Rias Gremory looked at the board, looked at the wheel, and pinched the bridge of her nose with the weary grace of a woman who had spent six chapters watching a cosmic machine air her family's laundry — once literally.

Irina Shidou, angel of Heaven, self-appointed childhood best friend, fourth bride, was already sinking down in her seat with both hands over her face.

"Oh no," Irina said, muffled. "Oh no. I know what banner this is. I know what it *has.*"

The wheel dropped its first token into the crystal bowl. The chime was not a bell tonight, and not a doorbell either. It was, unmistakably, the sound of a front door opening, and a voice — his mother's voice, the theatre would realise a moment later — calling that dinner was almost ready.

**PULL ONE: ADOPTION OF IRINA'S CHILDREN FROM ANOTHER WORLD — FAMILY-CLASS.**

---

The vision opened on a sky that was ending.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible part. The parallel world on the screen was going out the way a lamp goes out when the bill isn't paid — colour draining from its edges, distances quietly giving up, a horizon that had stopped bothering to stay horizontal — and the captions explained with the machine's usual brutal economy: a small offshoot reality, structurally unsound, evacuating through a dimensional fault under the supervision of Heaven, because Heaven kept its promises even to worlds that had never heard of it.

Through the fault came Irina Shidou in her ace's uniform, wings of white light, carrying two children.

Behind her, in battered Scale Mail with the helm dismissed so the kids could see a human face, came Issei Hyoudou, carrying five.

"Structurally," said Miles Morales, leaning forward, "how is he carrying five children."

"Two on the arms, one on the shoulders, two in the wing-joints," said Kamala Khan, who had not blinked since the pull began. "He's using the *wings* as *seats.* He made himself into playground equipment. I am writing this down."

The evacuation itself was almost over; that was clear from context. What the pull actually wanted to show them was the argument afterward — an argument conducted in a way-station of light between worlds, where a functionary of Heaven's administration (the theatre had learned in earlier chapters that Heaven ran on a literal celestial computer, and had elected not to be okay about it) explained, kindly and correctly, that the seven children on the ledger were unaffiliated souls of a terminated reality, that placement processing would take time, that facilities existed—

"They're coming home with us," said Issei.

He didn't say it loudly. That was what got the theatre. Twenty-six chapters had taught them his volume settings, the *Harem King* declarations, the shouted promises with dragons behind them. This was none of those. He said it the way a man says the sky is up, already turning to count heads, already learning names, and when the functionary began to recite the relevant regulation, Irina — flushed scarlet, halo tilted, voice cracking exactly once — pulled rank as the Ace of Heaven and co-signed.

**PLACEMENT: HYOUDOU HOUSEHOLD, KUOH,** stamped the screen. **CLASSIFICATION: WARDS. GUARDIANSHIP: SHIDOU, I. (SPONSOR) / HYOUDOU, I. (RESIDENCE). STATUS: HOME.**

And then the montage, because the machine had learned what montages did to this audience.

Seven children from a dead world arriving at a suburban Japanese house that had already been rebuilt once — the theatre had seen the Hyoudou residence expand over the arcs into something politely enormous — and Gorou and Miki Hyoudou meeting the news that their son had brought home seven refugee children from a terminated reality by *setting seven more places at the table.* No summit. No crisis meeting. Miki Hyoudou asked whether any of them had allergies. Gorou went to find futons. The theatre watched a small girl who had not spoken since her sky went out get handed a rice bowl and a pair of chopsticks and a seat between Asia Argento and a boy with a dragon in his arm, and watched her decide, over the course of one meal, in real time, that the universe might be willing to continue.

The vision ended on the household chore rota — a running gag since chapter twenty-six — now amended in seven small, different handwritings.

The lights came up on a theatre that was having several distinct emotions at maximum volume.

---

"SEVEN?" said Sam Alexander, Nova helmet under his arm. "He adopted SEVEN KIDS from a DEAD UNIVERSE on his way HOME?"

"Wards," said Irina from the Bridal Gallery, still partially behind her hands, but sitting up now, and there was something under the embarrassment that shone straight through it. "Formally they're wards. Heaven's paperwork is very precise. The paperwork also took me four months and Issei signed his part without reading it, because he'd already decided in the first four *seconds*—" her voice did the crack again, and Xenovia Quarta thumped her on the back with the tenderness of a war hammer.

"She cried for a week when the placement cleared," Xenovia reported to the theatre at large, deadpan. "Happy crying. It was very loud. We are proud of her."

"*Xenovia.*"

Up in the X-Men's block, something had gone very quiet.

They had all noticed it — the whole theatre had, gradually, over six family chapters, the way you notice weather — that this banner landed differently on the mutant rows than anywhere else. Tonight it landed square. Charles Xavier sat with his hands folded, watching the afterimage of a dinner table, and the look on his face was the look of a man reading a mission statement he had written in his own blood decades ago, in someone else's handwriting.

"A school," Xavier said at last, softly, "is what I built, because the world produced lost children faster than it produced homes. I have spent my life on the institutional version of what that boy just did between battles, with a rice bowl." He paused. "I note that his version required no entrance exam."

"Your version has a jet," said Logan, from the aisle seat he always took, arms crossed, and the room braced for the growl and did not get it. Wolverine was watching the screen's dark with an expression nobody in the theatre had a file on — nobody except maybe Jubilee, sitting next to him, and Laura, sitting on his other side, both of whom had *been* the small girl with the rice bowl, in their different decades, and both of whom were looking at Logan looking at the screen.

"Go on," said Storm gently. "Say it."

"Nothin' to say." A long silence. "Kid runs a nest." Another silence, and then, quieter, in the voice he used maybe once an arc: "Takes one to know one. Every stray I ever picked up, I told myself it was temporary." He didn't look at Jubilee or Laura. He didn't have to. "It's never temporary. It's the only permanent thing there is."

The betting board, which had been waiting with the patience of a professional, chose that moment to update. **THE ENTIRE XAVIER INSTITUTE (GROUP RATE)** appeared on the odds list at 12-1, and Kitty Pryde made a sound that was legally a laugh.

---

The Nova Corps had been arguing since the placement stamp, in the low urgent register of people who process refugees for a living.

"I want to be clear about what we just watched," said Richard Rider, Nova Prime, to his row. "Because everyone's crying about the rice bowl, and fine, valid, but I've done evacuation duty on eleven dying worlds and I need this room to understand the *load-bearing* part. Placement is the hard part. Rescue is minutes. Placement is *years.* Language, trauma, food, school, nightmares, the day the kid asks why their sky went out and you have to have an answer ready — that's the job, that's the whole iceberg under the hero part." He gestured at the dark screen with his helmet. "That teenager looked at the iceberg and said *they're coming home with us* in under four seconds. Either he didn't see the iceberg—"

"He saw it," said Sam Alexander. "He's *been* the iceberg. First chapter, remember? Kid knows exactly what it costs to be the one nobody comes back for."

Richard sat with that. The Worldmind, whom the theatre could not hear but whose commentary Richard's face reliably subtitled, evidently said something long. "Yeah," Richard answered it, out loud, tiredly. "Yeah. Corps regulation says never promise a refugee anything you personally can't guarantee. He guarantees it *personally.* That's the difference. That's the whole difference. We built an organization to make that promise because no individual should have to be strong enough to keep it." He looked at the screen. "He's just... strong enough to keep it."

"And that," said Uatu the Watcher, from the high dark, in the tone of a man annotating a file that should not exist, "is not the concerning part."

The wheel was already descending again.

**PULL TWO: YASAKA'S DAUGHTER KUNOU — FAMILY-CLASS.**

---

They knew her before the vision drew her. Of course they did. The theatre had met her at the bottom of a mountain in chapter twenty — the small fox-eared girl who had cried on Issei's shoulder about her stolen mother, the reason the summit of that mountain no longer had geometry — and half the room had spent seven chapters privately hoping the machine would tell them she was all right.

The machine told them.

Kyoto, after. Yasaka — nine golden tails, restored, terrifying, radiant, the leader of the youkai and by every visual metric one of the most beautiful beings the screen had yet rendered — formally receiving the boy who had audited a mountain for her daughter's sake. The theatre expected ceremony. What they got was Kunou hitting Issei at a dead run for the second time in the pull's first minute, climbing him like the playground equipment he apparently was, and appointing herself — with the full nine-tailed authority of a princess of Kyoto — his guide, his student, his correspondent, and his responsibility.

The montage unrolled. Letters between Kyoto and Kuoh in a child's careful brushwork. Kunou arriving for visits with a formal escort she immediately ditched. Kunou being taught to focus her fox-fire by a boy who had learned control the hard way and had the scar tissue to prove it, and who — the theatre noticed — taught her the *safe* way, patiently, the way Captain America had spent two arcs saying somebody should have taught him. Kunou asleep on a train home, fox ears down, gripping his sleeve. Yasaka watching from a palace doorway with an expression that was politically complicated and personally very simple, entrusting the heir of Kyoto to a foreign teenage dragon because he was the one being in creation she was certain would never spend the child as a resource.

And one beat of pure sitcom: Kunou announcing, with royal formality and a full court bow, that when she was grown she intended to marry him — and the vision cutting mercilessly to Issei's face performing a system crash, to Rias's smile going cordial in the way the theatre had learned meant *volcanic,* and to Kunou's mother laughing behind her sleeve in a manner that suggested the treaty negotiations had already begun.

In the Bridal Gallery, present-day Koneko Toujou said, flatly, "She keeps a countdown."

"She's *nine,*" said the theatre, more or less as one organism.

"She keeps a countdown," Koneko repeated, and ate another chocolate.

**CLASSIFICATION,** stamped the screen, over a final image of Issei carrying a sleeping fox-child through a train station with the deadly seriousness of a man transporting a crown. **WARD OF THE HEART. GUARDIAN BY DECLARATION OF KYOTO. STATUS: HOME IS ALSO A PERSON.**

And then, instead of ending, the pull did something it had never done.

It kept scrolling.

**HYOUDOU HOUSEHOLD REGISTRY — DEPENDENTS, WARDS, AND PERSONS WHO SIMPLY MOVED IN,** said the screen, and a list began to unspool down the gold: the seven from the terminated world, each with a name and a small crayon-bright status icon. Kunou, cross-referenced to Kyoto. Asia Argento — *taken in by Gorou and Miki Hyoudou as a daughter, formal adoption unspecified, beloved regardless,* and Asia, in the gallery, went pink and teary while six brides converged on her. Rescued names the theatre didn't know yet, from arcs it hadn't reached — a church-orphan here, a half-youkai there — each flagged **PROTECTED**, each with a story-shaped silence around it.

"How many pages does this have," said Miles, not really asking.

**BIOLOGICAL ISSUE,** the registry noted, in passing, as a header line, and the whole theatre inhaled — **[DATA UNSPECIFIED. NO RECORD DISPLAYED. DO NOT SPECULATE.]** — and the whole theatre exhaled, except for Mephisto, who wrote something down, and the Watcher, who did not breathe anyway but somehow gave the impression of a held breath filed for later.

And at the bottom of the registry, in an entry that arrived with no crayon icon and no cozy font, in plain letters that made the temperature of the gold drop several degrees:

**OPHIS. — RESIDENT. STATUS: FAMILY (SELF-DECLARED).**

The screen showed a small girl in a black dress, sitting on the Hyoudou living-room floor, expressionless, watching television with two of the refugee kids and holding an unopened bag of snacks out to Issei — not as a gift; as a request, because opening the bag was, apparently, his job. The image was seven full seconds of perfect domestic nothing.

Then the screen added the caption.

**OPHIS. THE OUROBOROS DRAGON GOD. INFINITE. ONE OF THE TWO STRONGEST EXISTENCES IN HIS COSMOLOGY. CURRENT PRIORITIES: SNACKS. QUIET. THIS HOUSEHOLD.**

---

The silence lasted four seconds, and then the cosmic tiers stood up. All of them. At once.

"Define *infinite,*" said Richard Rider, very carefully, as the Worldmind evidently began screaming. "Machine. Screen. Define the word as used."

**INFINITE,** said the screen, unhelpfully.

"The dragon god of *infinity,*" said the Silver Surfer, who had come fully off his customary altitude and was now at eye level with the frozen image of a small girl holding snacks, studying it the way he had once studied approaching worlds, "wearing the shape of a child, sitting on a *living-room carpet.* I have knelt to a power that eats galaxies. I know what it looks like when strength beyond argument chooses a form. Forms are chosen *toward* something." He turned to the room. "That being could wear the void. It wears a child in that house. Ask why."

"Because," said Charles Xavier quietly, who had been watching the two refugee kids in the frame rather than the god, "in that house, children are what get taken care of."

The theatre sat down slowly, in stages, like a tide going out.

"Let me — okay — let me just—" Kamala Khan had both hands in her hair. "Recap. The nicest family in two universes has been quietly accumulating: seven interdimensional orphans, a fox princess, a saint his parents adopted, an unknown number of PROTECTED footnotes the machine is saving for later, and an *infinity god* who moved in because his house is the only place in creation where the strongest being alive gets to just... be somebody's kid." Her voice wobbled on the last word and she powered through it. "And the countdown-keeping fox is the *normal* one."

"The Gremory line has a saying," said Rias, from the gallery, and the theatre quieted for her, because when the brides spoke on family the room had learned to listen. "*Once you are ours, you are ours.* I was raised on it. I thought I understood it." Her hand found Asia's. "Then I met a boy who had never heard the saying and was already living it harder than my entire ancient house. He doesn't recruit. He doesn't collect. He just — refuses to walk past. And the lost can tell. They can *always* tell. They find him the way water finds low ground."

"**Nest law,**" rumbled a dragon's voice over the theatre, warm and old, and even the betting board dimmed respectfully for Ddraig. "**You keep calling it adoption. Your word is a document. Ours is simpler: what shelters under a dragon's wing is of the dragon's nest, and the nest is not negotiable, and the wing does not close. My partner never read the old law. He is the first bearer in ten thousand years to be born already obeying it.**" A pause, and the ember of pride in the voice banked brighter. "**The nest has other records. One of them is a brother. But that... is the next telling.**"

The registry's final line flickered — **BEAUCEPHALIS — RECORD SEALED: SEE NEXT PULL** — and the theatre, trained by now, groaned at the cliffhanger in eleven languages.

---

Which left the board.

It had been updating all night, patient as an actuary, and now it flared to full brightness and rang the doorbell chime, and the theatre finally, helplessly, looked at the odds.

**WHO WOULD HE ADOPT? — LIVE MARKET**
**KAMALA KHAN — 3:2 (FAVOURITE).**
**MILES MORALES — 2:1.**
**SAM ALEXANDER — 7:2.**
**JUBILEE — 4:1 (PRIOR EXPERIENCE BEING ADOPTED BY GRUFF DRAGON-ADJACENT MENTOR: NOTED).**
**LAURA KINNEY — 9:2.**
**THE ENTIRE XAVIER INSTITUTE (GROUP RATE) — 12:1.**
**GROOT — 20:1 (ELIGIBILITY DISPUTED: MAY BE OLDER THAN KYOTO).**
**ROCKET — 66:1 (SUBJECT INSISTS HE IS RUNNING THE BOOK, NOT IN IT).**
**LOKI — 500:1. SELF-NOMINATIONS VOID. STOP RESUBMITTING.**

"I'm the *favourite?*" said Kamala, in a voice that started as outrage and finished somewhere in the neighbourhood of a squeak. "That's — I'm not — I have *parents,* excellent parents, I don't need — why am I the favourite?!"

"Fangirl energy," said Miles. "Reads as duckling. He'd imprint in a day."

"Oh, like YOU'RE any better, *two-to-one*—"

"I'm just saying if a guy with a dragon offered to teach me power control without the part where I learn it by almost dying, I would *listen to the offer.* That's not adoption. That's mentorship. There's a difference." A beat. "The difference is about four months, based on tonight's evidence."

"It really is about four months," said Irina, from the gallery, with feeling.

Sam Alexander was staring at his own line. "Seven-to-two and my helmet's a hand-me-down from a whole *Corps* of surrogate dads, this board has no idea how the market works—" at which point the board silently shortened his odds to 3:1, and the Guardians' section made the *ooooh* noise usually reserved for street fights.

And Jubilee popped her gum, looked sideways up at Logan, and said, sweetly, "Four-to-one. You gonna let some *teenager* poach me, old man?"

"Kid," said Wolverine, "I'd help him pack your bags," in the tone that everyone in the theatre — every single soul, hero and villain and god — correctly understood to mean *over my dead body, and I don't stay dead, so never.* Laura almost smiled. The board, which apparently understood the tone too, quietly moved Jubilee to 15:1 with the footnote **INCUMBENT GUARDIAN UNWILLING TO SELL.**

Only one gallery wasn't laughing. In the villains' dark, the beings who collected pawns, hosts, apprentices, and souls looked at a registry full of children no one had leveraged, a fox princess no one had ransomed, and an infinity no one had weaponised, sitting on a carpet waiting for snacks — and did the maths that had been ruining their evenings since Arc Two. You could not co-opt a man by threatening what he loved. Not because he loved nothing. Because he loved *everything,* openly, in a fortified suburban house, with a dragon god on the carpet and eight brides on the walls, and every hostage-shaped plan in the room had just been re-costed against the phrase *the wing does not close.*

"A nest," murmured Doom, who designed around constants now, "is a fortress that recruits its own garrison." He did not open the notebook. He had said it was finished. But his gauntlet tapped the cover, once.

The lights dimmed. The registry rolled itself up like a scroll, paused, and stamped one last line across the dark in the cozy gold font that fooled no one:

**THE LOST FIND HIM. THIS IS NOT A COINCIDENCE. NOTHING IN THIS THEATRE IS.**

And under it, one frame, gone before all but the fastest eyes caught it — the old line, the prophecy, wearing the gold like it had always belonged there:

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT.**

"...Anyone else notice," said Miles slowly, into the settling dark, "that it showed us the *whole* registry, headers and everything, and the one header it slammed shut was—"

"Do not speculate," recited the theatre, wearily, in chorus.

The betting board, dimming last of all, quietly added one more line at the bottom of the market, at odds it refused to display:

**THE THEATRE — [ODDS WITHHELD].**

Nobody laughed at that one either.
