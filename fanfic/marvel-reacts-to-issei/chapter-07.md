# Chapter Seven: Weakness Is Not Innocence

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Avengers (Captain America), Martial Artists (Shang-Chi, Iron Fist), Mutants (Cyclops)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Early Training Montage" — Rare
2. "Beating Sairaorg the Hard Way" — SSR

---

The theatre had learned, over six viewings, to read the great golden wheel the way sailors read weather.

Common pulls meant comedy — Issei Hyoudou faceplanting into something, usually with witnesses. Rare pulls meant story, the connective tissue of a life. And the higher rarities meant the room would go home — insofar as anyone could go home from an indestructible cosmic theatre with no doors — having recalculated something about the boy in red.

Tonight the wheel was already spinning when the house lights dimmed, and the first card that slid out of its slot burned a steady silver.

**[ EARLY TRAINING MONTAGE — RARE ]**

"Montage," Tony Stark said, settling deeper into his seat with the air of a man who had seen every training montage cinema ever produced. "Great. Cue the synth music. Two minutes of jogging, one inspirational sunrise, and suddenly the kid can bench-press a bus. That's how these work."

"That is how *movies* work," Steve Rogers said quietly, eyes already on the screen. "Training doesn't."

Stark opened his mouth, caught the look on Rogers' face, and closed it again.

The screen bloomed.

---

It opened before dawn, which was itself a small betrayal of the genre. No sunrise. Just a grey hillside behind Kuoh Town, wet grass, and Issei Hyoudou on his hands and knees, throwing up beside a running trail while Rias Gremory stood over him with a stopwatch and an expression of clinical patience.

"Again," she said.

"Buchou—" he wheezed.

"Again. Your legs work. I checked when I resurrected you."

He got up. He ran. He fell. He got up.

The montage did not compress this the way montages were supposed to. The theatre's cruel projector lingered — on the fourth lap and the ninth, on Issei dragging a boulder in a rope harness while Rias read a book on his back, on his hands shaking too badly at dinner to hold chopsticks so that Asia Argento, glowing faintly green, healed the blisters and then looked away so he wouldn't see her cry about them.

"He's carrying that pack wrong," Clint Barton observed. "Weight's too high. Rookie mistake."

"He fixes it," Shang-Chi said.

Barton glanced over. "You can't know that."

"Watch his left hand." Shang-Chi hadn't moved since the card was drawn; he sat the way he always sat, spine like a plumb line, hands loose on his knees. "Every time something hurts him, the left hand checks it. Cataloguing. He doesn't complain about the same pain twice."

On screen, the next morning, Issei re-rigged the pack low against his spine without being told.

Barton said nothing, but he sat up slightly.

---

Then came the sparring, and the laughter — which had been ambient, comfortable, the theatre's default setting for this boy — began to die by degrees.

The scene shifted to a gymnasium marked with the crest of the student council, and a girl in glasses watching from the sidelines with folded arms: *Sona Sitri*, the caption read, *Heiress of the Sitri Clan*. Her peerage filed onto the floor, and her Knights moved the way Knights apparently moved — like the laws about where a body could be at a given moment were polite suggestions.

Issei lasted eleven seconds.

He lasted eleven seconds the second time, too, which drew a chuckle from the villain tiers. The third time he lasted nine, because he'd tried something clever and it had cost him. A blur, a flash of a practice blade, and Issei Hyoudou hit the floor hard enough that half the theatre winced in sympathy.

"Boost me all you want," the Knight said, not unkindly, standing over him. "Power you can't land is just noise."

"He's outclassed," Cyclops said. Scott Summers had claimed a seat near the aisle, arms crossed, and he analyzed the screen the way he analyzed everything — as a problem with a solution he hadn't found yet. "Badly. Speed differential like that, raw power's irrelevant. He needs to stop trying to win and start trying to learn."

"He is learning," said Danny Rand.

Cyclops turned. The Iron Fist was leaning forward with his elbows on his knees, and there was something in his face that hadn't been there during the comedy chapters — recognition, old and specific.

"Look where he falls," Danny said. "First bout, he goes down anywhere. Third bout, he goes down *facing her*. He's eating the losses to buy information. In K'un-Lun we had a name for students like that." He smiled faintly. "Mostly it was 'concussed.' But sometimes it was 'dangerous, eventually.'"

Eleven seconds became fourteen. Fourteen became twenty. He never won. Not once, across what the montage implied was weeks. But the Knights of Sona Sitri stopped smiling when they squared up against him, and that was its own kind of scoreboard.

---

And then there was Koneko.

The theatre had mostly filed Koneko Toujou under *small, quiet, eats sweets* — a background figure, four-foot-something of white hair and flat affect. The screen now dismantled that filing system with prejudice.

"Spar with me," Issei said in the flashback, bouncing on his toes, gauntlet gleaming. "I've been getting faster. I can feel it."

Koneko looked up at him from her seat on the clubroom couch. She finished her youkan. She set down the plate.

"...Okay," she said. "Don't cry this time."

"That was ONE time—"

What followed was not a fight so much as an essay, written by a very small girl, on the subject of Issei Hyoudou's habits. He feinted left; she was already where the feint ended, because he always feinted left. He boosted; she stepped inside the wind-up, because boosting made him greedy. He tried the combination Kiba had taught him, and she caught the third strike in one small hand, looked up at him with enormous golden eyes, and said, in perfect deadpan:

"...You looked at my shoulder first."

"So?!"

"Don't." She threw him over her hip with a sound like a mattress falling off a truck. "It's rude to announce things."

Laughter rippled through the theatre — but it was different laughter now, and everyone in the room could hear the difference. It wasn't laughing *at* the boy on the floor. It was the laugh a crowd gives a comedian who is also, somehow, breaking their heart.

Because Issei got up. That was the whole montage, in the end. Sona's Knights ran him down and he got up. Koneko read him like a large-print book and threw him into next week and he got up. Rias set training loads that would have broken a varsity team and he woke before his alarm to add to them. The projector showed one night, unglamorous and unmusical: Issei alone in the gym at 2 a.m., throwing the same jab into a beam, over and over, whispering the count to himself because his voice was the only company left awake.

"Two hundred eleven. Two hundred twelve."

The gauntlet on his left arm pulsed with a soft green light.

**[Partner,]** Ddraig's voice rumbled, low, meant for no one. **[Sleep. The beam will still be there tomorrow.]**

"So will Koneko-chan's hip throw," Issei muttered. "Two hundred thirteen."

A pause. Then, gruff, almost embarrassed:

**[...Two hundred fourteen. Your elbow is drifting. Fix it.]**

In the theatre, in the present, the real Ddraig said nothing at all — but the Boosted Gear's gem glowed like a banked hearth, and Thor Odinson, seated in respectful distance of it, inclined his head as though something had been confirmed for him.

"HAH." Fin Fang Foom's contempt rolled down from the upper dark like a rockslide. "The great Welsh Dragon, reduced to counting a hatchling's push-ups. Heavenly Dragon, wyrm of legend, and *this* is your legacy? A boy who cannot defeat a child half his size?"

**[He gets up,]** Ddraig said simply.

"Any fool can stand."

**[No,]** said Ddraig, and there was two thousand years of sealed patience in it. **[They cannot. I have been carried by champions since before your kind learned speech, lizard. The talented ones break the first time the world says no to them. I have felt them break, from the inside. This one—]** the gem flared, **[—the world has said no to every single day of his new life, and he treats it as a scheduling conflict.]**

Fin Fang Foom had no answer. Notably, he did not laugh again for the rest of the chapter.

---

Steve Rogers watched the boy on the screen fail to land a hit for the fortieth consecutive time, and felt something in his chest he hadn't felt since a scrawny kid from Brooklyn kept climbing back up in an alley behind a movie theatre.

"Stark," he said.

"Yeah, Cap."

"You said this was how montages work. Two minutes, sunrise, bench-press a bus." Steve nodded at the screen. "Count the losses. They haven't skipped one. Whoever's showing us this *wants* us to feel every single time he hits the floor."

"Yeah, I noticed. Sadistic editing."

"No." Steve's jaw was set. "Honest editing. That's the whole point. Anybody can respect the bus. I respect the floor." He leaned back. "I've been the guy who couldn't win a fight in this room's weight class. The serum gave me the body. It didn't give me the getting-up. That part I had to bring myself, and I know exactly how much it costs." He was quiet a moment. "That kid's paying full price, every day, with no serum coming."

Cyclops, two rows over, had gone very still behind his visor.

"Summers?" Jean asked softly.

"He can't turn it off," Scott said. "The gauntlet. The dragon. The doubling. It's all bolted to him — like it or not, want it or not." His hand rose, unconsciously, to the edge of the ruby quartz. "And instead of resenting the machinery, he's building the man to carry it. Drills. Repetition. Fundamentals. Nobody makes a seventeen-year-old do fundamentals at two in the morning." He exhaled. "You do fundamentals at two in the morning because you've decided who you're going to be, and your body hasn't caught up to the decision yet."

"That," said Shang-Chi, "is the best definition of training I have ever heard a man in a visor give."

The montage ended on Koneko and Issei, sitting side by side on the gym floor, both wrecked, sharing a bag of chocolate. Koneko chewed, swallowed, and delivered her verdict to the middle distance:

"...You lasted forty seconds today." A beat. "...Gross. Now I have to try."

Issei beamed like she'd handed him a trophy.

The screen went dark, and the theatre — which had come into this chapter expecting comedy — sat in a silence that had weight to it. Somewhere in the cosmic architecture, the threat-assessment ticker that had been quietly climbing for six chapters climbed again, and this time nobody joked about it.

Then the wheel began to spin, and the slot burned gold — deep gold, the gold the room had learned to hold its breath for.

**[ BEATING SAIRAORG THE HARD WAY — SSR ]**

"'Beating Sairaorg,'" Loki read aloud, delighted. "Finally. Our mongrel underdog wins one. I confess I've grown almost fond of— why are you making that face, Captain?"

"Because I read the whole title," Steve said. "'The hard way.'"

---

*Sairaorg Bael*, the caption read, and the screen gave the theatre a young man built like a monument — tall, broad, dark-haired, wrapped in an aura that somehow contained no magic whatsoever, and a smile with absolutely nothing hidden behind it.

*Heir to the Great King's house. Firstborn of the Bael. Born without his clan's Power of Destruction. Born, by devil measure, with nothing.*

The screen showed it in brutal shorthand: a small boy examined by clan elders and found empty. A mother shamed. An heir set aside like a defective tool, exiled to a backwater with the woman who bore him. And then the same small boy, alone in a field at dusk, punching the trunk of a dead tree with hands too young for the damage he was doing to them.

*So he built himself. No Sacred Gear. No spells. No inherited annihilation. Muscle, bone, breath, and ten thousand mornings — until the empty boy could shatter what the gifted could not scratch, and the clan that discarded him had to hand his birthright back with both hands and an apology.*

"Zero magic," Danny Rand said slowly. "A devil — a species *made* of magic — with zero magic. And he's their equivalent of a top seed."

"There were monks in K'un-Lun who spoke of such men," Shang-Chi murmured. "The ones the mountain could not give anything to, so they took it from the mountain instead. My father feared exactly one category of enemy: the kind that cannot be disarmed, because nothing was ever handed to them in the first place." He watched Sairaorg stretch, roll his shoulders, and bow to the arena. "This man is a blade with no hilt. There is no piece of him you can take away."

On screen, the Rating Game arena assembled itself — a formal dueling dimension, floating stone and broadcast circles, referees and rules. And across the field from Sairaorg Bael stood Issei Hyoudou in full crimson Scale Mail, older than the montage had shown him, harder, the armor sitting on him now like it belonged.

"He's grown," Natasha noted.

"So has the mountain," said Shang-Chi.

Sairaorg's first words carried across the arena, and they were warm.

"Hyoudou Issei. I've wanted this since I first heard about you. A Low-class devil, no pedigree, no talent — everything you have, you bled for." He raised his fists. No stance the theatre's martial artists could name; something older, self-made, all his own. "People like us weren't given anything. So show me what you *took*."

---

What followed was not comedy. The projector made sure of that.

Issei fought well — that was the terrible part. The theatre could see every hour of the montage paying out: the footwork Koneko had beaten into him, the patience Sona's Knights had cut into him, boosts banked and spent like a miser, feints layered three deep. He landed the Penetrate strike that had made the whole room gasp a chapter ago, the ability that ignored defense entirely — landed it *clean*, dead center of Sairaorg's chest.

Sairaorg rocked back half a step, coughed blood, and smiled wider.

"Good," he said. "*Good.* Again."

"That should have ended it," Loki said, no longer smiling. "That strike bypasses defense. We watched it bypass defense."

**[It did bypass his defense,]** Ddraig said grimly. **[There is nothing behind that man's defense except more man. Penetrate ignores armor, barriers, scales. It cannot ignore the fact that some bodies are simply built to continue.]**

And then Sairaorg started walking forward, and the education began.

The screen did not flinch and did not cut away. Sairaorg Bael took Issei apart the way weather takes apart a coastline — no cruelty, no wasted motion, just force applied with total honesty. Fists that broke sound. The Scale Mail, the crimson armor the theatre had cheered for in Chapter Three, spiderwebbed. Cracked. Shed pieces. Issei was driven through a pillar, and got up. Through the arena floor, and got up. His boosted counters landed and were *answered*, and every exchange ended with the boy further into the red.

The theatre had gone dead silent. Even the villain tiers.

"Stop the fight," someone said quietly, in the Avengers' section — Sam Wilson, half out of his seat. "There's a referee. Somebody stop the fight."

"He wouldn't thank them," Steve said. His hands were locked on his knees, white-knuckled, and his eyes had not left the screen. "Look at his feet."

On screen, Issei Hyoudou — armor in ruins, one eye swollen shut, aura guttering like a candle in wind — set his feet. Again. The stance was terrible now, ruined by damage. It was also, unmistakably, a stance.

"You could stay down," Sairaorg said. He wasn't taunting. He sounded almost pained. "No one in either world would blame you. The gap between us today is real. Yield, heal, and grow. That's wisdom, Hyoudou."

Issei spat blood, and grinned with the half of his face that still worked.

"Can't," he rasped. "Sorry. There's... a bunch of people who brought me back from being nothing. And a partner in my arm who's been counting my push-ups since two a.m." He raised the shattered gauntlet. "You said it yourself. Guys like us weren't given anything. So I can't *give* anything back. Not one step. You want it—" he lifted his fists, "—take it."

**[Partner,]** the on-screen Ddraig said, and every soul in the theatre heard the ache under the gravel. **[Your body is finished.]**

"Yeah." Issei's grin didn't move. "Body was always the weakest part of me anyway. Boost."

He crossed the arena one more time. It was not fast anymore. It was not clever. Sairaorg met him with something like reverence, and the final exchange lasted four seconds, and at the end of it Issei Hyoudou was face-down on broken stone, and he still tried — the theatre watched him try — to get his arms under himself. Once. Twice. The arms shook, and defied him, and the referee's voice rang out across the dimension:

"*Resignation by inability to continue. Winner — Sairaorg Bael.*"

Sairaorg knelt beside him before the medics arrived. Took the ruined gauntlet-hand in both of his own, carefully, like it was something holy.

"I won today," he said. "Remember that word, Hyoudou. *Today.* I've fought gifted devils my whole life and forgotten most of them by dinner. But a man who spends everything and then reaches for more that isn't there—" his voice roughened, "—I'll be training against the memory of you for years. Come find me when you're stronger. I'll be waiting at the top, keeping it warm."

The screen held on Issei's face — unconscious now, wrecked, and wearing, impossibly, the ghost of a satisfied smile — and faded to black.

---

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

It was Doctor Doom who broke the silence, which surprised everyone, because Doom had spent seven chapters treating the theatre as an inconvenience staffed by inferiors.

"Play it again," Doom said.

The room turned. Victor von Doom stood at the rail of the villain tier, gauntlets folded behind his back, green cloak still, the eternal mask unreadable — and yet something in his voice had changed register.

"Doom has watched this hall celebrate the boy's power," he said. "Power is common. Doom has watched you laugh at his weakness. Weakness is universal. Neither interests me." The mask tilted toward the dark screen. "But mark what you did *not* see. Broken, outmatched, granted every honorable exit — the boy did not beg. Not the referee, not his king, not his enemy, not his gods. Weakness is not innocence; the weak are not owed the world's mercy, and the universe is right to test them. But there is a species of creature that meets the test on its feet and *declines to kneel*, and Doom—" the faintest pause, the sound of an old memory in a Latverian winter, a boy over his mother's grave refusing to weep where the villagers could see, "—Doom was such a creature once, in a village that has since learned better than to laugh."

He turned from the rail.

"The dragon chose adequately," he said, which from Doom was a coronation, and sat down.

**[...Huh,]** said Ddraig, into the stunned quiet. **[I will take that.]**

"As will I," Thor rumbled, rising with his arms crossed, grinning through his beard. "A toast, then, since this cursed theatre stocks no mead — to the boy who loses better than most gods win! Aye, and to the Bael as well. A warrior with empty hands and a full heart. Volstagg would weep."

"He got destroyed," Barton said, but there was no bite in it — he was checking, the way you press a bruise.

"He got *up*," said Steve Rogers, and stood, and the room found that it was, on the whole, not willing to argue with the particular way Captain America said those three words. "Nine times, by my count, after the fight was already lost. You want to know what I saw tonight?" He looked around the theatre — at gods, monsters, kings, and killers. "I saw the montage and the defeat, back to back, and they're the same clip. That's the trick of it. Getting up in a losing fight isn't something you decide in the arena. He decided it at two in the morning, alone, on rep two hundred fourteen. The arena just published it."

Shang-Chi rose as well, pressed fist to palm, and bowed slightly toward the dark screen — toward a boy universes away who would never know.

"In my father's house," he said, "we were taught to fear the gifted. It was bad teaching. Note it in your ledgers, all of you: the gauntlet is not the weapon. The dragon is not the weapon." He straightened. "The *getting up* is the weapon. And it is still growing."

Above them all, the golden wheel clicked softly, resetting, and the threat-assessment ticker — which had once ranked Issei Hyoudou somewhere below "unattended toaster" — revised itself upward for the seventh consecutive chapter, and this time added a small annotation that had never appeared beside any name before:

*Trajectory: unbounded.*
