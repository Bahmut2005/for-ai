# Chapter Twenty-Six: Domestic Dragon Warfare

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Married heroes (Reed Richards, Sue Storm, Vision, Wanda Maximoff) • The summoned fiancées (Rias, Akeno, Xenovia, Irina, Asia, Koneko, Kuroka, Rossweisse) • Comedic villains (Loki, Mephisto, MODOK) • Tony Stark, unmarried and unprepared

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Household Argument Over Laundry" — **Comedy-Class**
2. "Decision on Bedroom Assignments" — **Secret**

---

The theatre had learned to read its own weather, and tonight the weather was — cozy.

That was the alarming part. Storm-grey meant grief; tournament-hush meant a lesson; but ever since the FAMILY-CLASS banner had unlocked at the end of Arc Two with its warm gold light and its little doorbell chime, the room had discovered a fourth kind of dread, one that smelled faintly of fresh laundry and someone else's dinner. Sam Wilson had named it in Chapter Twenty-One, roughly four seconds before the gacha machine misfired and deposited eight furious supernatural women into the mezzanine: *domestic weather.*

The eight were still here.

The theatre had, over five chapters, adjusted. The fiancées of Issei Hyoudou occupied a velvet box of their own on the mezzanine's west rail — the machine had grown it for them overnight, complete with a tea service that refilled itself and a small sign reading RESERVED: HOUSEHOLD, which Rias Gremory had accepted as her due and Rossweisse had appraised for resale value out of pure reflex. They could not leave either. They had stopped demanding to. They had watched, alongside a captive multiverse, as the screen aired their courtships, their family politics, and Ravel Phenex's devastating injury ledger, and they had settled into the wary truce of people who know the projector has their whole photo album and is choosing slides *chronologically.*

Tonight the wheel came down in its warm gold configuration, chimed its doorbell chime, and printed, in the cozy rounded font that fooled absolutely no one:

**TONIGHT: A BATTLE THE RED DRAGON EMPEROR CANNOT WIN.**

The theatre sat up as one. Thor set down his tankard. Doom opened the *second* notebook, the one he had sworn he would not need.

In the household box, Koneko Toujou said, flatly, into her tea: "...It's the laundry thing."

"It is *not* going to be the laundry thing," said Rias Gremory, with the serene confidence of a high-class devil who had personally petitioned the screen, in writing, to never show the laundry thing.

The wheel dropped a single token into the crystal bowl. It landed with a sound like a coin going into a washing machine.

**"HOUSEHOLD ARGUMENT OVER LAUNDRY" — COMEDY-CLASS.**

"It's the laundry thing," said Koneko.

---

The vision opened not on a battlefield but on a corkboard.

Pinned to the corkboard, laminated — *laminated*, with reinforced corners, the work of someone who had been a teacher and would die a teacher — was a wheel-chart labeled **HYOUDOU HOUSEHOLD DUTY ROTATION**, its wedges color-coded by person, its rules appended in small, fierce Norse-tinted handwriting. The camera of the vision panned down the hallway of the Hyoudou residence — the theatre had seen the house before, a modest family home rebuilt by devil contractors into six stories up, three stories down, with a pool, because supernatural politics apparently paid in square footage — and arrived at the laundry room.

Or where the laundry room had been.

Suds. Suds to the ceiling. Suds venturing out into the hallway with the calm confidence of a rising tide. And in the middle of the suds, smoking gently, lay the twisted wreck of an industrial-grade washing machine that appeared to have *died mid-transformation* — drum swollen, panels peeled back like petals, a faint red glow guttering out of its ruined heart.

"Is that machine wearing *armor?*" said Peter Parker, from the middle rows.

"It attempted to," said Vision, in the tone of a coroner. "Observe the plating. That machine achieved, briefly, a Balance Breaker."

The vision helpfully rewound. There stood Issei Hyoudou, eighteen years old, savior of the underworld, slayer of evil dragons — three weeks behind on his assigned laundry rotation and due at training in forty minutes — placing his left hand flat on the washing machine and saying the six stupidest words the theatre had ever heard him say, which was a competitive category:

"Ddraig, we're gonna Boost it."

"**Partner,**" said the dragon-voice, from the gauntlet, with the weariness of ten thousand years, "**I want the record to show—**"

"**BOOST!**"

**[BOOST.]** said the gear, because the gear was loyal.

The machine's spin cycle doubled. Doubled again. For nine glorious seconds, Issei Hyoudou stood before the fastest laundry appliance in any recorded cosmology, arms folded, nodding, a man solving his problems. On the tenth second the machine broke the sound barrier internally, attempted to evolve, and detonated into foam.

The theatre was already laughing. The theatre did not yet understand. The vision panned, with terrible patience, to the laundry itself, flung across the room by the blast: one load of whites, belonging to the household's church faction — Asia's choir dress, Irina's blessed vestments, Xenovia's training gis — every single item dyed a soft, unrepentant *pink* by the crimson Gremory-crest bathrobe that had been in there with them, against three separate laminated rules.

And there, atop the pink, like a war crime on a war crime: one sweater. Formerly adult-sized. Formerly wool. Now approximately large enough for a well-dressed hamster.

In the household box, Rossweisse rose to her feet with a sound like a glacier calving.

"SEVENTY PERCENT OFF," she announced, to the assembled might of the Marvel multiverse. "That sweater was seventy percent off at the Shibuya end-of-season sale. Do you know what wool of that quality *costs* at full price? I waited in line for two hours. I fought a woman for it. I had planned to wear it for *fifteen years.*"

"We know," said the other seven fiancées, in unison, with the cadence of people who had heard the eulogy many, many times.

---

On-screen, the tribunal convened.

The vision gave it to the theatre without mercy: the living room of the Hyoudou household arranged with instinctive courtroom geometry — eight women in a crescent, one dragon emperor on the sofa in the exact posture of a man before a parole board. Rias presiding from the armchair like a throne. Akeno beside her, hand over her mouth, enjoying this in a way she made no effort to conceal. Asia sitting closest to Issei, which he clearly believed meant she was on his side, an error he would understand shortly. Koneko on the floor eating pocky with the eyes of a bailiff. Xenovia standing, arms crossed. Irina holding up, as evidence, one pink vestment. Kuroka draped along the back of the sofa directly behind the accused, tail swaying, having the time of her life. Rossweisse holding the hamster sweater with both hands, like a fallen comrade.

"Let the record show," said Rias, "that this is the third rotation you have skipped, Issei."

"Okay — okay, but — context," said the Red Dragon Emperor, and the theatre watched a man who had stared down god-class beings without blinking begin to *sweat.* "Week one there was the emergency in the Underworld. Week two there was training with Sairaorg. This week—"

"This week you played the new fighting game with Matsuda until three in the morning," said Koneko, without looking up from her pocky.

"That was *reconnaissance*—"

"You streamed it," said Koneko.

There was a pause on-screen, the specific pause of a man watching his own defense counsel set fire to the courtroom. And then Issei Hyoudou, hero of two wars, did the thing that made the entire theatre — heroes, villains, gods, and one Watcher — lean forward with the shared, delighted horror of people watching a man reach for a live wire.

He drew himself up. He set his jaw. The green gem on his left hand glinted as he raised it, and his voice dropped into the register the theatre knew from mountaintops and miracles.

"I am the *Red Dragon Emperor,*" he said. "I have fought gods. I have died, twice. This week alone I erased a hill. And I don't think the guy who's supposed to stand against, like, the end of the world, should have to—"

"Finish that sentence," said Rias Gremory, pleasantly, "and see what happens."

The temperature of the vision dropped eleven degrees. Every married being in the theatre made an involuntary sound, low and universal, the sound of recognizing a cliff edge from having personally gone over it.

"—should have to *carry this burden alone*, which is why I'm so grateful for my supportive household," finished Issei, at roughly triple speed.

"Nice save," said Sam Wilson, in the theatre. "Terrible, but nice."

"It won't hold," said Sue Storm, with the calm of a forensic expert. "Watch. He's going to try the other thing. There's always another thing."

There was another thing. On-screen, Issei — visibly cornered, visibly rummaging through his own head for anything, *anything* — straightened again, and this time his voice took on the unmistakable cadence of a man inventing scripture in real time.

"Also — and I didn't want to have to bring this up, because it's a *sacred dragon matter* — laundry division in a dragon's nest follows *ancient nest law.* The nest-lord provides protection and… fire… and the, uh, the nest-mates handle the… textile domain. Ddraig can confirm. It's been custom since — for ages. Ancient ages. Ddraig, back me up."

The theatre held its breath. In the gauntlet, the green gem glowed. And the voice of the Welsh Dragon Emperor, elder terror of an alien Heaven, partner and — by now, the whole theatre knew — something close to a father, spoke with the slow, savoring deliberation of a being who had waited ten thousand years to be a character witness.

"**Partner. I have lived since the age of myth. I have known every dragon of every line. I have slept in the great nests of the old world.**" A pause, expertly weighted. "**There is no textile domain.**"

The theatre *detonated.*

---

It took some time for order to return. The Thing had to be thumped on the back by the Human Torch. Volstagg had fallen into the row in front of him and was being helped up by people he was crushing. In the villains' dark, MODOK was emitting a rhythmic mechanical wheeze that his chair's diagnostics kept flagging as a malfunction and was, in fact, laughter.

And from his high scornful perch, Fin Fang Foom — who had spent twenty-five chapters radiating contempt for a "true dragon" leashed to a human boy — rose to his full height, wings mantled, and delivered his verdict with the outrage of a scholar hearing his field defiled.

"There is NO SUCH CUSTOM," the great dragon thundered. "I am a dragon of Kakaranthara! I have kept nests older than your civilizations! Nest law concerns hoard-boundary, egg-turning, and the order of precedence at a kill! It says NOTHING of laundry! The whelp is inventing *dragon scripture* to escape chores, and the insult of it is that he is inventing it BADLY—"

"Aye, but note that he *tried*," boomed Thor, wiping his eyes, one arm around a wheezing Volstagg. "That is the most dragon thing the boy has ever done! Lying about ancient law to protect the hoard of one's own laziness — Foom, be honest, your uncles did exactly this!"

"...My uncles," said Fin Fang Foom, with dignity, "are not the subject of this theatre," and sat down in a way that confirmed everything.

In the front rows, where the married heroes sat, a different and much quieter reckoning was underway.

Reed Richards had gone very still in the specific manner of a man watching his own biography performed by strangers. On the screen, the frozen frame still showed Issei mid-scripture, hand raised, gem glinting. Reed cleared his throat.

"The appliance modification," he said, carefully. "I just want to note, for the group, that attempting to *improve* a household device instead of simply performing the task as assigned is — that is — there are circumstances in which—"

"Reed," said Sue Storm, not looking at him, "tell them about the dishwasher."

"The dishwasher was a *prototype*—"

"Tell them what it did to the kitchen, Reed."

"...It achieved a low orbit," said the smartest man alive, and folded in on himself, literally, about eight percent.

Sue patted his arm, merciless and fond, and looked up at the screen — at the crescent of women, at the sweating dragon — with the warm, terrible smile of recognition. "Four for four," she said. "The skipped chore. The 'my work is too important' defense. The escalation to grand cosmic duty. And when that fails, the invented technicality. I've been married to this argument for years. It's a good argument. It has never once won." She raised her voice slightly, toward the household box across the theatre. "The pink vestments — did he at least apologize before or after the excuses?"

"After," eight voices replied, resigned, in unison.

"They always apologize after," said Sue and Wanda Maximoff, also in unison, from thirty seats apart, and then looked at each other across the rows with the startled kinship of strangers who have just discovered they attend the same church.

Vision had been running quietly beside Wanda, eyes at instrument-glow, and now he spoke with the gentle bewilderment that was his version of a laugh. "I have completed a fairness audit of the duty rotation," he said. "The laminated chart is equitable to within one-half of one percent, adjusted for combat deployments, school schedules, and Underworld obligations. It is, frankly, a masterpiece of household jurisprudence — Rossweisse, if you can hear me, I would like a copy. Which makes the subject's evasion mathematically indefensible and—" he paused, tilting his head— "and entirely familiar. Wanda. When we shared a residence. The matter of whose turn it was to prepare dinner."

"You mean the fights," said Wanda.

"I mean the *discussions*—"

"Vizh. I can make dinner appear out of the air. Fully cooked. You don't even *eat.* And we still fought about whose *turn* it was." Wanda was smiling — the small, complicated smile she wore in domestic weather, the one with a whole lost sitcom behind it — and her eyes stayed on the screen, on the crescent of women and the cornered dragon, with something that wasn't laughter underneath. "Because it was never about the dinner. It's about whether you show up for the boring parts. Anyone will show up for the apocalypse. The apocalypse is *interesting.*" She nodded at Issei's frozen, sweating face. "That right there is the only test he's ever failed. Look at him. He knows it."

And Tony Stark — Tony Stark stood in the aisle, arms hanging, wearing the face of a man whose entire model of the universe had received an unpatchable update.

"I need the room to acknowledge what we're seeing," he said. "That kid — that specific kid — punched a hole in a mountain in Chapter Nine. Carol has him classified as a regional calamity. Doom keeps him in the *constants* notebook. And he is currently losing — losing *badly*, unconditional-surrender losing — to a chore wheel and eight people who love him." He turned a full circle, appealing to the theatre at large. "None of them are even using powers! The scariest thing in that room is a *laminated chart!* Is this what marriage is? Is this what you people have been *doing?*"

"Yes," said every married being in the theatre, with one voice, and Tony sat down slowly, like a man easing into cold water, and did not speak again for some time.

In the villains' dark, Mephisto chuckled, low and appreciative. "Eight claimants, one estate, and a sovereign with no veto," he murmured. "I've drafted contracts for less interesting kingdoms." And beside him — two seats along, hunched forward with an actual notebook, an actual quill, writing at ferocious speed — sat Loki of Asgard.

"Say nothing," Loki said, without looking up. "I am *working.*"

"Brother," said Thor, delighted, from across the aisle. "What could the god of stories possibly be recording from a laundry dispute?"

"Everything, you enormous absence of subtlety, *everything.*" Loki's quill did not stop. "For a thousand years I have studied thrones. Coups. Successions. Poisons. And in one evening this theatre has shown me the only political system I have ever seen that is genuinely coup-proof. Consider!" He gestured at the screen with the quill. "A power incarnate — mountain-eraser, god-slayer — held in *perfect check* by a council of eight, wielding no weapon but the fact that he *cannot bear to disappoint them.* No army. No law. No leverage but love and a chore wheel, and it binds him tighter than the All-Father's chains ever bound me. Do you understand? All my life I sought the force that constrains the mighty, and it is *this.* It is Tuesday. It is whose turn it is. I am taking *notes,*" he finished, with a scholar's shining fervor, "on Nest Law."

"There IS no Nest Law," said Fin Fang Foom, from above, wounded.

"There is now," said Loki. "He invented it. Badly. I intend to draft it *properly.*"

---

The wheel chimed again before the theatre had recovered — a softer chime this time, and the gold light dimmed toward something quieter. The second token dropped into the bowl without any clatter at all.

**"DECISION ON BEDROOM ASSIGNMENTS" — SECRET.**

In the household box, all eight women went abruptly still.

"No," said Rias Gremory, standing. "That one is *private.* That one is — machine. Machine, I am telling you—"

The machine, which had aired her brother's baby photos of her in Chapter Twenty-Three, did not respond to threats.

The vision opened on the same living room, weeks earlier, and on a whiteboard. The theatre had learned to fear this household's stationery. The whiteboard bore a calendar grid, color-coded in eight colors, titled in Rias's elegant hand: **ROOM ROTATION — REVISED (FINAL) (REVISED) (FINAL v3).**

What followed was, the theatre would agree afterward, the most sophisticated negotiation ever aired in that room — and it had seen Doom watch peace conferences. The rotation of who shared the main bedroom, argued clause by clause with the solemnity of an arms treaty. The bath-order dispute, entered into evidence as a rider (Xenovia held that bath order should be decided by sparring; Rossweisse held that hot water was a finite resource with a *cost per liter*, and produced figures; Kuroka simply took her baths at 3 a.m. and had thus achieved what analysts would call an extra-treaty position). Akeno proposing amendments engineered purely to make Rias turn the color of her own hair. Asia — gentle, kind Asia — revealing herself as the bloc's kingmaker, because no one, *no one*, would vote against Asia. Koneko presenting a sleep-quality dataset. Irina invoking childhood-friend seniority, a claim struck down by a 7–1 vote for the fourth consecutive quarter.

And Issei Hyoudou, Red Dragon Emperor, sat at his own kitchen table throughout with the voting power of a potted plant.

He tried — of course he tried. The theatre watched him rise, watched the gem glint, watched him invoke, with a straight face, "the ancient nest-custom of the Warm Center, wherein the dragon sleeps at the heart of the nest and all arrangement proceeds *outward from the dragon*—"

"**Partner,**" said Ddraig, instantly, "**there is no Clause of the Warm Center.**"

"You are the *worst* wingman in mythology—"

"**I am an honest dragon.**"

The motion failed 8–0. The chair noted, for the record, that the accused had also drawn laundry duty for a full month, non-transferable, and the *last* bath slot for a week, and the vision began to dim on the sound of the theatre's laughter—

—and then it did not dim.

It lingered, instead, on the whiteboard, at night, after the lights were out. It drifted close, the way the machine did when it was about to spend its Secret. And it turned the whiteboard around.

On the back, in handwriting the theatre knew — the graceless, earnest scrawl they'd seen on twenty-five chapters of exam papers and training plans — was one line. It was not color-coded. It was not versioned. It was, the caption noted, the only rule in the entire Hyoudou household charter authored by Issei himself, ratified 8–0, never once revised:

**RULE ZERO: IF ANYONE CAN'T SLEEP — BAD DREAM, BAD DAY, BAD ANYTHING — THEY OUTRANK EVERYBODY. NO QUESTIONS. WAKE ME FIRST. — I.**

The vision gave them one memory to go with it. Brief. A hallway at 2 a.m. A door opening before the knock finished. No jokes, no scripture, no Red Dragon Emperor — just a boy in a rumpled shirt stepping aside instantly, saying, "Hey. Hey, you're okay. Come on, you get the middle," to whichever of them stood there that night; the vision, kindly, did not say who.

The caption printed itself small and cold and factual, the way the machine printed the things that mattered:

**THE SUBJECT DIED AT SUNSET, ALONE, IN A PARK. HE HAS NEVER SPOKEN OF THIS RULE'S ORIGIN. NONE OF THEM HAVE EVER NEEDED TO ASK.**

The laughter was gone from the theatre. Not fallen — *set down*, gently, the way you set down something you intend to pick back up.

In the household box, Rias Gremory was no longer demanding anything of the machine. She sat with her hands folded in her lap, and after a moment she said, quietly, to a few thousand heroes and monsters and to the empty air where a boy in another cosmology couldn't hear her: "He wrote it the first month. Before the rotation. Before any of it." A breath. "He thinks we don't know why."

"We know why," said Koneko, to her tea.

"We always knew why," said Asia, and no one in the theatre said anything at all for a while.

It was Sue Storm who finally spoke, softly, to Reed but loudly enough, and the theatre let her have the last word because she had earned it four chapters ago. "That's the whole thing, right there. That's what the machine wanted us to see tonight." She nodded at the screen — at the chore wheel, the pink laundry, the 8–0 defeats, the one rule on the back of the board. "The arguments are real. The losing is real — and he *should* lose; he was wrong about all of it. But you don't measure a marriage by the arguments. You measure it by the size of the thing the arguments are safely inside of." She leaned into Reed's shoulder; Reed, with great care, extended his arm around her exactly one seat's width. "They can afford to fight about laundry. Do you know how much love it costs, to afford that?"

Vision looked at Wanda. Wanda looked at the screen.

"Rule Zero," she said, mostly to herself, and wrote it somewhere no one could see.

---

The lights came up on domestic weather. Tony Stark was explaining to Rhodey, with charts, that he was *fine.* Loki's notebook had a title page now — *ON NEST LAW* — and a dedication he refused to show Thor. Fin Fang Foom was pretending, with total commitment, not to have wiped his eye with a claw the size of a bus.

The gacha wheel rose toward the ceiling, gold and warm and smug. As it went, the FAMILY-CLASS band ticked forward one notch, and across the screen, for just a moment, moved a new teaser: small silhouettes. *Short* silhouettes. One with fox ears. One impossibly, ominously familiar to no one yet. A line of them, holding hands, and beneath them in the cozy font:

**NEXT: THE CHILDREN HE CHOSE.**

"Children?" said Kamala Khan, sitting bolt upright. "He has *children?* Since when does he have—"

"Adopted," said Koneko, from the household box, with the faintest ghost of a smile. "Mostly."

"*Mostly?*" said the entire theatre.

The lights went down on the sound of Loki turning to a fresh page.
