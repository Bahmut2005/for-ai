## SUBSECTION ONE: THE MISSION BRIEF THAT GOES WRONG

The briefing is routine. A hostage extraction in Eastern Europe, a trafficking ring dismantled, twelve civvies held in a compound that needs surgical precision. Issei listens, processes, nods at the right times. He's been trained for this since he could walk.

What he's not trained for is the choice.

"How many combatants?" Wanda asks, because she likes to know what firepower they're walking into.

"Estimated twenty," Issei says, reading the intel feed. "Heavy armaments. Structure's a converted Soviet facility, which means multiple levels, multiple exits, potential for collapse if we're not careful."

Steve nods. "Standard protocol. Get in, secure the hostages, suppress the opposition, get out."

This is where standard ends.

"We're going in at 0600 hours," Issei says. "Recon at 0200. The compound has three contingency explosives on the eastern wall, triggered if they sense breach. Disabling them is priority alpha."

Natasha's sharp intake of breath is audible. "Eastern wall is structural support."

"Yeah," Issei confirms. "Blow that section, the whole building comes down in seventeen seconds. Estimated casualty rate: ninety-three percent of the hostages if we haven't extracted them yet."

The room goes quiet.

"So we disable the explosives first," Tony says, like it's obvious.

"Someone has to," Issei says. "But the guard rotation changes at 0230. Three-minute gap where the eastern perimeter is unmonitored. After that, it's triple-covered for eight hours. Next gap isn't until 1800 hours."

"So we go at 0230," Steve says. "We use the three-minute window to disable the charges, then—"

"Then we're extracting civilians during high-alert status," Issei interrupts. His voice is flat now. Gone distant. "The three-minute window creates a cascade. We trigger it, alarms start blaring. Twenty minutes to full lockdown of the complex. We're cutting it to the last second."

"Issei," Wanda says, and her voice is careful, "you can run the calculations. You always run the calculations. What are you not saying?"

He looks up.

"My wife is one of the hostages," he says.

The room fractures.

"What?" Steve stands.

"Intel came in eight hours ago. She was part of the diplomatic envoy that got taken. We didn't announce it because protocol says separation during operations reduces risk of compromised judgment."

Natasha's hands tighten. "That's the Red Handbook. That's the spy playbook. The protocol exists for a reason."

"I know," Issei says. "And I've been running calculations. Every scenario. Every path. And there's one optimal solution, and it's not in the playbook."

He pulls up the hologram. Shows the compound layout, the hostage positions. Points to one section.

"She's on the second level, northern corridor. Close to the main stairwell. If I have forty-five seconds of distraction, I can extract her first. Before the chaos starts. Before the odds go sideways."

"That's extraction priority deviation," Tony says slowly, catching up. "You're talking about pulling her out of sequence."

"I'm talking about saving a life that matters to me," Issei says. "And I'm aware that makes me unreliable. I'm aware that it compromises the operation. I'm aware that it's exactly what the Red Handbook says I shouldn't do."

Steve sits down. Stays sitting for a long moment.

"How many of the other hostages do we lose?" he asks quietly.

"Run the numbers," Issei says. "See what happens when I stop being a weapon and start being a man with a wife."

## SUBSECTION TWO: THE CALCULATION NOBODY WANTS

The mission happens at 0230 hours, precisely as planned.

It doesn't go as planned.

The Avengers are practiced enough at improvisation that "close" usually means "acceptable margin." Close means two, maybe three people die instead of twelve. Close is the calculus of war.

This isn't close.

Issei gets into position, neutralizes the guard patrol, disables the explosives with ninety seconds to spare. Technical perfection. Then he detours. Just for forty-five seconds, he tells himself. Just long enough to reach her.

The perimeter alarm triggers at second thirty.

He has her hand, has her moving toward the stairwell when the compound goes active. Every light floods. Every speaker screams warning.

They make it down the stairs. Three flights. Her hand is in his and she's pregnant—he didn't know, didn't see that in the intel, and it changes every calculation he's running—and she's moving as fast as she can while he's trying not to drag her, and—

The secondary team hits the main corridor at the same moment.

They extract the hostages, all twelve of them. Issei and his wife make thirteen. They breach the extraction zone at minute 4:47, within acceptable parameters.

But here's what happens while he's playing husband:

Two of the hostages in the eastern corridor, where one of the backup teams is supposed to be moving to secondary holding, don't get reached. There's a structural collapse. Minor. Calculated failure in one of the support beams, probably compromised by the explosives team's work, probably a margin of error nobody anticipated.

Two people die under concrete.

Issei learns about it when they're back at base, after the debrief, after medical clearance. Tony tells him, voice neutral, because this is what happens when you break protocol. This is the cost of being human.

"They were in my assigned sector," Issei says. "I should have—"

"You weren't there," Steve says. Not unkindly. But with certainty. "You were extracting your wife. That's what you chose."

"I chose wrong."

"No," Natasha says. "You chose honestly. That's different."

## SUBSECTION THREE: THE JUDGMENT

The Avengers come to their own conclusions quietly. Steve sits with it, processes it, and tells Issei that he understands. That impossible choices are the weight of command, and sometimes there's no good answer, only the answer you can live with.

Clint nods, doesn't push, moves on.

But the antiheroes? The ones who live by different codes?

Frank Castle corners him in the training room.

"You made a choice," Frank says. Not a question.

"I made the only choice that let me live with myself."

"Yeah." Frank's voice is ice. "You did. And two people died because you chose love over protocol."

Issei doesn't argue. "They did."

"So you're a liability." Frank's hands flex, not into fists, just a motion like he's considering violence. "You're a beautiful, talented liability with superpowers. And the next time you're in the field with my people, I'm going to be thinking about whether you're going to pull your weapon when it matters most or whether you're going to go running after something shiny."

"I would die for your people," Issei says.

"That's not the issue," Frank replies. "The issue is whether you'd let them die for something you love more. And the answer is yes. And I respect that, kid. I respect it and I don't want you near my operations."

The Punisher leaves. Doesn't slam the door, which is somehow worse.

Daredevil is harder, because he understands both sides.

Matt Murdock sits across from Issei in the safe house, senses working overtime, listening to the rhythm of his heartbeat, the pressure of his blood, the things Issei can't hide from a man who's been blind his whole life.

"You made the moral choice," Matt says. "The human choice. The choice that acknowledges that the woman you love matters more than protocol."

"Two people died."

"Yes," Matt says. "And if you could go back and change it, you would choose differently, because you're a good person. But the question isn't whether you would change it. The question is whether you could."

He leans forward.

"If you went back to the moment where you had to choose, and you knew exactly what would happen—that two people would die, that the mission would still succeed, that everyone you love would be safe but two strangers would die—could you choose protocol over them?"

Issei can't speak.

"That's the moral calculus," Matt continues. "Not whether you made the right choice. But whether you *could* make the choice that would let them die. And the answer is no. It's no, and that's not a flaw in you, Issei. That's the thing that makes you human. It's also the thing that makes you dangerous to the mission."

"So what do I do?"

"You acknowledge the contradiction," Matt says. "You exist in the space where you are both morally right and operationally wrong. And you don't try to resolve it. You just live in it. Because the moment you stop seeing the contradiction, you've become something else entirely."
