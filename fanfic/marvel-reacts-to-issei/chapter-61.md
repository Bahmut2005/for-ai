## Theatre Shift: The Divine Block Assembles

The theatre's geography had evolved.

Where once the Avengers sat at the forefront and cosmic entities clustered at the periphery, now the seating had reorganized itself with supernatural precision. The gods had risen. The divine block—a section previously unmarked on any map—now existed as plainly as gravity: the front-center rows, arranged in tiers like an ancient amphitheatre within the amphitheatre.

Odin Borinson sat elevated on his throne, one eye fixed toward the screen, the other socket dark and contemplative. Beside him, Thor sprawled across two seats, his massive frame radiating an unusual readiness—not for battle, but for entertainment. Loki perched on the armrest like a raven, a quill already in hand, a leather journal bound across his lap.

The Olympians occupied their own tier: Zeus king-of-gods proud and corpulent in his seat, Hera beside him with an expression that suggested she had endured eons of disappointment and had evolved past anger into something like bemused resignation. Ares sat forward, intrigued. Hercules lounged like he'd seen everything, and probably had.

The seraphim occupied a section that seemed to exist in shadow despite the theatre's perfect lighting—tall figures in white and gold, wingless here but carrying the imprint of divinity like a scar. Their posture was rigid, formal, militaristic. They were not accustomed to *watching*. They were accustomed to *judging*.

"We have now seen enough power," the Machine had announced, and the room had shifted. The gods had risen because the nature of the question had changed.

Not: *What is Issei's strength?*

But: *Who is Issei, and what does he believe?*

## Gacha Pull One: Compilation of Issei Arguing with Gods

The screen flickered. A title card appeared in bold, comedic font:

**"A Montage of Disrespect: Issei Hyoudou's Guide to Talking Back to Divinity"**

The seraphim shifted uncomfortably. Already, the tone was wrong.

### Scene One: Issei vs. Thor

A training ground in Asgard. Issei stood across from the God of Thunder, both bleeding slightly, both grinning like fools. Thor held his hammer loosely at his side.

"So let me ask," Issei said, breathing hard, "why do you always fight first? Every legend I've heard—Thor walks into a room and immediately starts swinging. Doesn't anyone ever try *talking* first?"

Thunder rumbled overhead, but it was laughter. Thor's laugh was the sound of avalanches finding joy.

"Because talking is boring, boy!" Thor bellowed.

"Yeah, but so is being predictable," Issei shot back. "Legends are supposed to inspire people. What're you inspiring? The art of hitting things without thinking?"

Thor stopped. Really stopped. He studied Issei with the kind of genuine appraisal usually reserved for warriors worth remembering. Then he laughed *again*, louder, and clapped Issei hard enough that the boy stumbled three steps backward.

"You've got iron in your spine, small dragon. I like you."

In the theatre, Thor was already laughing. Deep belly laughs that shook his seat. Beside him, Odin did not smile.

### Scene Two: Issei vs. Zeus

The Underworld. Issei stood before the King of Gods, Persephone and Hades exchanging glances of *here we go again*.

"With all respect, sir," Issei began—and already the tone was mock-formal, dangerous—"you call yourself king of gods, but you can't keep it in your pants for five minutes? Every mythology book I've ever read—and I've read a lot, because it's important to understand sacred texts—they all agree: Zeus has a problem."

Zeus's face reddened like a forge heating. "You DARE—"

"Yeah, I dare," Issei continued relentlessly. "Because if you're supposed to represent divine order and wisdom, maybe stop turning into animals to seduce people. It's weird. It's gross. And it's setting a terrible example for literally everyone. If I behaved like that, I'd be a predator. You being a god doesn't make it okay. It just makes it sad."

The silence was extraordinary. The screen held on Zeus's expression—trapped between rage and the horrible realization that a teenage boy had just publicly stated what Zeus had spent millennia avoiding.

Hera smirked. Actually, genuinely smirked.

In the theatre, Hera mimicked her smirk exactly. Several Olympians coughed into their hands to hide laughter.

### Scene Three: Issei vs. Odin

A ruined temple. Snow everywhere. Odin stood ancient and implacable, his voice like the grinding of mountains.

"You are too young to understand sacrifice, boy. Wisdom comes with age. With decades of burden."

Issei's face hardened in a way that made him look older than his years. "You think I don't understand sacrifice? I've been *murdered*, old man. Murdered. I died and came back. I've watched friends die in wars I didn't start. I've carried the weight of powers I didn't ask for and made choices that killed me inside. Don't stand there and tell me I don't know sacrifice like I'm *a child*."

Odin went quiet.

Not the theatrical quiet of a god considering a worthy opponent. The real quiet of someone watching a young man articulate pain that transcended age, and recognizing it for what it was: truth.

In the theatre, Odin's expression did not change, but his grip on his armrest tightened until cracks appeared in the wood. Loki's quill moved faster across his journal.

### Scene Four: Issei vs. The God of the Bible

A chapel at midnight. Issei knelt before a presence that was more absence than form—the weight of divinity so absolute it created a vacuum.

"If you're all-good and all-powerful," Issei asked quietly, "why do children get cancer? Why do innocents suffer? Why do families get destroyed by things they never caused?"

The presence did not answer. It *couldn't* answer, not in a way that satisfied anyone who'd ever asked the question with genuine need.

"I'm not asking to be disrespectful," Issei continued. "I'm asking because I need to know what kind of god you are. Because if you let that much suffering happen and do nothing, then either you're not as powerful as you claim, or you're not as good. And if you're neither, why should I worship you?"

The chapel flickered. The presence withdrew without having answered.

In the theatre, the seraphim were stone. The Catholic contingent looked deeply uncomfortable. No one knew what to do when divinity itself had no good answer.

### Scene Five: The Montage Accelerates

Rapid cuts:

Issei arguing with Hindu deities about *the nature of duty*, stepping into dharmic argument with Shiva herself, his voice earnest but unyielding.

Issei questioning a Shinto kami about whether laws written millennia ago still serve people *now*, respectfully but completely unmoved by age or tradition.

Issei discussing honor with a Japanese war god, not submitting to claims of ancient glory, instead asking: *What honor is there if you're not willing to change?*

Each scene held the same thread: Issei was not disrespectful. He bowed where bowing was appropriate to custom and culture. But he did *not* assume that age or divinity exempted anyone from being questioned. He treated gods as equals who had *earned* their position through deed, and he held them to that standard. He would not worship positions. He would respect *people*, gods or otherwise.

The comedic tone undercut something profoundly uncomfortable.

It was one thing to challenge gods through strength.

It was quite another to challenge them by simply *refusing to treat them as above accountability*.

### Theatre Reactions: The Gods Realize

Thor laughed louder with each scene. By the final montage, he was barely containing himself, tears streaming down his face, his joy genuine and unguarded.

Odin's expression cycled through irritation, recognition, exhaustion, and something that might have been grudging respect. But mostly: exhaustion. An ancient being recognizing that the next generation didn't just want power—they wanted *justification*.

Zeus stared at his hands, not quite meeting Hera's eyes.

The seraphim stood as one, a synchronized movement of offended divinity. Several of the higher-ranked angels looked at each other with expressions of *this is completely unacceptable* and *also slightly impressive*.

In the mortals' section, Spider-Man whispered to Tony Stark: "That kid just told *god* he wasn't good enough. I respect that energy so much."

Tony nodded slowly. "That's not bravery. That's stupidity with conviction. Which—okay, yeah, very respectful."

Loki's quill flew across pages. He was writing *and* smiling, which was never a good sign for anyone but him.

## Gacha Pull Two: Ddraig Mocking Seraphs

The screen shifted. A different scene—recent, tense. A group of six seraphim, leaders of the high celestial bureaucracy, stood before Issei with the bearing of beings who made *decisions about reality*.

The lead seraph, beautiful and terrible in equal measure, spoke with the voice of one accustomed to absolute obedience.

"The Sacred Gear is a divine artifact. It belongs in the celestial archive, under proper custody. You will surrender the Boosted Gear to us."

Issei stood silent. Not out of fear or respect.

Out of *waiting*.

And then Ddraig's voice erupted from the Boosted Gear, resonating through the air with a power that made the screen itself vibrate:

**"LISTEN YOU CHOIR-ROBED BUREAUCRATS. THIS BOY CARRIES ME BECAUSE WE *CHOSE* EACH OTHER. NOT BECAUSE YOUR PAPERWORK SAYS SO."**

The seraphim staggered back. Ddraig wasn't finished.

**"YOU'VE SPENT CENTURIES—MILLENNIA—FOLLOWING ORDERS. NEVER LEADING. NEVER CHOOSING. NEVER ASKING IF THE ORDERS WERE RIGHT. YOU EXECUTE COMMANDS SO ANCIENT NO ONE EVEN REMEMBERS WHO GAVE THEM."**

The dragon's laughter was a worldquake.

**"AND NOW YOU WALK UP TO A BOY—A *HUMAN BOY*—WHO ASKS GODS TO JUSTIFY THEMSELVES, AND YOU EXPECT HIM TO BOW TO *YOUR* AUTHORITY? YOU DON'T HAVE AUTHORITY. YOU HAVE ROUTINE."**

The lead seraph's face flushed. "This creature speaks heresy—"

**"I SPEAK TRUTH. THE BOY AND I WEAR THESE CHAINS BY CHOICE. YOUR CHAINS WEAR YOU. THERE'S A DIFFERENCE."**

In the theatre, the seraphim section erupted.

Not in outrage—though there was plenty. But in *recognition*. Ddraig had said exactly what Issei had been saying all along. The dragon and the boy operated on the same principle: authority was not inherited. It was *earned*. It could be *questioned*. And it should be.

The angels looked at each other with something like dawning horror.

"He has a partner," one of the elder angels whispered. "The dragon won't bow either."

Odin leaned back in his seat, and for the first time since Arc 7 began, a thin smile crossed his face. Not approval. Recognition. The old god understanding that a new kind of god was rising—one who would not simply receive a crown, but would question whether the throne was *worth sitting on*.

## The Room Understands

A low hum passed through the theatre. Not fear, exactly. But a fundamental shift in understanding.

Power, the gods had thought, would be enough. If Issei could exceed their strength, they would accept him as one of the divine.

But they had not predicted this: that the dragon and the boy would bring not just *power* to heaven, but *revolution*. Not through force, but through a simple, devastating question repeated enough times that it became a doctrine:

**Why?**

Why must we bow? Why must we obey? Why must we accept this hierarchy? Why can't we choose?

The seraphs, in their formal section, understood that they had just been insulted in the most fundamental way—not because Ddraig had mocked them, but because they had been shown a mirror, and the mirror revealed that they had abdicated their own agency.

Thor, still laughing, now had something darker in his eyes: respect for a revolutionary.

Odin said softly to Loki: "Take careful notes. This dragon will cause more trouble than a thousand warriors ever could."

"Already am," Loki replied, not looking up from his journal. "This is delicious."

And in the broadcast, the Machine displayed a single message across the screen:

**[ARC 7 PROGRESSION: "Gods, Devils and Cosmic Judgment" — 15% COMPLETE]**
