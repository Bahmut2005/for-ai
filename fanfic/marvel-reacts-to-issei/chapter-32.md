# Chapter Thirty-Two: The Kingdom That Asked for Him

Between arcs, the theatre redecorated. No one ever saw it happen. The warm-gold banner of the family nights had gone dark like windows at bedtime, and in its place the steel-grey track of ARC FOUR ran the ceiling's length, martial and cold — and on the second night of it, the audience discovered the machine had built a new row.

Front and center-left, raised half a step above the tiers around it: five thrones. Not seats. Thrones — each one unmistakably itself. A chair of dark vibranium-woven wood with panther-claw feet. A coral seat that stayed wet no matter how dry the air. A block of green-draped iron. A fourth of pale Attilan crystal, which Black Bolt regarded from a polite distance and declined, on the reasonable grounds that the machine did not get to tell him where kings sat.

And a fifth chair. Plain steel. Unmarked. Facing the screen at the row's end like an accusation.

"Subtle," said Magneto, from the mutant tiers, not moving toward it.

"It matched the rest of us within a molecule," Shuri observed, running a scanner over her brother's throne with open professional resentment. "The weave is *correct*. I hate that the weave is correct."

T'Challa sat. Namor sat, arranging himself with the boredom of a man who had held actual thrones before most of this room's nations had flags. Doom sat last, and made even that a verdict.

Last night still hung in the air the way thunder hangs after the flash. The machine had ended the arc's opening chapter with two words burning over the image of a boy standing on a dead god-serpent the size of a river: **EMPEROR OF HOPE.** No explanation. Steve Rogers had disliked the noun. Doom had approved of it. And Magneto — Magneto had risen in the dark and asked the question that had followed everyone to whatever passed for sleep here:

*Who granted him legitimacy?*

Tonight the gacha wheel descended with, the room could have sworn, a certain smugness.

**"VILLAGE ELECTION OF ISSEI AS PROTECTOR" — RARITY: RARE.**

"*Election*," Namor repeated, as if the word had been fished out of a bilge.

The theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

The Underworld had frontiers. The vision established this first, patiently, because the audience still thought of Hell as a single administrative horror: no — it had provinces, and beyond the provinces it had valleys where the war had gone and the government had not. This one lay under a sky the color of an old bruise: terraced fields, a mill, a shrine with its roof stove in, and claw-gouges in the earth wide enough to lie down in.

The war was over. Its leavings were not. The vision gave the theatre ten seconds of what a remnant evil dragon does to a settlement of low-class devils and reincarnated farmers who have no peerage, no pieces, no one — ten seconds was enough — and then it gave them the red blur that arrived on the eleventh, and the audience, thirty-one chapters educated, did not need the fight narrated. They watched Issei Hyoudou put himself between a burning granary and something with too many jaws, and they watched the something learn what the whole theatre had learned in Arc One: that the boy did not know how to be anywhere else.

What the machine wanted them to see came *after*.

It showed him staying. Two weeks, three. Scale Mail dismissed, sleeves rolled, hauling roof-beams. Asia in the shrine with a line of patients out the door. Koneko relocating boulders with the expression of someone doing light gardening. Issei teaching the village children — there were always children; the theatre had stopped being surprised — how to spot a stray-beast track, getting the lesson wrong, being corrected by a nine-year-old goatherd, and laughing so hard he sat down in the mud.

And then, on an evening when the roofs were whole, the vision showed the millhouse: lamplit, packed to the walls, every adult in the valley crowded in, and on the table a bread bowl filling up with folded scraps of paper.

"They're—" Okoye leaned forward, general's eyes narrowed. "They are *voting*."

They were voting. The village headwoman — old, iron-grey, a reincarnated human with flour on her forearms — counted the ballots aloud while Issei stood by the door with the deeply hunted look of a student summoned over the intercom. The count was unanimous, minus one ballot cast for a goat named Understudy, which the vision made clear was the miller's standing protest vote in all elections and was not personal.

"We are asking," the headwoman said, "not offering. Offering is for tribute, and we've nothing you'd take. The valley has voted. We want you as our lord protector."

The theatre watched the Red Dragon Emperor — mountain-eraser, dragon-slayer, Emperor of Hope — go into full retreat against a millhouse door.

"*Lord?* No. Nope. Absolutely not. Do you know what my grades — look, I once flooded my own bathroom fighting a towel rack, you cannot put *lord* in front of—"

"Protector, then," said the headwoman, who had raised six children and recognized the genre of the argument. "We'll strike the lord."

"You don't have to strike anything, because I'm not—"

"You came when no one came," she said, and the millhouse went quiet, and so did the theatre. "The war ended and the capitals lit their lamps and nobody's lamps came out here. Yours did. We're not asking you to rule us, boy. We can rule ourselves; we've been doing it in a monster-run valley for forty years, which is harder than anything a throne does. We're asking you to *answer*. When something comes over that ridge — be the one who answers."

The vision held on Issei's face while he lost. The theatre had seen him refuse gods, refuse thrones-by-conquest, refuse Mephisto-grade bargains without blinking. It watched an old woman with flour on her arms beat him in under a minute, because she had asked for the one thing he had never once in his life been able to refuse anyone.

"...Okay," he said. "Protector. *Just* protector. And I get veto power over statues."

The millhouse cheered. The goat received one ballot's worth of dignified applause. The vision faded on Issei signing his name — laboriously, tongue between his teeth — to a charter the headwoman had clearly had drafted for a week.

---

"Well," said Namor, into the theatre's exhale, "I have seen many coronations. That was the first conducted in a *flour barn*, by *ballot*, over the candidate's objection." He laced his fingers. "It was also the most honest. I despise it slightly less than I intended to."

"You intended to despise it aloud and at length," said Shuri. "We had a betting line."

"Oh, I still shall." The Sub-Mariner's voice carried down the kings' row, imperial and unbothered. "A reluctant king is a *tourist*. Hear me — I do not doubt the boy's arm, I have watched thirty-one chapters of his arm. I doubt his *residence*. Atlantis does not want a protector who answers a horn; Atlantis wants a king who is married to her, who breathes her, who cannot leave because leaving would be severing a limb. Reluctance means the door is open behind him. One day the horn blows and the reluctant king is at a *barbecue*." He gestured at the screen. "That valley elected a hero. Heroes visit. Kings remain. When the crisis comes that lasts ten years instead of ten minutes, they will learn the difference."

"Or," said T'Challa quietly, "they elected the only kind of king worth having."

The theatre turned. The Black Panther had not raised his voice in four arcs; he had never needed to.

"In Wakanda, on the day of challenge, I stand in the water at Warrior Falls stripped of the Panther's strength, and any Wakandan of the blood may fight me for everything I am. My father stood there. His father. Do you know what that ritual is, Namor? It is my nation *asking*, once each reign — renewing the question. The throne is not mine. It is *lent* to me, publicly, revocably, and the day my people stop being able to take it back is the day I have become something Wakanda must survive rather than something it has." He nodded at the frozen frame of the millhouse. "Magneto asked last night who granted the legitimacy. There is the answer, and it is the only answer that has ever been real: the governed granted it. Everything else — bloodline, conquest, divine right — is a story the strong tell the tired."

"Spoken," said Doom, "like a man whose borders have never required *correction*."

The iron voice put frost down the row. Doom had watched both scenes without motion, gauntlets flat on the arms of the green-draped throne the machine had built him, and when he continued it was with the terrible patience of a lecture.

"Doom will grant the room this much: the transaction was clean. Service rendered; authority conferred; no fraud in it anywhere. It is the purest mandate Doom has witnessed, and Doom has manufactured several." A pause for the room to decide whether that was a joke; Doom did not assist them. "But T'Challa mistakes a *village* for a proof. Forty souls and a goat may know their own good. Forty *million* do not — they are frightened, divisible, and infinitely persuadable, and the ruler who waits to be asked will stand in polite silence while demagogues burn the granaries to sell the survivors water. Latveria did not ask for Doom." The gauntlet closed. "Latveria was a carcass that six larger nations were carving. Doom took it, ended the carving, and Latveria has not buried a child of famine or invasion in decades. Ask my Latverians whether they would trade their unasked king for a *ballot in a bread bowl*."

"They can't answer that honestly and you know it," said Storm, from the mutant tiers, "which is the entire point, Victor."

"They *would* not, which Doom finds sufficient."

The screen, with the timing the theatre had learned to dread, chimed.

"Ah," said Xavier softly. "Now we see what he does with it."

---

What he did with it was give it away.

The vision opened weeks later: the millhouse again, now with a long table, and on the table a document under construction. Ravel Phenex presided over the drafting with her terrible ledger open — the theatre's old friend from Chapter Twenty-Five — and Rossweisse stood at a slate board writing actual *law*, clause by clause, in a hand so precise it looked typeset. Issei sat not at the table's head but along its side, between the headwoman and the goatherd's father, visibly relieved that other people knew words.

The charter took shape and the theatre read it over the valley's shoulder. A council of seven, elected yearly. The schoolteacher made magistrate, because — Issei's actual argument, delivered with actual passion — "when two of you argue, you already go to her *anyway*, I've watched you, I'm just writing down what's true." The miller: keeper of the granary and the grain-tithe, with public accounts. A retired border soldier, one-armed, to captain a watch that Issei would train but *not* command day to day. Disputes settled by the council; appeals to the magistrate; and the protector—

The vision closed in on the clause. Rossweisse's handwriting, formal as scripture:

*Article One. The Protector holds no land, levies no tax, and judges no dispute. The Protector answers to this charter and to the council, the same as any soul in the valley. The Protector's sole office is this: when the horn sounds, he comes.*

"He wrote himself *under* it," Okoye said. "The strongest thing in that world, and the first law they drafted is the leash, and *he asked for the leash*."

Then the tribute scene, and the theatre got the comedy it needed: a delegation arriving at the Hyoudou residence with a chest of the valley's pooled coin, and Issei chasing them back down the road with it. The chest returned twice more by increasingly creative smuggling — baked into a delivery of bread; disguised as a donation to Asia's shrine, which nearly worked. Final settlement, brokered by Ravel with contractual gravity: the protector would accept, in perpetuity, one standing dinner invitation per season, first rights to the burnt bits, and a scarf knitted annually by the headwoman, quality not guaranteed.

"Mephisto." Loki's voice floated from the villains' dark, silken. "You've gone quiet. A soul refusing *payment* — is that not your least favorite weather?"

"It is the only weather that ruins my business entirely," said Mephisto, and did not elaborate, and looked as though the burnt bits personally offended him.

The vision's last image was small. The horn — an actual horn, ox-bone, hung by the mill — and a night two seasons on when it sounded, once, cracked with panic. And the sky over the ridge opening red before the echo died.

He came. That was all. The vision did not even show the fight. It showed the valley's faces, upturned in the dark, already unafraid — and it ended.

---

"There is your answer, Namor," T'Challa said. "Two seasons. The door behind him stayed open the entire time. He never used it."

"Two seasons is a courtship," Namor said. But he said it slowly, and the theatre noted — the ledgers all noted — that the Sub-Mariner's eyes had stayed on the horn. "Ask me again at twenty years... I will say this and no more: when the horn sounded, he did not send a *deputy*. A king who comes personally to the smallest village of his smallest border — Atlantis has a word for that. It is an old word. We have not needed it recently."

"Delegation," Doom said, "is how power admits it is mortal. Doom delegates *nothing* that matters, because no one else can bear it — and Doom will concede, before this room, that the boy's arrangement is therefore either the greatest foolishness on that screen or a thing Doom's Latveria cannot attempt." A silence. "It is not foolishness. That valley now functions *without him*. If he dies, it holds. Nothing Doom has built holds without Doom, and Doom has always called that design." The mask tilted a fraction. "Tonight Doom calls it inventory."

And then Magneto stood, and the theatre remembered that the question had been his.

"It's a beautiful answer," he said, and the bitterness in it was old, older than the theatre, older than the Brotherhood. "Truly. The governed asked; the strong man knelt to their charter; the leash was his own request. Beautiful. Now let me tell you how it goes when *my* people are the valley." He did not look at the empty steel chair. He looked at nothing this room contained. "Humanity has offered mutants protectors beyond counting. Registration acts — *protection*. Sentinels — built, I remind you, as *guardians*. Camps with lovely names. Every protector we did not choose became a warden within a generation, and the one nation we built for ourselves, the world sank into the sea while the protectors watched. So forgive me if I ask the ugly question under tonight's pretty one: that valley could *afford* to ask, because the thing they asked was kind. What is the mechanism — show me the clause, Rossweisse's fine handwriting, any of it — that keeps the asking real when the protector stops being kind? Article One is a leash held by *forty farmers*. It binds him because *he chooses to be bindable.* That is not law. That is luck wearing law's coat."

"Erik." Xavier's voice, gentle as ever and giving no ground. "You've just described every constitution ever written. They are all promises kept by the people strong enough to break them. That has never made the promises worthless. It makes the *choosing* the entire substance of the thing — and we have now watched this boy choose, for thirty-two chapters, under torture, in grief, at the summit of power that erases mountains. At some point, old friend, the evidence outweighs the wound."

"Nothing outweighs the wound, Charles. Things are merely built beside it."

It was Storm who ended it — Ororo Munroe, who sat with the mutants and had reigned in Wakanda, the only soul in the theatre who had lived on both sides of the row.

"I have been the protector who was asked," she said. "A goddess to villages that voted for me with harvests and prayer, and a queen by marriage and by combat, and a mutant the world would not have elected to *anything*. So hear the one thing I know that the kings' row doesn't. The asking is not a moment. It is not the millhouse, the ballots, the charter. The asking is *renewed every time the horn sounds and he comes* — and it is withdrawn the first time he comes when the horn didn't sound. Watch for that, all of you. Watch every chapter of this arc for the night that boy arrives uninvited." Her eyes, white a moment, dimmed to calm. "I don't believe we'll see it. And I have never said that about power before, in my whole life, and I am still deciding how I feel about saying it now."

The steel-grey track pulsed. The screen printed, unhurried:

**A KING WHO REFUSES TRIBUTE MUST STILL EAT.**

**NEXT: WHAT THE DRAGON HUNTS. AND WHAT HE WILL NOT.**

The wheel rose into the dark. And at the end of the kings' row, unremarked by almost everyone, the fifth throne — the plain steel one, the unmarked one — was gone, as if it had only ever been a question, and had heard enough tonight to withdraw it. Magneto noticed. Magneto said nothing. But he remained standing a long moment after the lights went down, an old king of a country that had never gotten its millhouse, listening to a horn only he could hear.
