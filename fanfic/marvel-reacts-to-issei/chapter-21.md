# Chapter Twenty-One: Wife-Class Summon

**Primary Reaction Groups:** The Eight Brides (summoned) • Romance-interested heroes (Peter Parker, Kamala Khan) • Villains (Doom, Mephisto, Loki, Taskmaster, Kang)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Summoning Rias, Akeno, Xenovia, Irina, Asia, Koneko, Kuroka and Rossweisse" — **Family-Class**

---

The warm gold band had been circling the gacha wheel for what the theatre's body clocks insisted was three days, chiming its little doorbell chime at irregular intervals, and by now everyone hated it.

"It's the *coziness,*" Sam Wilson explained, for the fortieth time, to nobody. "Twenty chapters of business fonts — blood font, prophecy font, audit font — and now it rolls out a bakery menu and rings a *doorbell.* That's not comfort. That's a predator that has learned what comfort looks like."

"Or," said Kamala Khan, who had claimed an aisle seat the moment the FAMILY-CLASS banner lit and not surrendered it since, "it's the family arc. Every long story has one. You do the powers, you do the costs, then you do the *people,* because the people are what the powers are for." She was sitting on her own hands to keep them still. "I've read like nine hundred serials. This is the good part."

"The good part," repeated Peter Parker, hanging upside down from a structural nothing above her row, "of the story where the last arc ended with a kid bruising causality. Sure. Cozy."

The ceiling dimmed. The doorbell chimed once more — and this time the whole warm-gold track lit end to end, and the wheel came down.

It came down wrong.

The theatre had watched that wheel descend twenty times, smug and ceremonial. Tonight it wobbled. Its rings stuttered against each other like gears meeting a foreign object, and around its rim the chess-piece band — pawn, pawn, knight, rook, bishop, greyed queen — flickered and was replaced, piece by piece, with something the room had never seen on it before.

Rings. Small, plain, golden. Eight of them, orbiting.

"Uh," said Peter.

**FAMILY-CLASS PULL,** announced the screen, in the bakery font. **"SUMMONING RIAS, AKENO, XENOVIA, IRINA, ASIA, KONEKO, KUROKA AND ROSSWEISSE."**

"Summoning," said Doctor Strange, and stood up. He did not usually stand up. "That word has a technical meaning. Every prior pull has said *showing.* Vision. Memory. Record." His hands were already moving through the opening figures of a ward. "Summoning is not a genre of theatre. Summoning is a door."

The wheel spun. It screamed — an ugly, grinding, delightful sound, the sound of a machine either breaking or pretending to break, and afterwards no two factions would ever agree on which — and instead of dropping a token into the crystal bowl, it dropped the bowl. The bowl fell, hit the stage, and shattered into a circle.

A summoning circle. Two hundred feet across, crimson, wrought in a heraldry of roses and thorns that Mephisto recognized one full second before anyone else, which was why Mephisto was the only being in the theatre already smiling when the light went up like a struck match and the Gremory crest opened like an eye.

**ERROR,** said the screen, cozily. **ERROR. PHYSICAL INSTANTIATION IN PROGRESS. THE MANAGEMENT APOLOGIZES.**

"It doesn't apologize," said Uatu the Watcher, very quietly, to no one. "It has never once apologized."

The light collapsed. The circle faded. And on the stage of the cosmic theatre, where twenty chapters of visions had played untouchably, stood eight young women — physically, actually, ankle-deep-in-the-real stood — arranged back to back in a formation so practiced it had clearly been drilled, weapons and auras already live.

They had been summoned mid-evening from somewhere with a dinner table; one of them was still holding chopsticks. This detail made the formation more frightening, not less. It takes a certain kind of household to go from dinner to war-footing in the space of a teleport.

The theatre got its first look at the family of Issei Hyoudou.

At the point of the formation: a tall young woman with crimson hair to her waist and eyes of a blue that had never once asked permission, wrapped in an aura of black-red annihilation that made Wanda Maximoff's head come up like a hound's. Beside her, a taller girl with a violet-eyed smile and a long black ponytail, golden lightning crawling up her forearms with what could only be described as *enthusiasm.* A blue-haired swordswoman had materialized already drawing — the blade she pulled out of empty air was so loudly holy that three separate demonic entities in the villains' gallery hissed on reflex — and beside her a brown-haired girl with twin tails had summoned a spear of pure light and a pair of white angel wings, and was standing on the swordswoman's left in the manner of someone who had held that exact position in a hundred fights. A small blonde girl in the middle raised trembling hands wrapped in green healing light and planted herself, terrified and immovable, in front of everyone as if her body were a shield, which made half the street-level rows fall in love with her instantly. A very small white-haired girl with cat ears had simply dropped into a stance that Danny Rand and Shang-Chi both leaned forward to study. A taller, black-haired woman with matching cat ears and golden eyes had gone smoke-edged and lazy, purple mist coiling off her kimono sleeves, positioned — the tacticians noted — to cover every angle her little sister wasn't. And at the rear, a silver-haired young woman was already, in the first three seconds of arrival, surrounded by forty-one rotating Norse runes and an expression of profound fiscal disapproval.

"Contact," said the crimson-haired one, in the flat calm of a commander. "Unknown location. Akeno?"

"No exits, Rias." The lightning-wreathed one's voice was a purr with a blade in it. "No sky. No ley lines. Ara ara. Someone has built a *very* expensive box."

"Koneko?"

The small cat-eared girl sniffed the air once. Her nose wrinkled. "...Thousands of people," she reported, deadpan. "Gods. Monsters. Metal men. Grief. Popcorn." A pause. "No Issei."

The crimson-haired girl — Rias Gremory, and by now the entire theatre had made the connection, because they had spent twenty chapters watching a younger version of that face resurrect a murdered boy with eight chess pieces — turned, slowly, and looked out at the risen house lights. At the Avengers, half out of their seats. At the X-Men. At the shining cosmic tiers. At the villains' dark. At several thousand of the most dangerous beings of an entire multiverse, caught mid-popcorn, watching her.

And above them all, frozen on the vast screen where the machine had helpfully paused it: a frame from her own memory. Her own bedroom. Her own younger self, crying over a dying boy.

The theatre watched the Crown Princess of the House of Gremory understand, in one rotation of her head, exactly what this place was and exactly what it had been doing.

The temperature dropped eleven degrees. That was measured; Vision measured it.

"You have," said Rias Gremory, to the assembled Marvel multiverse, in a voice of court-perfect, arctic courtesy, "been watching my husband's life. Without his consent. Without ours." The black-red aura rose off her shoulders like a slow tide coming in. "I am going to give whoever is responsible one opportunity to explain. I recommend it be excellent."

---

Several things then happened at once, and the theatre would spend days arguing about their order.

Thor stood up, beaming, because Thor's response to nearly all escalations was delight. Carol Danvers stood up glowing, because hers was not. Steve Rogers said "Nobody move" in the voice that worked on gods. The Hulk, interested, knocked over a structural column. And in the villains' dark, Taskmaster began — quietly, professionally — counting combatants and exits, got as far as *eight and none,* and stopped enjoying his evening.

It was the screen that answered her.

**THE AUDIENCE IS NOT RESPONSIBLE,** it printed, and for once the font was neither cozy nor cold, but something almost careful. **THE AUDIENCE IS ALSO BOUND HERE. THEY CANNOT LEAVE. THEY CANNOT ALTER WHAT IS SHOWN. THEY WERE BROUGHT TO LEARN ISSEI HYOUDOU.**

"Learn him," repeated Rias. "Why."

The screen flashed, one frame, the line the theatre had learned to dread:

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT.**

The eight women went very still — a different stillness. The theatre, which had spent twenty chapters being unsettled by that sentence, watched it land on people who loved him, and understood from their faces that they were not hearing it as prophecy. They were hearing it as a threat aimed at someone whose bedside they had all, at one time or another, occupied.

"Ara ara," said Akeno Himejima at last, softly, and the lightning on her arms did not diminish so much as *braid* itself, patient now, worse. "It wants to point our Issei at something. How familiar. Everyone always wants to point him at something." Her smile was serene and her eyes were not. "The last several who tried are variously dead, sealed, or reformed and doing community service."

"Spoilers, Akeno," said Koneko.

**SPOILER SUPPRESSION ACTIVE,** confirmed the screen, primly, and a soft *bleep* trimmed the end off Akeno's next sentence, to her visible, dangerous amusement.

The blue-haired swordswoman — the captions had labelled her **XENOVIA QUARTA** with a small holy-sword icon — had not lowered her blade, because she processed situations in her own order. "I have questions," she announced, to the entire theatre, in the tone of someone reading requirements off a checklist. "One. Is this a hostile force? Two. If it is a hostile force, is it the kind Durandal can cut? Three—" she looked at the impossible ceiling, the sealed geometry, the audience of thousands — "if we destroy this building, does the multiverse fall out?"

"Xenovia, we are *in* the building," said the twin-tailed angel girl — **IRINA SHIDOU**, said the caption, with a halo icon and, inexplicably, a childhood-photo icon.

"That has not always stopped us."

"That has *never* stopped us, that's the *problem*—"

"If I may." Doctor Strange descended the aisle with his hands open and empty, which for Strange was a diplomatic concession on the order of disarmament. "Stephen Strange. Sorcerer Supreme of this Earth — of one of these Earths. What the screen says is true. We are prisoners, same as you. We've watched because we were given nothing else to do." He paused, and then, being fundamentally honest under all the cape: "Though I won't pretend we haven't argued about him like he was ours to argue about. You're owed that admission."

"You are owed nothing, and she should incinerate the room on principle," remarked Mephisto pleasantly, from the dark, because chaos was free.

Rias's gaze went to Mephisto and stopped there.

Every head in the theatre turned with it. The demon lord of one multiverse and the devil princess of another examined each other across the risen house lights, professional to professional, and the room held its breath waiting to see which apex predator blinked.

"You," said Rias, "are a devil."

"Guilty. Chronically." Mephisto spread his hands, delighted to be perceived. "And an *admirer,* Lady Gremory. Of one transaction in particular." He leaned forward, and there it was — the question he had been paying into for twenty chapters, the question the entire theatre had wanted answered since chapter one. "Eight Pawns. Every Pawn you would ever have, spent in one evening, on a dying pervert with no name and no prospects. I have run that trade ten thousand ways and it prices as madness. What did you *see?*"

The theatre leaned in. Uatu leaned in. Somewhere in the dark, Doom's pen was already moving.

Rias Gremory looked at the frozen frame of her own memory above her — the dying boy, the crimson circle — and her arctic court-face did something small and human and completely unbothered by its audience.

"Nothing," she said. "I saw nothing, devil. It was dark, he was dying, and the pieces would not stop until there were eight of them on his chest." She turned back to Mephisto, and her smile was her mother's, which is to say, a diplomatic incident. "You mistake me for a buyer. I wasn't purchasing an asset. I was refusing to let a boy die alone in the dark on his first date. The value came *after.* That's the part your kind never prices correctly: it never exists until you've already paid."

Silence. Then, from the young-heroes rows, Kamala Khan burst into applause, entirely alone, was mortified, and discovered that the sound had broken something open — because a wave of it followed, rolling up through the street-level rows, the Spider-family, half the X-Men, and one (1) rapidly self-silencing Ravager.

Mephisto sat back with the expression of a man who has just watched the market do something religious. "No wonder the boy is incorruptible," he murmured. "He was *founded* wrong. You can't buy out a company whose opening capital was a gift."

---

Introductions, after that, happened the way weather happens: everywhere, unevenly, and beyond anyone's control.

The small blonde one broke formation first. This surprised the tacticians, who had pegged her as the protected asset — and then it stopped surprising anyone, because **ASIA ARGENTO** (caption icon: green rings; second icon: a small church, crossed out; the street rows remembered her story, and hurt) walked to the edge of the stage, bowed formally to several thousand of the most dangerous beings in existence, and said:

"Um. Thank you for being sad when Issei died."

The theatre — a room containing Thanos — did not know where to put itself.

"We watched some of it. Just now, while everyone was shouting. The screen showed us the first night here. When he — the spear." Her hands were knotted in front of her, and her voice wobbled and did not stop. "People he never met were sad for him, and angry for him, and some of you wanted to avenge him even though he was a stranger. He would say that's the coolest thing that ever happened to him. He would be *unbearable* about it." A watery, luminous smile. "So. Thank you for loving him a little. We know how easy it is."

Frank Castle looked at the floor. Logan looked at the ceiling. Daredevil, who could hear eight heartbeats and knew exactly which parts of that speech had been hardest to say, put his hand over his mouth like a man in church.

"Oh no," Kamala whispered to Peter, wiping her face with her sleeve. "It's the healer. It's always the healer. I have no defenses against the healer."

The very small cat-eared one — **KONEKO TOUJOU**, said the caption, with a cat icon, a chess-rook icon, and, after a moment's consideration by the machine, a warning icon — conducted her own diplomacy. It consisted of walking the aisles in silence, sniffing selected legends of the Marvel multiverse, and issuing verdicts.

"Smells like metal and old grief," she reported, in front of Logan. "Approved." In front of Spider-Man, upside down and rigid with the effort of being respectful: "Smells like guilt and web fluid. Approved." She paused longest in front of Frank Castle, who met her yellow stare without blinking, and the theatre remembered — because the screen unhelpfully displayed the memo — that this man had privately *marked* Raynare in chapter one.

"You put a mark on the fallen angel," Koneko said. "The one who killed him."

"Yeah," said Frank.

"Save your bullet." Nothing moved in the deadpan little face. "Rias melted her years ago."

The villains' gallery made a low, involuntary, professional sound, like an auction house seeing a lot withdrawn. Frank Castle studied the crimson-haired princess across the theatre for a long moment — the princess who was at that moment coldly renegotiating spectatorship rights with a cosmic machine — and then nodded once, the nod of a man closing a file.

"Good," he said.

Her elder sister worked the *other* aisle. **KUROKA** — caption icons: a cat, a bishop piece, and a lengthy criminal record that scrolled for visibly longer than anyone else's — had slunk into the villains' dark within ninety seconds of arrival, as though she'd read the seating chart in a previous life, and draped herself over an empty chair between Loki and Taskmaster with a warmth that alarmed them both.

"Nya. So *this* is the naughty section," she said, approvingly. "Cozy. Bad lighting, good posture, everyone doing math about my family. I used to sit in rooms like this." She let that hang, kimono sleeve swaying, golden eyes half-lidded and not remotely soft. "Word of admin from a retired professional: the math doesn't work. You're all thinking, *eight of them, leverage, pressure points.* I know, I know — I once thought in pressure points too, it's very soothing. But you saw what the little red boyfriend does when someone touches Asia." She smiled, and every predator in that gallery recognized the smile as one of their own, which was precisely the problem. "Now imagine eight of us, nya. And imagine that we are *not* the restraint in this family. We are what the restraint protects *you* from."

"I like her," said Loki, to no one, in the tone of a man reporting a symptom.

"She was a criminal," observed Kang, coldly. "It's in her file."

"*Was,* nya." Kuroka examined her nails. "He forgives people. It's the most annoying thing about him. It's why I'm getting married in a white kimono instead of dying in a cell." She flicked her golden eyes up at the time-conqueror, and just for a moment the playfulness parted like mist off a cliff face. "You should hope you never meet him. He'd probably forgive you too. You don't survive that, you know. You just become somebody new who owes him."

Mephisto, two seats down, wrote that sentence somewhere behind his eyes and filed it under *known toxins.*

Meanwhile the silver-haired one had located, without difficulty, the two most fiscally responsible entities in the theatre, and was having the evening of her life in the worst way. **ROSSWEISSE** — caption icons: Valkyrie helm, rook piece, and a stack of coupons that made her groan aloud when she saw it — stood surveying the impossible architecture with an expression the theatre's accountants recognized in their bones.

"This structure is dimensionally sealed, self-repairing, and climate-controlled for several thousand occupants of wildly incompatible biologies," she said, to Vision, because he looked like someone who would know. "What is the energy budget. Who *pays* for this."

"Unknown," said Vision. "The theatre appears to draw on—"

"Because if a facility of this size is being run *rent-free* to force strangers to watch my fiancé's most embarrassing moments in ultra-high definition," Rossweisse continued, over him, gathering momentum like a fjord flood, "then someone has effectively set *infinite money on fire* for the purpose of ruining my honeymoon planning, and I will find the line item, and I will have *words*—"

"A VALKYRIE!" boomed Thor, arriving like a festival. "I knew it! The bearing, the runework, the righteous anger about budgets! Sister-in-arms, well met! Tell me, how fares Odin All-Father in your—"

"*Your* Odin or *my* Odin?" Rossweisse pinched the bridge of her nose. "Because if he is anything like my former employer, he is currently reading indecent magazines somewhere and letting his security detail — which was *me* — do his paperwork." She stopped. Composed herself. Bowed, with full formal precision, because she was, under all of it, a professional. "Forgive me. Rossweisse. Ex-Valkyrie, current teacher, future wife. Your Majesty's reputation precedes you even across cosmologies, and I am — genuinely honored, and also *someone here is going to explain the catering costs to me before this night ends.*"

Thor turned to the theatre at large, misty-eyed, and declared, "I love this family."

---

It was Irina and Xenovia who changed the room's temperature back, between them, without meaning to.

Irina had found the young heroes. It was inevitable; she was the same wattage. Within minutes she was sitting backwards on a chair in front of Kamala and Peter and half the champions' row, wings tucked, chopsticks (she had kept the chopsticks) gesturing, cheerfully cross-examining them about being teenage soldiers of dubious institutions, a subject on which all present had opinions.

"—so *he* got resurrected as a devil, and *I* got reincarnated as an angel, which technically makes us a workplace-compliance nightmare," she was explaining, glowing faintly. "But my church said love that protects people is the point of the whole thing, so! We made it work. Heaven approved the engagement. There was *paperwork.*" She beamed with an aggressive purity that made Peter feel like he should hydrate more and call his aunt. "Also I've known him since we were little, so I have blackmail-quality memories of him from before *any* of these women, which in a harem is called *seniority.*"

"It is called being seventh in the engagement order, Irina," said Xenovia, from three feet away.

"IT'S CALLED SENIORITY."

"I need — okay. Sorry. Hi. Question." Kamala had her hand up. She had had her hand up for some time. "Big fan, love the formation you guys did, very cool, extremely scared of all of you. But — the *arrangement.*" She gestured at the eight of them, scattered across a theatre they had conquered in an hour. "Eight fiancées. And you all just — work? Like, we've been watching this show for twenty chapters and half this room keeps arguing about whether it's a red flag, and now you're actually *here,* and I have to know, because from outside it reads either really beautiful or really—"

"Complicated," said Peter quickly.

"I was going to say *fanfictiony.*"

Xenovia handled it, because Xenovia handled every question at the same setting: maximum bluntness, zero shame.

"It works because we decided it would, and then we did the work," she said. "This confuses people. They think the number is the hard part. The number is arithmetic. The hard part is the same one everyone has — being known and staying anyway — multiplied by nine." She sheathed Durandal, finally, which lowered the room's ambient holiness to survivable levels. "He fell in love with all of us stupidly, one at a time, usually while bleeding. We fell for him the same way. We fought about it. Some of us fought *literally*; I have dents. Then we built rules, like a peerage, or a sword style. Nest law." She looked around the theatre, blunt as a hammer and twice as honest. "You have a man made of iron and a soldier out of time and a wizard surgeon and you form *teams* and call it found family. We formed a family and everyone calls it scandal. I do not understand the difference. Possibly the difference is marketing."

The theatre's cultural-analysis section — T'Challa, notably, and one entire row of thoroughly divorced Asgardians — began arguing about that sentence immediately, and would not stop for three chapters.

But it was Rias who closed the night, because the stage had always been hers.

She had finished her negotiation with the machine — negotiation being a generous word for what had occurred; the screen would later display terms and the terms would favor House Gremory — and now she stood at center stage, beneath the frozen frame of her own younger self, with her seven co-fiancées drifting into formation around her as naturally as iron filings finding north.

"We cannot go home," she announced, to the theatre, "until the machine's purpose is served. So. Terms." She raised one finger. "One. We stay. If you are going to watch my husband's life, you will watch it with the eight people who can tell you when the machine is lying by omission — *within,*" a glare at the screen, "the spoiler rules."

**ACCEPTABLE,** said the screen.

"Two. We are not exhibits. The next being who points a scanner, a probability engine, or a *sonnet* at any of my sisters without permission will be returned to their seat in a number of pieces I have not yet decided." The black-red aura flickered, briefly, in the shape of good manners. "Three. When the machine shows something private — and it will; I have seen tonight's banner and I know what our life contains — you are permitted to laugh. He would want you to laugh; he is, God help us all—" (she winced; three of the eight winced; Irina's halo fizzed) "—*funny.* But you will laugh the way family laughs. At the table. Not through the window."

She surveyed them — Avengers, X-Men, gods, monsters, the villains' respectful dark, one Watcher whose vast pale face was turned toward her like a man rereading a page that had changed — and the theatre understood that its power dynamic had been rewritten in an evening. For twenty chapters they had watched a boy who did not know he was being watched. That innocence was over. The story had noticed them back, and the story had *standards,* and the story's standards were currently generating a black-red aura at center stage.

"Agreed?" said Rias Gremory.

Several thousand of the deadliest beings in the Marvel multiverse said, in effect, yes ma'am.

"Good." She sat down — front row, center, in seats the theatre swore had not existed that morning: eight of them, warm gold, upholstered like a bakery menu. "Then somebody explain the popcorn system to Rossweisse before she audits it, and *roll the next pull.*"

---

Far above, the wheel rose home, humming — and no one who had watched it scream and shatter its own bowl could quite call the hum anything but satisfied. Uatu the Watcher stared up at it and did not sit for a long time.

*Malfunction,* he thought. *A machine that has never misprinted a comma. And tonight, by accident, it acquires eight witnesses who love him, embeds them in the audience, and teaches this room that the boy's family is an army.*

*Nothing was summoned tonight,* thought the Watcher, who existed to watch and had just been watched by a machine, *except on purpose.*

The screen dimmed to warm gold, and rang its little doorbell, and printed the hook it had been saving all evening:

**NEXT PULL PREPARING.**

**RUMOR LEDGER DETECTED IN AUDIENCE SPECULATION: "SEVENTEEN WIVES."**

**THE MANAGEMENT LOOKS FORWARD TO THE CORRECTIONS.**

From the front row, in the dark, came eight distinct sighs — regal, sultry, blunt, chirpy, gentle, deadpan, purring, and *fiscal* — and then, faintly, the sound of Rossweisse asking whether corrections were billable.
