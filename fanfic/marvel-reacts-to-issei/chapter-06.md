By the sixth session, the theatre had developed a rhythm, and the rhythm had developed factions.

It was not that anyone had forgiven anyone. Doom still stood in his aisle as if the aisle were a throne room awaiting furniture. The Green Goblin still laughed at the wrong moments, at wounds, and had twice been briefly and mercifully unconscious for reasons Jessica Jones declined to explain. But hostility takes energy, and the theatre metabolized energy into boredom, and boredom into seating charts. The Avengers held the center like a garrison. The X-Men had colonized the western mezzanine. The mystics clustered near the front where Strange could squint at the screen's underlying enchantment, which he had described, with increasing professional resentment, as *not being made of magic so much as magic being made of it*.

And in the last two sessions, a new bloc had formed without anyone planning it: everyone in the room who had ever meant something by the word *dragon*.

Thor sat at the heart of it, boots up on the seat in front of him — the seat forgave this; the seat forgave everything — with Valkyrie on his left running a whetstone down Dragonfang out of habit rather than need. Beta Ray Bill sat beyond her, monumental and courteous, having apologized to four separate rows on his way in. Loki had draped himself across two seats at the end like a knife pretending to be a scarf. And down one row, close enough to be included and far enough to make a point of it, sat Shang-Chi, hands loose on his knees, and beside him — occupying eleven seats that the theatre had silently reupholstered in a heat-resistant weave — the compressed, human-adjacent form of Fin Fang Foom, arms folded, green tail coiled twice beneath the row like a cable no one wanted to trip over.

"I still say," Valkyrie said, to no one in particular, "that if the lizard sneezes, I'm suing the venue."

"The venue," Foom said, without turning his head, "is the only thing in this room I respect. Do not tempt me to shorten the list."

Above them all, in the dark that was not darkness, the great tiered silences of the cosmic gallery had also begun to lean in — and that was the part nobody in the lower seats liked to think about. Uatu the Watcher had not blinked in two sessions. The Living Tribunal had manifested exactly once, considered the screen for a period of time that made three telepaths nauseous, and not left. And there was a seat in the highest tier that no one could look at directly, which Strange had privately concluded was Eternity being polite about the fact that he was also the walls.

The screen woke with its usual absence of ceremony.

The wheel spun. It had, by now, trained the room like a bell trains dogs: conversations guttered out, feuds went on standby, and forty thousand of the most dangerous beings in any reality settled in to watch a teenage boy's life get dealt out of a deck.

The wheel ticked. Slowed. Ticked. And then it did something it had never done before.

It stopped on a color that wasn't on the wheel.

Not gold. Not the platinum shimmer of the SSR pull that had shown them Issei dying in a fountain. The wedge under the pointer turned a deep, banked-ember red, the red of a forge nine hours after the smith went home, and the screen's flat dead voice acquired — for one word only — something almost like respect.

**"DDRAIG'S INTRODUCTION" — RARITY: MYTHIC.**

"Mythic," Loki repeated, sitting up by degrees, the way a cat un-liquefies. "New tier. How interesting. The machine has been saving that word."

"The machine," Strange said quietly, from the front, "has been saving it for *him*."

The screen opened not on Kuoh Academy, not on Rias Gremory's clubroom with its absurd Victorian furniture, but on darkness — a warm, enclosed, breathing darkness — and the room understood, with a collective prickle, that they were looking at the inside of Issei's dream. The boy stood in it, small, in his ridiculous school uniform, and before him, filling the dream the way a mountain fills a window, was an eye.

It was the size of a longship. It was the color of the wheel's new wedge. It opened slowly, with the grinding patience of something that had not been in a hurry since before the local star systems had settled their orbits, and the slit pupil narrowed on the boy the way an archivist's attention narrows on a document that should not exist.

**[So,]** said a voice, and the theatre's sound became a physical pressure, gravel and bronze and depth, **[you are the one.]**

Fin Fang Foom's arms came unfolded.

It was a small motion. From a being who had spent two sessions in reptilian stillness broken only by contempt, it was a shout.

On screen, the boy did what the boy always did, which was to lead with the worst possible question. "Are you — wait, are you the voice from my arm? Are you my *arm*? Have I been dreaming inside my own arm?"

**[I am Ddraig,]** the voice said, and paused, and the pause itself had grammar — the pause of a speaker deciding how much of the truth a small creature could carry. **[The Red Dragon Emperor. In the old tongues of your world, Y Ddraig Goch. I have been called the Welsh Dragon, the Heavenly Dragon, the Dragon of Domination. Gods have called me a catastrophe with wings. They were not wrong. Once, there were two of us who could not be measured — myself, and a white nuisance named Albion, whom I fought across three skies until the skies filed complaints.]** Another pause. **[Then the war of gods and dragons ended badly for everyone, as wars do, and the victors could not kill us, so they did something crueler. They folded us into tools. Sacred Gears. Gauntlets for the arms of mortals. I have lived in the left hands of your kind for longer than your kind has had history.]**

"Blasphemy," said Fin Fang Foom.

He said it conversationally, which somehow made it worse. Heads turned along the dragon bloc.

"You object to the *screen*?" Shang-Chi asked mildly.

"I object to the *sentence*," Foom said. His voice had lost its bored register. "A Heavenly Dragon. An apex of that world's dragonkind, by his own telling — the equal of gods, the terror of gods — reduced to a *utensil*. Look at him." One taloned finger indicated the eye that filled the screen, the vast banked-ember patience of it. "That is not a spirit that was meant to fit inside a boy's wrist. They did not seal him. They *insulted* him, eternally, and called it mercy. If it were done to me, I would spend ten thousand years learning to hate, and the ten thousand years after that teaching the lesson back."

"And that," Thor said, "is precisely why it was never done to you, Foom. No one dared come close enough."

"Flattery."

"Assessment." Thor's boots came down off the seat. On screen, the boy was babbling — *so I've got, like, a legendary dragon, in my ARM, does that come with, is there a monthly fee* — and the vast eye was watching him with an expression that Thor, who had grown up on the stories his grandfather's skalds told of the great wyrms, was beginning to find familiar. "Listen to the beast, though. Truly listen. Does he sound insulted?"

**[You are,]** Ddraig was saying on screen, with the air of a scholar annotating a specimen, **[without exaggeration, the least promising wielder I have ever been assigned. And I want you to understand the size of that statement. I have been carried by warlords. By saints who burned. By a woman who conquered a third of a continent and wept about it every night. By men so hungry for my power that it hollowed them out and wore them like a coat. Some of them mastered in a decade what will take you thirty years. All of them heard my name and asked me for strength.]** The pupil narrowed. **[You heard my name and asked if I come with a fee.]**

"That's a no, then?" dream-Issei said. "On the fee?"

**[...Partner,]** said Ddraig, and there was a sound from the dragon like tectonic plates sighing, **[the fee is that I have to listen to you.]**

The theatre laughed. It surprised them — it kept surprising them, session after session, that a room containing Hela and the Goblin and a Watcher could laugh together at anything, but the boy had that effect, and now, unexpectedly, so did the dragon. There was something in the timing of it. Something in the dryness. Rocket Raccoon was wheezing. Even Doom's fingers, drumming the rail, had briefly stopped.

But Loki was not laughing. Loki was leaning forward with his chin on his laced fingers and his eyes very green, and when he spoke it was low, pitched for the dragon bloc alone.

"He said *assigned*," Loki murmured. "Wielders come to the gauntlet by fate, or blood, or the whim of whatever system distributes these Sacred Gears. Ddraig doesn't choose them. He *receives* them. Centuries of tenants. Centuries of being inherited, like a debt." His teeth showed, not quite a smile. "Do you know what a being like that is worth, brothers, if one could learn what he actually wants? Power that ancient, that leashed, that *tired* — tired things make bargains—"

"Loki." Thor did not look at him. "The last person in this theatre to try to acquire something of that boy's on a technicality was Mephisto, and the room fed him his own contract. Sit with your hands where I can see them."

"I'm merely appreciating—"

"Sit," said Valkyrie, "with your hands where *I* can see them," and Dragonfang shifted an inch in its scabbard, and Loki sat.

On screen, the dream deepened, and Ddraig kept talking, and the theatre discovered that it was possible to be held rapt by exposition, provided the exposition was delivered by something that had personally attended the events. He spoke of the Great War between the three Biblical factions the way a man speaks of a bad neighborhood he used to live in. He spoke of Albion — **[my rival, my mirror, sealed as I was sealed; somewhere in your world right now a person is walking around with the White Dragon Emperor in their body, and one day, partner, that person will try very hard to kill you, and I will need you to be less stupid by then]** — with an enmity so old it had fermented into something adjacent to fondness. He spoke of his prior wielders in inventory, in numbers: how many had died in battle, how many had been consumed by his power, how many had spoken to him, in the end, as anything other than a weapon.

**[Three,]** he said, to that last one, unprompted, as if the count had been sitting in him a long time. **[Out of all of them. Three ever asked me a question about myself that was not tactical.]**

"Four," said dream-Issei.

**[What?]**

"Well, I'm gonna. Ask you stuff. Like — okay, first question." The boy sat down cross-legged in his own dream, in front of an eye the size of a longship, utterly failing to be afraid, and the theatre watched an ancient catastrophe with wings experience surprise. "What was your favorite one? Of the skies. You said you fought across three skies. Which sky was the best sky?"

The silence on screen lasted four full seconds.

**[...The second one,]** Ddraig said at last, gravel gone strange and slow. **[Over the northern sea. There were lights in it. Green and violet, in curtains. Albion and I stopped fighting for the better part of an hour and neither of us ever admitted it.]**

Beta Ray Bill made a sound. It was low, and it was in his chest, and Valkyrie glanced at him and then away, because the sound was recognition. A creature engineered into a weapon, hearing a creature folded into one, remember the aurora.

"There," Thor said softly, into the dragon bloc's quiet. "*There.* You asked whether he sounds insulted, Foom. He sounds like every old warrior in every hall I have ever drunk in. He is not looking for vengeance. He is looking for one person who asks about the sky."

"Then he has decayed," Foom said, but the scorn had developed a crack in it, and everyone in the row could hear the crack. "A dragon is pride. A dragon is *sovereignty*. We do not — " the tail shifted beneath the seats — "we do not bond. We are not horses."

"No one said horses," Shang-Chi said.

"Your people carved us into gate guardians and dance costumes—"

"My people," Shang-Chi said, unruffled, "understood something your pride will not let you say aloud: that the dragon who serves nothing serves his hunger, and hunger is the smallest king there is." He nodded at the screen. "In the stories I was raised on, the dragon is not diminished by attachment. The dragon *is* attachment — to the river, to the rain, to the household that honors it. Power that touches nothing is just weather. That one—" the eye, vast, patient, banked-ember — "was weather for a long time. Listen to how he says *partner*. He is done being weather."

Fin Fang Foom did not answer. Fin Fang Foom watched the screen with his arms folded again, but his claws, this time, were not quite still.

The wheel spun, and stopped on honest gold.

**"BOOSTED GEAR PENETRATE" — RARITY: SUPER RARE.**

"Okay, hold on—" said Peter Parker, from the middle rows, already half-standing. "New rule. Whoever's naming these needs to run them by literally one adult—"

The screen cut to a training clearing at dusk, and Peter sat down, because the footage did what the footage always did: it made the joke feel like it had happened in a different year.

Issei was losing. That part was familiar; the theatre had watched him lose in Chapters Two and Three with a completeness that had been almost artistic. He was sparring against a summoned training golem of Rias's design, a hulking thing of layered rock and defensive magic circles, and his boosted punches were splashing off its barriers like rain off a war memorial. Double, quadruple, eightfold — the green gem on his gauntlet chanted **[BOOST]** in Ddraig's voice, and the numbers climbed, and it did not matter, because the golem's defense scaled like a cliff scales a wave.

"This is the lesson," Doom said, from his aisle, to nobody, in the tone of a man grading homework. "The lesson every brawler learns. Multiplied force against structured defense is arithmetic against architecture. Architecture wins."

**[Partner.]** Ddraig's voice, on screen, low, inside the boy's head. **[Stop. Breathe. You are trying to be strong enough. That is Albion's game — his and every knight and every god who ever built a wall. Walls are a question: 'are you strong enough?' Tonight I am going to teach you my answer.]**

"Which is?" Issei gasped, on one knee, gauntlet smoking.

**[The question is stupid.]**

The gem flared. Something moved through the boy's arm on screen that made three separate sensor-brained beings in the theatre — Vision, the Ghost Rider's flame, and something in the cosmic tier — physically flinch, and Issei stood up, and hit the golem once, gently, almost a tap, and the word the gauntlet spoke was new:

**[PENETRATE.]**

The fist did not break the barriers. The barriers were not broken. That was the part the theatre spent the next several minutes failing to process: the layered circles remained intact, the rock armor remained intact, and the golem's core — its anchoring sigil, buried beneath a meter of enchanted stone at the center of everything — quietly shattered, as if the fist had been *delivered* there, as if distance and armor and durability had been items on a form and someone had declined to fill the form out. The golem stood whole for a moment, a fortress with nobody home. Then it came apart into gravel.

The silence in the theatre had texture.

"Explain that," said Steve Rogers, quietly, to Strange, in the voice he used when he already understood and was hoping to be wrong.

"It ignored the defense," Strange said. He had gone very still. "Not overwhelmed. Not bypassed a weakness. There was no weakness. It treated 'defense' as a category that doesn't apply to it. The attack resolved directly against the target's core as though everything between fist and core were... commentary."

"So the question 'how tough are you'—" Sam Wilson started.

"Is no longer a question," said T'Challa. He was looking at his own hand, at the seam where a Vibranium claw slept, and the theatre's habit of showing people the exact thing that kept them alive being made irrelevant was beginning to be recognized as a pattern. "The suit does not matter. The shield does not matter. Doom's force fields, the Hulk's hide, an Asgardian's bones. If that ability matures, every defensive technology and every durable body in this room is a costume."

"It won't scale," Tony Stark said, too fast. "Nothing scales like that. There's always a cost curve—" and then he stopped, because on screen Ddraig was already answering, the way the screen sometimes seemed to answer, and it was worse than a cost curve.

**[It will cost you,]** the dragon said, as the boy stared at his own smoking fist. **[Penetrate is one of mine — one of my original abilities, from when I was a sky-filling thing and not a wrist ornament. My flame did not burn castles. It burned the *idea* of the people inside them, and the castles fell down out of grief. Wielders have unlocked this before you. Most of them I did not offer it to twice. A power that ignores every wall will, eventually, ask you which walls of your own you are willing to live without. The hollow ones loved it. It made them hollower.]** The gem's light banked low, ember-warm. **[You, I think, might be safe. You are too stupid to be hollow. It is, and I want this recorded somewhere permanent, the only tactical advantage you possess.]**

"Then why," Issei said, grinning through obvious exhaustion, "do you sound proud of me?"

**[I do not.]**

"You totally do. Ddraig. Buddy. You sound like my dad at sports day."

**[I have incinerated pantheons for less than 'buddy.']**

"You *do* though."

Another tectonic sigh. And then — quietly, in the tone of a confession filed under something else — **[I have carried hundreds of you. I outlast all of you; that is the shape of my sentence. I long ago stopped learning your names before your funerals. It was easier.]** A pause. **[I know your name, Issei Hyoudou. I knew it before you unlocked anything. I do not know yet what that means, and at my age, not knowing something is the closest I come to being young. Do not die soon. That is not tactical advice.]**

The boy, being the boy, ruined it — "Aw, you LOVE me, I have a giant dad dragon" — and the screen let his delighted laughter run, and the theatre sat in the sound of it, and nobody said anything for a while, because there was nothing to say that the ledger of the room hadn't already recorded: the strategists had a new nightmare, and the dragon had a son, and only one of those facts was going to matter in a hundred years.

It was Thor who broke it. Afterward, he would insist he'd only meant to think aloud.

He rose from his seat with the unhurried gravity of a man about to toast a hall, looked up at that vast banked-ember eye still glowing on the paused frame, and said, warmly and clearly and to a screen:

"Ddraig of the Red! Your rival's skies notwithstanding — if ever you tire of that sentence of yours, know that Asgard's halls have always kept a place at the fire for old dragons, and better mead than that boy's world will ever—"

The eye on the screen moved.

Not the footage. The footage was paused; the frame-counter in the corner had stopped; the boy's laughing face hung frozen mid-frame. The eye moved anyway, rotating in its socket with a slow grind of attention, through the fourth wall as if the fourth wall were, like every other wall, commentary — and found Thor, in a theatre outside its universe, and held him.

**[Mead,]** said Ddraig, into a room of forty thousand suddenly extremely still legends, **[would be a start, thunder god. And tell the green one draped on the end seat there that I have eaten things that look at me the way he does.]**

The eye slid back into frame. The counter resumed. The boy's laughter unfroze and rolled on, oblivious.

For a long moment, the only movement in the theatre was in the highest tier, where Uatu the Watcher — who had spent his existence secure in the one-way glass between observer and observed — leaned back, very slowly, in a seat that had never before needed to hold his full weight.

Loki had gone the color of old paper. Valkyrie's whetstone had stopped. Fin Fang Foom was on his feet, all eleven seats' worth, and the expression on that ancient green face was not scorn and was not fear, and it took Shang-Chi, watching him, a moment to place it, because it was so foreign there.

It was respect. And underneath the respect, buried where pride kept its unpaid debts, something that looked almost like envy — of a sentence, of a wrist, of an idiot boy who asked about the sky.

"Well," said Rocket, into the silence, in a voice about a third its usual size. "The arm knows we're here. Anybody else wanna rethink the seating chart?"

**Secrets Revealed**
- Ddraig is the Red Dragon Emperor (Y Ddraig Goch), one of the two Heavenly Dragons; sealed into Sacred Gear form after the great war of gods and dragons, and has been passed between mortal wielders for millennia.
- Albion, the White Dragon Emperor, exists as Ddraig's sealed rival — carried by an unknown wielder who will one day come for Issei.
- Penetrate is one of Ddraig's original abilities: attacks resolve directly against a target's core, treating armor, barriers, and durability as nonexistent — it invalidates "how tough are you" as a question.
- Ddraig stopped learning wielders' names ages ago to spare himself their funerals — but he learned Issei's name before the boy unlocked anything, and admits he doesn't know what that means.
- Ddraig is aware of the theatre and its audience — he addressed Thor (and threatened Loki) directly through a paused frame.

**Alliance / Opinion Shifts**
- Thor: full-throated advocate for Ddraig; publicly extended Asgard's hospitality to the dragon — and was answered.
- Fin Fang Foom: shifted from scorn ("a dragon reduced to a utensil") to open respect, with a buried thread of envy for the bond itself.
- Loki: covetous scheming sharply chilled after being singled out by Ddraig through the screen; now frightened as well as fascinated.
- Shang-Chi: articulated the counter-philosophy that a dragon is defined by attachment, not sovereignty — quietly won the room's dragon debate.
- Doom, T'Challa, Stark, Strange, Rogers: strategist bloc now privately treats Penetrate as a category-level threat that obsoletes every defense in the theatre.
- Uatu the Watcher: visibly unsettled — the observed can see the observers.
