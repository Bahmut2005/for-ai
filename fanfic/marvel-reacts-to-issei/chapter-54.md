# Chapter Fifty-Four: Wakanda Watches

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Wakanda • Dora Milaje • Shuri • Black Panther (T'Challa)

1. "Shuri and Rias Discuss Dragon-Forged Materials" — **Rare**

---

The theatre held its breath. Not literally—the theatre's entire mechanical philosophy was built on the impossible, so the literal cessation of air happened only as metaphor and consequence. What happened here was subtler: the absence of movement, the sound machine making when a room agrees it is about to witness something that matters.

The Avengers block had spoken its piece over three nights. The X-Men had convened their careful, suspicious judgment. The Illuminati had argued themselves into neutral exhaustion. Now the focus shifted, the way a spotlight turns when the stage manager has decided the audience has seen enough of one story and must see another.

The machine had been quiet during the Latverian chapter. Doom's invitation had hung in the theatre like a gauntlet dropped into still water—it rippled, but nobody rushed to pick it up. Now the screen flickered, and the banner descended:

**ARC SIX CONTINUES: MARVEL JUDGES THE DRAGON.**

**PRIMARY EVALUATION BLOCK: WAKANDA. PRIMARY OPERATOR: PRINCESS SHURI.**

**GACHA PULL: 1 OF 2.**

---

In the Wakandan section, Shuri was already leaning forward, eyes bright with the kind of focus that made her brother—sitting two rows back in the Black Panther's elevated seat—both proud and cautious. T'Challa knew that look. It was the look she'd worn the night she'd recalibrated the kimoyo beads in under forty seconds, then spent the next four hours redesigning them because she'd thought of an improvement that couldn't wait until morning.

The screen bloomed with color: the Boosted Gear, crimson and intricate, held up to scrutiny in what the captions identified as a private workshop in some corner of the dragon's territory. Rias Gremory was there, the high-devil whose name had appeared in various pulls across the evening—she moved with the confidence of someone born to power and had kept it close without ever having to shout about it. Beside her, Issei Hyoudou rotated the Sacred Gear slowly, the light catching on its geometric patterns, its channels, the places where power flowed.

"The construction," Rias was saying, her voice carrying the precision of someone who spoke both draconic politics and the language of artifice, "is based on layering, yes? The core doesn't multiply power so much as it chains exponential growth—each boost compounds the last."

"Ten-second windows," Issei said. He had a way of talking that the theatre had learned to listen for: he was respectful without being deferential, casual without being careless. "Ddraig built it that way on purpose. Anything longer and the containment system would fracture. The dragon had to balance acceleration against integrity."

Shuri's eyes had gone very still.

"The containment architecture," she murmured, and the theatre heard her—the kimoyo bead array carried her voice clear as intention into the Wakandan section. "That's not mechanical constraint. That's active compensation. The gear is recalibrating itself *during* the boost cycle."

Rias smiled. Not a diplomatic smile—a genuine one, the kind that happens when someone says something true and you weren't sure they were listening. "Exactly. The Boosted Gear doesn't just accelerate power. It distributes the strain across temporal slices. If you think of ten seconds not as a duration but as a series of iterations—"

"—you're spreading the load across multiple harmonics instead of one continuous catastrophe," Shuri finished. She stood, and T'Challa—who had seen his sister stand in the presence of genius and refuse to be impressed by it—recognized the posture of capitulation. "That's Vibranium-adjacent. Not the metal itself, but the principle. We've spent fifteen years trying to distribute impact load across the atomic lattice. You've done it across *time*."

The vision pulled tighter, showing the internal mechanism of the Boosted Gear in cross-section. The captions obliged: power conduits arranged in recursive patterns, each one slightly offset from its neighbor, creating a kind of tessellated network that allowed force to cascade without creating feedback loops.

"How often does it fail?" Shuri asked.

There was a pause in the vision—not a cut, but a hesitation, the way a conversation pauses when the question asked is the one that matters.

"Once," Issei said quietly. "Against an opponent I should have prepared better for. The gear took damage I couldn't immediately repair. It cost me a hand and most of a week in recovery. Ddraig and I redesigned the secondary channels after that. The redundancy buffer is better now."

"A catastrophic failure at the ninth boost level," Rias added, "occurred during a test phase before Issei was ready for combat. The Boosted Gear was destroyed and had to be rebuilt from memory. This is why the gear has a learning curve—it adjusts its calibration based on the wielder's physiology and the specific opponent's energy signature."

Shuri was writing notes now, her kimoyo bead array projecting equations into the air faster than most people could think. "So the gear is partially adaptive AI. It's learning your style and pre-compensating for anticipated load spikes."

"Ddraig's personality is written into every layer of it," Issei confirmed. "He's not just a power source. He's the algorithm."

The vision pulled back. Rias was lifting another object—something smaller, more delicate, a gauntlet that shone with a different kind of light, something closer to the divine than the raw draconic power of the Boosted Gear.

"This is Transfer," Rias said. "He can push power through his wives, his allies, his peerage. The mechanism is—"

But Shuri was already moving, her hands sketching patterns in the air, reconstructing the architecture in real-time as she watched. "Sacred energy pathways that don't create sympathetic resonance because the differential is managed through willful intention, not mechanical separation. He's routing power through *consent*, not constraint. The recipients have to *allow* the power in, and because they're choosing it, there's no rejection response."

Rias watched her work, and something fond crossed her expression—the look of someone encountering another consciousness that worked at the same velocity. "It's why Transfer requires emotional and spiritual alignment. Mechanical power systems fail on unwilling recipients. This one depends on trust."

The vision showed Issei demonstrating: power flowing from the Boosted Gear into Rias herself, who glowed brighter, stronger, wearing his amplification like a second skin. It was intimate in a way that had nothing to do with romance—it was the intimacy of absolute vulnerability, of letting another being's energy move through your own.

"He's never tried to force it," Rias said. "On anyone. Not once. The power is there; he could theoretically override someone's resistance if he wanted to. He's never wanted to."

Shuri had stopped writing. She was watching Issei's expression in the vision—watching him demonstrate trust to someone he loved, watching him explain it to someone he respected.

"That's not craftsmanship," Shuri said to the theatre at large. "That's *philosophy*. He's built his entire power system around consent and collaboration. The gear doesn't make him strong. It lets him make other people strong."

In the Avengers section, Tony Stark made a small noise—not quite a laugh, not quite something else. "The kid built a power system based on consent architecture. I'm going to need to sit with that for a while."

The vision released them.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

**"WAKANDAN COUNCIL DEBATES ALLIANCE" — RARITY: RARE.**

The shift was immediate: from intimate workshop to formal chamber, all soaring wooden arches and the kind of lighting that understood shadows as part of the structure's grammar. The Wakandan Council convened. T'Challa sat at the center, but the power distributed itself—Okoye commanding the Dora Milaje's representative presence, her posture radiating the specific certainty of someone who had killed for her nation and would do so again; Shuri at the data end, surrounded by holographic projections and the kind of organized chaos that was actually evidence of comprehensive thought; M'Baku representing the Jabari, massive and skeptical, his arms folded in the posture of someone waiting to be convinced; the elder councillors—W'Kabi among them, scarred and cautious—representing the established power structures that had kept Wakanda sovereign through centuries of pressure.

"The question," T'Challa was saying, and his voice carried the particular patience of someone who had asked a room to think carefully about something that mattered, "is not whether this young man is powerful. The Avengers and the Illuminati have established that conclusively. The question is whether his power represents a threat to Wakandan autonomy."

"Any foreign actor with power of his magnitude is a threat," W'Kabi said flatly. "We have seen what gods and Titans can do when they decide to interfere in human affairs. Whether his intentions are currently benign is irrelevant. The capacity for harm cannot be negotiated."

"His refusal to seize power when he could have, repeatedly, throughout his entire history—that suggests something beyond benign intention," said Ramonda, T'Challa's mother, her voice carrying the weight of someone who had survived coups and external pressure and had learned to read intent in patterns. "A threat would have already acted."

M'Baku shook his head. "A threat would recognize that acting now would destroy the negotiating position he has built. He waits because waiting serves his purposes. We assume goodwill when he may simply be patient."

Shuri's holographic array shifted, pulling up comparative timelines—moments when Issei had engaged with sovereign nations, with powerful kingdoms, with opportunities to seize control.

"He had the chance in the Eastern territory," Shuri said. "Seven years ago, according to the canon dossier. A kingdom fragmented by war. The survivors asked him to rule. He refused. He helped them establish democratic processes instead. Self-governance. He trained administrators and then he left."

"That proves nothing except that his goals are not territory," Okoye said. She was not being difficult—her voice carried the thoughtful tone of someone analyzing a military opponent. "It means his power serves something other than conquest. But that something is still unknown. That still represents risk."

"The risk matrix," Shuri said, and her projections shifted again, showing data laid out in clear categorical terms, "assumes his primary motivation is either conquest, wealth accumulation, or ideological domination. None of those show up in his pattern history. His wives gain property through their own efforts. He actively disarms conflicts rather than escalating them for leverage. His ideology is consistently pro-choice, pro-self-determination, pro-individual agency. Those aren't conquest motivations."

"They are the motivations of someone building a power base through ideological loyalty," W'Kabi countered. "He creates followers who thank him for empowering them. That's the most durable control system possible—it doesn't require violence. It just requires people to believe they chose it."

T'Challa was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, he addressed the room rather than responded to W'Kabi, which meant he was sharing something he had thought through carefully.

"I watched the footage from the trafficking fleet destruction," he said. "Not the gacha pull—the actual records, which our intelligence gathered independently. Do you know what I found remarkable? Not what he did. What he refused to do."

He waited, and the room waited with him.

"He could have taken the ships," T'Challa continued. "He could have seized the manifest records and the salvage rights and consolidated a fleet. He could have built an empire on that wealth and firepower. A seventeen-year-old with that level of power and that much suddenly available firepower—most would consolidate. He signed it all to the freed people. Every ship. Every asset. He kept nothing."

"That's idiocy," M'Baku said flatly. "That's a child making emotional decisions."

"That's a child making a choice about what kind of man he wants to be," Ramonda said. "I have raised children. I have seen the difference."

"So have I," Okoye said quietly, and there was something in that statement that carried weight—Okoye had trained warriors, had shaped choices between power and restraint. "The question is whether that choice is sustainable. Can someone maintain that level of refusal forever? Or does power eventually corrupt everything it touches, and we're just watching the arc before the corruption?"

Shuri pulled up one more set of data: comparative analysis, scaling across time. "Corruption markers in all known power-focused individuals show progressive entrenchment. Increased justifications for control, expanding definitions of necessary harm, rationalizations that expand the acceptable cost of objectives. Issei shows none of that. His kill-restraint is actually stricter now than it was seven years ago. His governance models are *more* democratic, not less. His refusal to consolidate power appears to be strengthening over time, not weakening."

"That doesn't prove trustworthiness," W'Kabi said. "It proves consistency within whatever pattern he's established."

"Yes," T'Challa said, and his voice had taken on the particular firmness of a king who had spent his life thinking about responsibility. "But here is what matters: I would watch a threat. I would study its patterns and predict its moves and prepare for the moment it breaks the constraints. What I see when I watch Issei Hyoudou is not a threat learning to hide itself. I see a person who has looked at the power available to him and *chosen not to use it the way power is usually used*."

He stood, and the council room recognized the shift—T'Challa about to deliver judgment.

"The absence of a desire to seize power," he said, "is the strongest evidence of trustworthiness I have ever encountered. A person who wants power badly enough will eventually find justification to take it. A person who *refuses* power despite having the capacity—that person has aligned themselves with something stronger than ambition. That person has chosen their morality more carefully than I chose mine, and I chose mine deliberately."

M'Baku made a dissenting noise, but he was listening.

"So the question is not whether to ally with him," T'Challa continued. "The question is the *terms* of our relationship. Do we trade? Do we share technology? Do we extend security guarantees? Do we invite him into formal alliance? Those are the choices available to us."

"Technology sharing is a vulnerability," W'Kabi said immediately. "Every piece of Vibranium-enhanced tech we show becomes a potential compromise if his intentions change."

"Then we don't share Vibranium secrets," Okoye said. She had been quiet, thinking. "But Shuri's analysis of his craftsmanship philosophy—that transfers without transferring vulnerabilities. We could learn from his approach to consent-based power transfer. We could share data in exchange for learning integration principles."

Shuri was already taking notes, her projections shifting to begin modeling trade possibilities.

"And if he needs assistance?" Ramonda asked. "If a threat emerges and he requires Wakandan military support?"

T'Challa considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "We evaluate the threat. We make the decision for ourselves, not because of obligation to him. But I would not exclude the possibility. A person who refuses power when offered doesn't ask for power when in crisis. If Issei Hyoudou asks for military assistance, it means the threat is existential enough that his own capacity—and by all measures his capacity is exceptional—is insufficient. That would be worth fighting for."

---

The vision released them into the theatre's careful quiet.

Up in the Wakandan section, Shuri was still writing, her hands moving faster than speech could follow. Beside her, Okoye had the expression of someone who had just quietly decided that a certain risk was acceptable. T'Challa was looking up at the screen, and his expression carried something that the theatre had learned to recognize: the particular calm of a king who had made a difficult decision and found it aligned with both his pragmatism and his conscience.

In the Avengers rows, Steve Rogers was watching T'Challa carefully. When their eyes met, T'Challa nodded, very slightly. It was the gesture of one person communicating to another: *this is complicated, but it's right.*

"That was not a vote to arm him," Tony said to Carol, just quietly enough for his section to hear. "That was a king saying he respects someone's choice about what they refused to become. I'm going to be thinking about that one for weeks."

Carol didn't respond immediately, but when she did, her voice carried recognition: "That's a higher bar than most of us hold ourselves to. That's a *much* higher bar than most of us hold ourselves to."
