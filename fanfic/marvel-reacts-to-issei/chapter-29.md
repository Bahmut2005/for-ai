# Chapter Twenty-Nine: Every Dragon Bows

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Kings and sovereigns (Black Panther, Namor, Doctor Doom, Magneto) • Dragon-connected (Thor, Loki, Fin Fang Foom, Iron Fist) • Guardians of the Galaxy (Groot, Rocket) • The resident fiancées

---

The theatre had spent a full intermission arguing about nest law, and the argument had migrated, as arguments about family always did now, toward the eight women seated in the honoured centre rows.

It had been like this since the machine had committed its one great malfunction — or its one great joke; the room had never settled the question — and dropped Rias Gremory and seven of her co-fiancées bodily into the audience. The Hyoudou household had been watching its own life get pulled from a wheel for nine chapters, correcting the record with the weary authority of primary sources, and reorganizing the theatre's social gravity simply by existing in it. Kings, it turned out, drifted toward Rias. Warriors drifted toward Xenovia. Everyone with sense drifted away from Akeno when she smiled.

Tonight the visitors were the kings.

T'Challa had come down from the Wakandan seats with the unhurried formality of a state call. Namor had come because T'Challa had, and would deny it. Doom had not come to anyone — Doom did not come to people — but the aisle he stood in happened, tonight, to command a view of both the screen and the Gremory heiress, and Doom stood in it the way other men occupied thrones. Magneto sat one row above them all, at the altitude of a man who had opinions about kings and had buried several.

"Your husband's brother," T'Challa was saying to Rias, with genuine diplomatic care, "the dragon Beaucephalis. In last night's vision he addressed Issei as *nest-brother*. In Wakanda that word would carry legal weight. Does it carry weight there?"

"More than a treaty," said Rias Gremory. "Less than a wedding. About the same as a Pawn." She smiled the smile that made Mephisto take notes. "We're a very acquisitive family."

Above them, the wheel came down early — no chime, no preamble — and every conversation in the theatre broke off mid-word.

Because the wheel was doing something new. Its rings, which clattered and gloated and milked every pull for drama, had gone silent and *aligned*, ring within ring within ring, like an orrery agreeing on a date. The rarity band cycled past gold, past the cozy fireside glow of Family-Class, past the black-letter dread of Secret, and settled on a colour the theatre had no file for: a red so old it had opinions about the other reds — scale-red, ember-red, the red of a cave with a hoard in it.

**RARITY: MYTHIC,** said the screen, and the word did not glow. It *smoked*.

"New tier," said Shuri, sitting up. "Twenty-nine chapters and it's been holding a whole *tier* back—"

The wheel tipped. That was the strangest part, the part half the room would swear to and half would deny: the great wheel of destinies inclined on its axis, a few degrees, holding the posture of a thing lowering its head, and dropped its token into the bowl from that position, like tribute.

**"DRAGONS BOWING TO ISSEI" — MYTHIC.**

**A NOTE ON SEQUENCE,** the screen added, in its politest font, the one it used when it was about to cheat. **THE FAMILY BANNER RUNS AHEAD OF THE WAR BANNER. YOU HAVE NOT YET SEEN THE YEARS THAT PURCHASE THIS DAY. YOU ARE SEEING THE RECEIPT.**

"I hate when it does that," muttered Sam Wilson.

"I love when it does that," said Loki.

The theatre took a breath, and swallowed them.

---

A mountain range in the Underworld, under a violet sky. The gacha's captions arrived with unusual restraint, as if the machine itself had lowered its voice: **THE DRAGON'S RANGE. NEUTRAL GROUND, BY CUSTOM OLDER THAN THE FACTIONS. NO THRONE HAS EVER STOOD HERE.**

And on the wide stone shelf where no throne had ever stood, dragons were landing.

They came out of the violet like weather systems deciding to be personal. A colossal purple-scaled warrior whose wingbeats rearranged the clouds — **TANNIN, THE BLAZE METEOR DRAGON, FORMER DRAGON KING** — and the theatre's veterans of Arc Two made an involuntary noise, because they knew that one; they had watched him chase a screaming teenager up a mountain for a month of training visions and had privately ranked him above most drill sergeants and several natural disasters. A gleaming gold-scaled presence with a connoisseur's heavy-lidded eyes — **FAFNIR, DRAGON KING, HOARD-LORD** — whose caption the machine declined, mercifully, to elaborate. A lean jade serpent out of an eastern sky, grinning like mischief given wingspan — **YU-LONG, THE MISCHIEVOUS DRAGON**. A shadow that arrived without arriving, black flame in the shape of an apology, coiled politely at the edge of the gathering — **VRITRA, PRISON DRAGON (ATTENDING VIA HOST)** — with a very tired-looking blond boy waving awkwardly from beneath it. And behind and around and above them, the others: mountain dragons and sea dragons, young drakes jostling like starlings, elders with moss on their shoulders, a hundred shapes from a dozen mythologies that had never once agreed on anything.

Far off, at the edge of the vision, something white glinted against the violet and did not approach. The screen printed one line beside it — **[RIVAL: RECORD PENDING]** — and refused every eye that tried to read more.

In the centre of the shelf, in his school blazer, hands jammed in his pockets, profoundly underdressed for the assembled biomass of several pantheons, stood Issei Hyoudou. And at his right shoulder, wings half-furled, chin high, chest out, stood a young dragon the theatre had met one chapter ago: Beaucephalis, nest-brother, wearing the expression of a sibling at a graduation — insufferable, incandescent pride, plus the visible intention to embarrass him at dinner afterward.

*Stand up straight, brother,* said Beaucephalis, in the private telepathy the vision let them all overhear. *You're being historic.*

*I'm being* hungry, Issei thought back. *How long does this take? Nobody told me if there's food.*

"Oh, good," said Peter Parker, in the theatre, wiping his eyes for reasons he'd have called laughter. "He's still him."

Tannin moved first.

The Blaze Meteor Dragon — a former Dragon King, a being who had traded rank for the survival of his people, who had trained this boy by trying honestly to kill him — brought his colossal head down through all that violet air, slowly, deliberately, past the altitude of respect, past the altitude of greeting, down to the altitude of *fealty*, until one horn nearly brushed the stone.

And then, like a mountain range kneeling, the rest.

Fafnir lowered his golden head with the gravity of a hoard changing hands. Yu-Long stopped grinning — the theatre had not known he could — and bowed the full serpentine length of himself in a wave that took visible seconds to travel. The black flame of Vritra banked low, and the tired blond boy beneath it bowed too, grinning, mouthing something that the vision captured as *"you owe me lunch."* The starling-swarm of young drakes crashed into the ground in their haste to kneel, one of them bowing so enthusiastically it somersaulted, and an elder with moss on her shoulders inclined a neck that had not bent, the caption said, **IN NINE CENTURIES**.

A hundred dragons. A dozen mythologies. One held breath in two universes.

Every dragon on that mountain bowed to Issei Hyoudou — except one.

Beaucephalis stood.

He stood at his brother's shoulder with his chin up and his wings half-spread, and no dragon on that shelf so much as glanced at him for it, because — the caption printed it in small, almost tender letters — **NEST LAW: FAMILY DOES NOT BOW. FAMILY IS WHAT THE BOW PROTECTS.**

And Issei Hyoudou, seventeen-flavoured even in this out-of-sequence future, went scarlet to the ears, waved both hands like a man flagging down a bus, and said, audibly, to a hundred bowed dragons of the assembled mythologies of his world:

"Whoa — hey — no no no, you don't have to — please get up? Everybody? You're all way older than me, this is *super* weird — okay, you know what, has everyone eaten? Because my house does a thing, it's mostly rice but there's a lot of it—"

Not one dragon rose until he had finished being embarrassed. Then they rose together, and the sound they made was not a roar. It was lower than a roar, and older: a hundred dragons humming one note into the stone, the way the vision explained a nest greets its own.

On one small drake's face, as it came up from its somersaulted bow, was an expression the theatre knew from its own children's rows. It was how Kamala Khan had looked at Carol Danvers, once. It was not fear of a dragon. It was *want* — the want to grow up into something.

The vision held there — the boy, the standing brother, the risen host, the far white glint that had not come closer — and released them.

---

The lights came up on a theatre of kings, and the kings had all leaned forward.

T'Challa spoke first, quietly, and the room organized itself around his quiet the way it had learned to. "I have taken the throne of Wakanda by combat," he said. "Twice. And I will tell you what every monarch here knows and none of us likes to say aloud: you can win a crown. You can inherit one. You can seize one. But the *bow*—" he shook his head slowly, eyes still on the dark screen. "The bow you cannot take. Compel it, and you get choreography — bodies bending, nothing given, an inventory of future knives. What we just watched was not choreography. Did you see the elder? Nine centuries. A neck does not unbend nine centuries of *no* for power. Power is exactly what dragons already have." He turned, and found Rias Gremory watching him, and inclined his head to her fractionally, king to house. "It unbends for one thing only. For the belief that what you bow to will spend itself for you before it spends you. That is the entire technology of legitimacy, and your husband appears to have discovered it by accident, while asking whether anyone had eaten."

"He always asks if anyone's eaten," said Koneko Toujou, deadpan, from the fiancée rows. "He fed a Dragon King rice. Four helpings."

"He asked *Fafnir* if the hoard got heavy to sleep on," Rias added, with the serenity of a woman who had stopped being surprised years ago. "Fafnir now consults him on hoard arrangement."

Namor had listened to T'Challa with his arms folded and his chin at its customary altitude, which was above everyone's. "Kneeling is currency," the Sub-Mariner said flatly. "In Atlantis they kneel to me because the sea is cold and vast and mine, and drowns what displeases it. I have never once mistaken it for love, and I have never once required it to be." He paused. The pause went on one beat too long for contempt. "But no one drowns for that boy. Nothing compels them. They crossed a sky to lower their heads to a child in a school uniform who *told them to stop* — and they finished the bow first." His jaw worked. "I have commanded the bow of an ocean. I find I do not have the thing that was on that mountain, and I find," each word extracted like a tooth, "that I have spent several minutes wanting it."

"Imperius Rex admits envy," murmured Loki, delighted, from the dark. "Somebody carve the date."

"Wanting what a better outcome costs is not envy, trickster. It is *policy*."

But the theatre's weather had already shifted toward the aisle where Doom stood, because Doom had been silent too long, and twenty-nine chapters had taught the room what Doom's silences cost whoever they landed on.

"Doom has categorized the boy three ways," Doom said at last, to no one, which meant to everyone. "In the first arc: a phenomenon. A curiosity of foreign physics, to be studied. In the second: a constant. A fixed hazard, to be designed around. Doom closed his notebook on the boy nine chapters ago and considered the taxonomy complete." The mask tilted toward the dimmed screen. "The taxonomy is not complete.

"Consider what the vision actually depicted. Not power — the room has seen his power; power is arithmetic and Doom does not bow to arithmetic. It depicted a *polity*. A nation without borders, without census, without treasury — a nation of apex beings from rival mythologies who agree on nothing, and who have now, freely, unanimously, constituted themselves around a single person. Sovereignty is not land, whatever the cartographers pretend. Sovereignty is the ability to be *bowed to without compulsion*. By that measure — the only measure that has ever survived contact with Doom — the boy is not a phenomenon and not a constant." A pause, and the theatre heard the third category arrive like a door unbolting. "He is a *peer*.

"One does not study peers. One does not design around peers. One *negotiates* with them." Doom turned, deliberately, and faced the fiancée rows, and performed a thing the theatre would argue about for the rest of the hundred chapters: a shallow, precise, unmistakable nod of the crowned head, sovereign to sovereign's house. "Latveria recognizes the Red Dragon Emperor. Convey it, when you are returned to him." And then, because Doom was Doom, the mask swept the heroes' section: "Doom notes that he has now extended the boy more formal courtesy than any government represented in this room. You have had twenty-nine chapters. Doom advises the relevant departments to feel appropriate shame."

"Did — did Doctor Doom just establish *diplomatic relations* with the gacha?" said Sam Wilson.

"Yes," said Maria Hill, writing.

From one row above the kings came the dry rasp of Magneto not applauding. "How moving," Erik Lehnsherr said. "The monarchs have found a mirror. Allow an old man to state the other reading, since no one else at this altitude will." He rose, and though the seats were velvet the air around him carried its familiar iron weight. "I watched a species tonight. Feared, hunted, mythologized — every culture in that cosmology tells stories about slaying them; several of the entities in *this room* are wearing dragon-slaying reputations like medals. And what has that species just done? It has gathered its whole surviving strength on neutral stone and *lowered its head* to a member of the majority that hunts it. Tell me the difference between a liberator and a comfortable cage, T'Challa, because I have buried people in both. When the feared kneel to a protector, the protector's love is real and the kneeling is still a leash. The price of a savior," and the old anger in it was banked but alive, "is that you stop saving yourselves. My people have been offered that bargain by every smiling government on our Earth. I have made a career of declining it on their behalf."

The theatre's mutant rows were very quiet. So, for a moment, was everyone.

Then Magneto did the thing that separated him, forever, from the men he was often shelved with: he argued against himself in public. "And yet," he said, slower, "the brother did not bow. He stood — armed, proud, at the centre of the ceremony — and the ceremony *approved*. Note it, all of you, because it is the whole document: whatever that boy is building, it has a standing clause. A structure that ranks family above fealty, that leaves room at its own coronation for an unbowed head—" he sat back down, and finished like a man annoyed at his own conclusion, "—is not a cage I recognize. I will require more data. I *dislike* requiring more data."

"I am Groot," said Groot.

Several kings looked around. The Flora colossus was sitting where he always sat, among the Ravager and Guardian rows, enormous hands folded, bark-face tilted at the screen with an expression no one had been reading for twenty-nine chapters because no one had thought to.

"He says," Rocket translated, with the put-upon air he used to disguise exactly how carefully he listened, "'you're all measuring the wrong thing.'" A pause. "Don't look at me, I just work here."

The screen chimed before anyone could ask the right question.

**GACHA PULL: 2 OF 2.**

**"DDRAIG'S DECLARATION" — EPIC.**

---

The mountain again. The risen host, the humming stone. And then the hum stopped, because the gauntlet had appeared on Issei's left arm — unsummoned, of its own will, the green gem kindling like a hearth — and every dragon on the shelf went still in a way that taught the theatre something new about silence.

"**They did not come only for you, partner,**" said Ddraig, gently, from the gem. "**With your permission, I would speak. It has been… some time, since I addressed our kind with my own voice.**"

"'Course," said Issei. And then, because he was Issei: "Want me to hold the arm up higher? Is there an acoustics thing?"

"**The arm is fine, partner.**"

The gem flared, and the voice that came out of it was not the fireside rumble the theatre had grown fond of. It was the *other* voice — the one from chapter one, the one Thor had felt in his teeth — vast, tectonic, older than the mountains it addressed.

"**Kin,**" said Ddraig, the Welsh Dragon, the Red Dragon Emperor, one of the Two Heavenly Dragons whose war had once frightened God. "**Look at me. Look at what remains — no wings, no sky, no body; a voice in a jewel on a boy's arm. You bowed to him. Now hear what you bowed to.**

"**I have had hosts beyond your counting. Kings among them. Saints among them. Warriors the sagas still flinch from. Every one — every one — heard my power and asked the same first question:** ***how much?*** **And the red I gave them fed on the asking, and fed on their hatred, and fed on their fear, until—**"

The vision stuttered. For a quarter-second the mountain was gone, and there was fire in the shape of a screaming man, and a sound like a lullaby sung backward — and the screen slammed a black card across it:

**[RECORD SEALED: RARITY INSUFFICIENT. THE HISTORY OF THE RED WILL BE PAID FOR WHEN IT IS PAID FOR.]**

In the theatre, in the dark, the Watcher's great hands closed slowly on the arms of his seat. The fiancée rows had gone rigid, all eight women wearing the identical expression of people who knew exactly what lived behind that black card. Nobody asked. Everybody filed it.

The mountain returned. Ddraig had not stopped speaking.

"**—until the name I carry meant only ruin, and mothers in six pantheons used it to frighten children into bed. Ten thousand years, kin. Ten thousand years the Red Dragon Emperor has been a word for grief.**

"**Then a fallen angel murdered a foolish, lecherous, unremarkable boy in a park, and I woke in the worst host I have ever been given.**" A ripple went through the dragons; on-screen, Issei said "*hey*," wounded, and the theatre laughed damply. "**No talent. No hatred. Nothing to feed on. He heard my power — and his first question was not** ***how much.*** **His first question, kin, in the dark of his own soul, in the ruin of his own death, was whether I got bored in there. Whether I was** ***lonely.***

"**I have been a god's problem and a devil's weapon and a saint's burden. I had never once, in ten thousand years, been anyone's** ***friend.***"

The gem burned brighter, and somewhere in the crowd of drakes a hatchling — overcome, oblivious, magnificent — piped up with the first two bars of a children's song the theatre had encountered in an earlier, sillier chapter, a song about a certain dragon and his enthusiasm for a certain anatomy, and the entire shelf of assembled dragon-kind visibly clenched, and Ddraig's voice performed the audible equivalent of a long-suffering stare.

"**Yes,**" the dragon said, with terrible dignity. "**The song. I am aware of the song. I have required counseling because of the song. Hear me anyway, and hear this first: the children of two species sing my name now, and the song is not a warning. Do you understand what that is worth? Ten thousand years, and a dragon's name is sung without fear. He did that. Not with the power — the power has never once made anything better than it found. He did it with the rice, and the door that is never locked, and the arm that goes out before the thinking does.**

"**So hear my declaration, and carry it to every peak and every deep: this is Issei Hyoudou. He is not my host. He is not my cage. He is my partner, and the pride of my ten thousand years. From this day the Red Dragon Emperor is not a grief and not a warning. From this day, the Red is a NEST — and any dragon who has no nest, any hatchling orphaned, any elder abandoned, any of our kind alone in any sky of any heaven — may fly to him, and be home.**"

The sound the dragons made then was a roar, and it was not fealty. Fealty had been the bow. This was *assent* — a hundred dragons of a dozen quarreling mythologies answering one note with one note, and the mountains answered the dragons, and far off at the edge of everything the white glint rose, circled once, and was gone.

And Issei Hyoudou, Red Dragon Emperor, nest of dragon-kind, stood in the middle of the largest honour his cosmology had ever assembled, sniffed hard, wiped his face on his blazer sleeve, and said, wrecked and beaming:

"Okay — okay, that's — Ddraig, buddy, warn a guy — RIGHT. Everybody who can fit in a human house, follow me, everybody who can't, we're doing the yard. Rossweisse is gonna — actually don't tell Rossweisse the headcount, we'll ease her into it—"

In the theatre, Rossweisse fainted quietly into Irina's shoulder over the implied grocery arithmetic, and the vision released them on the sound of a hundred dragons laughing.

---

Nobody spoke for a while. The kings had run out of categories, and knew it.

It was Danny Rand who broke the silence, from the Defenders' block, looking at his own fist with an expression fifteen years old. "I fought a dragon for my power," the Iron Fist said. "Shou-Lao the Undying. You climb, you bleed, you beat it, you punch your fists into its molten heart, and you carry the mark forever. That's how it's done. That's how it's *always* done — dragons are the wall you break yourself against to become worthy." He closed the fist. "He never raised a hand to any of them. And they crossed a sky. I'm going to be sitting with that one for a real long time."

"In every saga of my people," Thor said, and his voice had gone to the low reverent register it saved for Ddraig alone, "the dragon is the ending. Fafnir under the mountain. The serpent at the world's rim, waiting for me — for *me*, waiting." He looked around the theatre, and the grin that broke on him was sunrise. "Dragons bow to no one. That is what the word *means*, in every tongue that has it. You have all just watched the sagas be rewritten by a boy with rice, and I am — I find I am *glad*, and I did not expect to be glad. Loki. Are you well? You've said nothing cruel in minutes."

"I'm thinking," said Loki, and he was; those nearest could see it, the god of stories turning the thing over and over and finding no seam. "You can steal a crown. I've done it — lovely work, highly recommended. You can steal a throne, an army, a *face*." His eyes did not leave the dark screen. "You cannot steal a bow that was never demanded. There's no object to take. It exists only in the giving. Twenty-nine chapters I have watched that gauntlet like a jewel in a case, and tonight I finally understand there is nothing in the case worth having *without the boy*, and the boy cannot be stolen, because the boy is not a possession, he is a *practice*." He smiled his thinnest smile. "I hate it here."

And then, from the vastness at the theatre's edge, from the quarter where the seats had been reinforced without comment sometime around Arc One, came a sound of shifting scale-mass that everyone had learned to brace against.

Fin Fang Foom had risen.

For twenty-nine chapters the dragon of Kakaranthara had radiated scorn at the screen — *a true dragon does not serve* — a sentence he had produced in various arrangements at least once per arc, to the room's increasing weariness. He looked at the dark screen now for a long moment, immense and unreadable.

"I said a true dragon does not serve a human," Fin Fang Foom said, and the acid was gone from it, leaving something bare. "I was answered tonight. He does not serve. There is no collar in that gauntlet, and there is no throne on that mountain. There is a nest, and the nest was built for the ones who lost theirs." The great head was silent a moment. "I am the last of my landing party. My kind is far, and my name on this world is a warning, and no child of any species has ever sung it without fear. I did not know, until tonight, that the other thing could be *had*."

And Fin Fang Foom lowered his head to a screen showing nothing at all — one full degree past the altitude of respect — and held it there, and the theatre understood it was watching its own universe's first dragon bow to the nest.

"I am Groot," said Groot, into that silence.

"Buddy, now's maybe not—" Rocket started, and stopped, because Groot had turned and looked at him, and whatever passed between them made the raccoon's ears go flat. "Fine. FINE." He cleared his throat, and his voice came out wrong, and he pretended it hadn't. "He says — he says you kings have been arguing all night about what the dragons got. Vassal, subject, liberated, whatever. He says you got it backwards, 'cause you're all… trunk-people. You think the tree is the trunk." Rocket's paws worked. "I was a twig, okay? Him. He's sayin' — he was a twig. Whole body gone, blown to splinters savin' a bunch of idiots, and everybody grieved the trunk. And he says the trunk was never the tree. The tree is the *roots*, and the roots only need one thing, which is good ground. Put the splinter in good ground and everything that ever was the tree comes back. Bigger."

Rocket stared at the floor.

"He says the dragon in the gauntlet lost his body, his sky, and his war. Ten thousand years a splinter in bad ground — kings and saints and psychos, and every one of 'em pots, not ground, 'cause a pot's just a cage with dirt in it. And then the splinter got planted in that kid." He jerked a thumb at the dark screen, roughly. "Good ground. First good ground in ten thousand years. So the dragons weren't bowin' to the kid, and they weren't bowin' to the gauntlet either. They came to see what *grew back*." A breath. "And he says the big green guy with the mask—" Doom's head turned, slowly, "—finally got somethin' right, but even he's measuring wrong, 'cause you can't recognize a nation, it ain't a nation." Rocket's voice cracked one notch and he bulled through it. "It's a *forest*. It's gonna be a forest. And forests don't got kings."

"I am Groot," Groot added quietly.

"Yeah." Rocket wiped his face with the back of a paw, fast, furious about it. "'And I am Groot.' He knows, pal. Somewhere in a arm in another universe. He knows."

Nobody in the theatre said anything for a long time, and none of the kings tried to have the last word, which — Uatu noted, from the highest tier, in the inventory he was keeping of impossible things — had never once happened before.

---

The wheel rose toward the ceiling, rings unlocking back into their usual smug clatter. As it went, the warm gold band of the Family-Class banner lit up along its rim, and a small counter appeared beside it that had been counting, apparently, for nine chapters where no one could see:

**FAMILY-CLASS JACKPOT: ACCUMULATED.**

**NEXT PULL: JACKPOT.**

A doorbell chime. The cozy font. The theatre regarded it with the flat wariness of people who had been taught, at rarity-point-blank range, exactly how this machine did arithmetic — nine chapters of cost before a purchase, nine chapters of warmth before a *what*?

"It's going to be nice," said Peter Parker slowly, reading the room's dread and naming it. "It's going to be really, really nice, and then it's going to do something to us. That's its whole move. Breakfast, birthdays, the yard full of dragons — and then something in the last frame."

"**NEXT PULL PENDING,**" said the screen, cozily.

Rias Gremory looked at the warm gold light for a long moment, and — those seated near the fiancées would report it later, and be believed — reached over and took Asia Argento's hand, though neither of them could have said against what.

The lights went down on a theatre that had watched every dragon bow, and one stand, and was beginning to suspect the machine had shown them the standing on purpose.
