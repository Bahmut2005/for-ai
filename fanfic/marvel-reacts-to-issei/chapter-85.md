### SECTION I: THE BROTHERHOOD CONVENES

The X-Men's approach to first contact with Issei was fundamentally different from both the Avengers and Doom. Where Steve had come with formal respect and Tony with honest confession, where Doom had come with intellectual challenge, the mutants came with something that felt almost like recognition.

Magneto arrived first—Erik Lehnsherr, master of magnetism, whose entire life had been defined by oppression and the decision to resist it. His presence was quieter than Doom's, less aggressive, but weighted with the kind of personal history that comes from genuinely difficult choices.

"Hyoudou," Magneto said, and there was no pretense in his greeting. "I came to witness your rage earlier. You spoke with the precision of someone who understands what violation means. You have experienced oppression."

"Not the same kind as yours," Issei said carefully. "But yes. In my world, I was marginal. Weak. Dismissed. The change came through my own growth, and through the choice to protect people I cared about."

"That is not dissimilar from my experience," Magneto replied. "Except that my growth came from recognizing that others like me would never be free unless we took freedom ourselves."

"Different conclusions from similar premises," Issei observed.

"Yes," Magneto agreed. "Which is worth discussing."

Charles Xavier descended to the platform soon after, his wheelchair moving with practiced ease across the Theatre's terrain. The telepath's presence was different from Magneto's—calmer, but somehow more penetrating.

"I hope you'll forgive the intrusion," Professor Xavier said, his mind already conducting gentle, careful scans of Issei's psyche to verify consent boundaries. "I wanted to meet you before we discussed the nature of consciousness, choice, and what happens when power intersects with both."

"You're a telepath," Issei said, not hostile but cautious.

"I am," Xavier confirmed. "And I have violated minds with my abilities more times than I'm entirely comfortable admitting. So I understand the nature of what happened in the Theatre from a uniquely personal perspective."

Scott Summers joined them—Cyclops, his eyes hidden behind a visor that barely contained the destructive power contained within. He moved with the bearing of someone who had spent his entire life understanding that his power could destroy, and had made the deliberate choice to use it only in defense.

"Cyclops," he said simply. "I'm the field leader of the X-Men. I wanted to meet someone who appeared to have made genuine philosophical choices about protecting people weaker than himself."

Logan—Wolverine—arrived last, and he carried with him the scent of whiskey and the bearing of someone who had spent centuries learning to accept his nature rather than fight it.

"Kid," he said to Issei, and there was surprising warmth in his gruff voice. "Heard you got violated by a multiversal archive without consent. That's rough. Come have a drink."

## THEATRE LEDGER — SECTION II: THE CHALLENGE OF POWER

The conversation between Issei and Magneto took on the quality of a serious philosophical debate almost immediately. The two men, both powerful, both protective of those they cared about, engaged in fundamental disagreement about the nature of freedom.

"Your philosophy is protective," Magneto observed. "You position yourself between those you love and the threats that would destroy them. This is admirable. But it is also fundamentally limiting. You position them as protected, which implies weakness."

"It does not," Issei countered. "Protection and autonomy are not mutually exclusive. I protect my wives from threats, yes. But I also support their development of power and independence. Protection is temporary. Empowerment is permanent."

"And when the system that oppresses you refuses to allow empowerment?" Magneto pressed. "When the only way to achieve freedom is to violently reject the system entirely?"

"Then you must measure whether the violence creates more freedom than the oppression removed," Issei said carefully. "Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes you genuinely do need to break systems. But you cannot assume that breaking systems automatically creates justice. It creates power vacuum, and vacuum is often filled by the next tyrant."

"You suggest submission instead?" Magneto's tone was sharp, challenging.

"I suggest strategy," Issei replied. "I suggest that some battles are won through cooperation and some through force, and wisdom consists of knowing which is which. You believed force was necessary. Maybe you were right. But assuming force is *always* necessary is as limiting as assuming it's *never* necessary."

"He has a point," Professor Xavier said quietly. "Erik, he has a point."

Magneto and Issei exchanged looks that suggested the debate would continue, but there was respect in it. Not agreement—they remained fundamentally opposed on several points. But recognition that the opposition came from different assessment of reality, not from malice or stupidity.

"You would choose cooperation with your oppressors?" Magneto asked finally.

"I would choose to offer cooperation first," Issei said. "And to respond with force only when cooperation was rejected. Your methods are valid if cooperation has been exhausted. But I believe you can exhaust it slower than you generally do."

## THEATRE LEDGER — SECTION III: FAITH AND REDEMPTION

**[HUD: Gacha Pull - "Nightcrawler and Issei Discuss Faith" (Rare) - TRIGGERED]**

Nightcrawler appeared with the sudden teleportation of his power, materializing in the middle of the Theatre with the casual grace of someone who had spent his entire life arriving at places unexpectedly.

"Guten Tag," he said cheerfully. "I heard there was a dragon-man in the Theatre, and I thought I should say hello."

Where Logan had moved with the bearing of a soldier and Magneto with the bearing of a revolutionary, Nightcrawler moved with the bearing of someone genuinely at peace with himself.

"Kurt Wagner," he said, offering a three-fingered hand. "Most people call me Nightcrawler because I am a demon to look at, but I prefer the name they gave me at birth."

Issei took the offered hand with genuine respect. "You're comfortable with who you are."

"I spent most of my life uncomfortable with it," Kurt replied. "But then I realized that discomfort was not coming from who I was, but from who I thought I was supposed to be. Once I stopped trying to be human and accepted that I was something else—something beautiful in its own way—the discomfort ended."

"That's a profound insight," Issei said thoughtfully.

"It's the insight of faith," Kurt said, and there was something deeply spiritual in his bearing now. "I came to accept myself because I came to believe that there was some principle behind existence that thought I was good as I was. That belief—that radical acceptance—it changed everything."

They spoke for a while about faith and redemption and the nature of accepting oneself when the entire world suggested that self-acceptance was not permitted. Kurt talked about his Catholic upbringing and how it had shaped his belief in redemption and second chances. Issei talked about draconic philosophy and the nature of accepting one's power without shame.

"You are trapped by your power," Kurt observed. "You have strength that other people cannot match, and you spend considerable energy ensuring that you don't accidentally destroy those you love. That is a form of self-discipline that most people cannot imagine."

"How do you manage the metaphorical equivalent with your power?" Issei asked.

"I pray," Kurt said simply. "I remind myself that the power is not the thing that matters. The person wielding the power is what matters. And if I am good, then the power is just a tool of good."

"That's not a guarantee," Issei pointed out. "That you'll use the power correctly."

"No," Kurt agreed. "But it's an intention. And intention matters. It shapes how we use power. It creates precedent. It becomes the foundation for everything we do."

When Logan suggested finding somewhere to get an actual drink, Issei agreed immediately. The Theatre's configuration allowed for spaces of genuine hospitality, and Logan led the dragon-man to what could only be described as a warrior's bar—rough furniture, simple alcohol, the kind of place where you could speak plainly without worrying about performative politeness.

"You're not the first dragon I've met," Logan said, pouring whiskey into two glasses. "There was this one time, century or so ago, when I ended up in a dimensional rift and came out in a world that had dragons walking around like it was normal. They didn't like humans much. But they respected strength and directness."

"That tracks," Issei said, accepting the drink. "Draconic culture is much more willing to accept strength than human culture is."

"Yeah," Logan agreed. "And less willing to accept weakness. That's the thing you got to be careful about. Power respects other power. But power without compassion eats itself."

They drank in companionable silence for a moment.

"You violated consent because you didn't think you had to ask," Logan said suddenly. "Not you, the room. But you were there for all the memories. You heard what I did to other people when I didn't have enough control to manage my strength."

"I did," Issei acknowledged.

"And you didn't judge me for it," Logan continued. "Most people judge the hell out of me. 'Oh, you're an animal, oh, you're a murderer, oh, you're out of control.' But you didn't. You just... understood. Understood that someone with power that great has to make choices about how to use it, and that sometimes those choices are wrong."

"I've made wrong choices too," Issei said. "I've hurt people trying to protect people. I've destroyed things trying to defend things. Power is not precision. It's never precision."

"Exactly," Logan said. "And most people don't get that. They want power to be clean. Surgical. They want strength to be used perfectly. But that's not how it works. Strength is messy. It's only the people who accept the mess and still try to do right by it that actually do any good."

They drank some more.

"I've decided something," Logan said eventually. "The violation of your privacy was wrong. But the fact that it happened means people know what you stand for now. They know your actual values. And that's worth something."

"It is," Issei agreed. "Though I would have preferred they learn it through choice rather than violation."

"Yeah," Logan said. "But we don't always get what we prefer. We get what the world hands us, and then we decide what to do with it."

Cyclops appeared after a while and joined them, and then Rias and Akeno descended to the bar as well, and it became less a formal meeting and more a gathering of warriors who understood the weight of carrying power. The conversation ranged from combat philosophy to the nature of responsibility to really terrible jokes that only made sense if you'd been alive for at least two centuries.

At one point, Wolverine told a story about accidentally punching an angel, and everyone—even Issei—laughed so hard that Nightcrawler thought something was wrong and teleported in to make sure they were okay.

As the evening drew to a close—if "evening" was a meaningful concept in a Theatre that existed outside normal time—Issei found himself in the central amphitheatre with all the various groups that had come to meet him.

The Avengers had learned to think about power differently. Tony was already sketching hybrid technologies. Carol was genuinely interested in a sparring match. Steve had established protocols for future contact that respected consent fully.

Doom had engaged in genuine philosophical discourse and would spend months rethinking his governance model as a result. Valeria had teased her father and made a friend in the process.

The X-Men had confronted their own relationship with power and consent. Magneto and Issei had found an area of profound disagreement, but one rooted in mutual respect. Kurt had found a kindred spirit in someone who understood faith as something different from religion. Logan had found a warrior who understood the weight of carrying strength.

"You came to the Theatre in anger," Professor Xavier said, and there was gentle wisdom in his voice. "And you have left it with something more valuable than apology or explanation. You have left it with genuine understanding."

"I'm not going to pretend the violation was acceptable," Issei said. "But I can acknowledge that something valuable emerged from the confrontation."

"That is growth," Xavier said simply. "And in my experience, growth is what separates tyranny from legitimate leadership."

The wives had formed a protective circle around Issei again, but now it felt less like defense and more like partnership. They had come through the violation together. They had established that their dignity was not negotiable. And they had emerged with new understanding of how power operates across multiversal boundaries.

As Theatre Keeper began to reconfigure the space—preparing to return the heroes and villains and scholars to their respective dimensions, their respective timelines, their respective realities—Issei found himself understanding why Rias had wanted him to come to the Theatre after all.

Not to be violated. Never that. But to understand that even violation, when confronted firmly enough, can become something that strengthens rather than destroys.

"We'll be back," Carol said as she and the Avengers prepared to depart. "If you'll have us. With proper consent frameworks and actual respect for your autonomy."

"You'll be welcome," Issei replied. "And I'll be waiting to see if you actually follow through on the reforms you've discussed."

Logan clapped him on the back hard enough that it nearly knocked him over. "You're alright, kid. For a dragon. Come visit sometime. I'll buy you a drink that's better than this swill."

"I'll take you up on that," Issei said.

And as the Theatre began to empty—as the observers and scholars and cosmic beings returned to their respective dimensions—the arena fell quiet. Just Issei and his wives and Theatre Keeper, who stood as the eternal witness to the transformations that occur when power genuinely encounters accountability.

"The archive has been restructured," Theatre Keeper announced. "Consent protocols are fully implemented. And your contribution to multiversal understanding has been recorded in a way that respects your autonomy."

"That's actually appreciated," Issei said.

As they prepared to return to their own world—to the draconic hierarchy, to Kuou Academy, to the familiar spaces where Issei's power was known and understood—he reflected on the week in the Theatre.

Violation had occurred. Consent had been violated. Privacy had been stolen. These things were true and unacceptable.

But from that violation had emerged genuine encounter. Heroes had been forced to confront their own complicity. Philosophers had engaged in serious dialogue. Warriors had recognized warriors.

And perhaps that was the lesson the Theatre had inadvertently taught: that violation, when confronted seriously, can lead to genuine transformation.

Not forgiveness. Not forgetting. But the possibility of moving forward differently.
