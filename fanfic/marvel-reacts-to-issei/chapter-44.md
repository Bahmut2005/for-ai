# Chapter Forty-Four: Yondu and the Red Dragon

**Primary Reaction Groups:** Yondu Udonta • Ravagers (Stakar, Kraglin, the captains) • Adoption-themed heroes (Quill, Nebula, Gamora, Rocket, Wolverine's row)

**Gacha Pulls:**
1. "Hypothetical Conversation between Yondu and Issei" — **Epic**
2. "Whistle vs. Dragon Shot" — **Comedy-Class**

---

Four nights into the Ravager Rolls, the theatre had learned two things about letting space pirates operate a cosmic gacha machine: first, that the machine did not respect them — the console still greeted Kraglin every evening with **OPERATOR: COMMON SENSE FAILURE (ACTING)** — and second, that the Ravagers had stopped pretending they weren't enjoying it. They had survived being insulted, being audited, and being shown a teenage devil who killed slavers with a straight face; Rocket had survived four hundred simulated heist failures and one impersonated AI. The arc had settled into a rhythm: crank the lever, get insulted, watch, argue, drink from flasks that had appeared in violation of at least three theatre bylaws nobody could locate.

Tonight the lever would not move.

Kraglin hauled at it twice, checked his hand, hauled again. "It's, uh. It's locked, Cap'n."

"Ain't locked, you're weak," said Yondu Udonta, ambling down the console steps with the specific bow-legged insolence of a man who had decided the cosmic theatre was just a bar he hadn't been thrown out of yet. He seized the lever. It moved on its own — smoothly, courteously, *away* from his hand — and cranked itself.

The whole Ravager section went quiet in the way a crew goes quiet when the ship does something the ship should not do.

The wheel came down through the dark trailing Epic violet, and the banner assembled letter by letter, taking its time, the way it did when it wanted a particular row to have a chance to leave and know they wouldn't.

**PULL ONE: HYPOTHETICAL CONVERSATION BETWEEN YONDU AND ISSEI — EPIC.**

**SIMULATION. NON-CANONICAL. NO AUDIENCE PRESENT. PARAMETERS: TWO MEN. ONE FIRE. FULL RECORDS EXCHANGED.**

"The hell'd *I* do," said Yondu, to the room, to the machine, to nobody.

Three rows up and one aisle over, in the seat he had occupied since Arc One — close enough to see the fin, far enough that no one had ever seen them speak — Peter Quill put down his drink.

---

The vision opened on a shore that belonged to no world: black sand, a sea of slow starlight, and a driftwood fire that burned a companionable orange because the machine had apparently decided some clichés were structural. On one side of the fire sat a simulation of Issei Hyoudou — school blazer, red gauntlet, the theatre's boy, seventeen forever. On the other side sat a simulation of Yondu Udonta, arrow at his hip, fin catching the firelight, whittling something he clearly had no plan for.

Each of them, the captions noted, had just finished reading the other's file.

"So," said sim-Yondu, not looking up. "Harem King."

"It's a legitimate life goal," said sim-Issei, with the weary instant defensiveness of a young man who had had this conversation across two pantheons.

"Ain't judging. I stole a Walkman off a corpse once. We all got aspirations." The knife took a curl of wood. "File says you collect kids."

The theatre felt the real Ravager rows shift. On the screen, Issei's easy slouch went away, replaced by something older that lived under it. "I don't *collect* anything. They just—" He stopped, tried again with more honesty, which the room had learned was his only actual fighting style. "They show up. Lost ones. They show up, and I'm there, and then it's done, because once they're under the wing, the wing doesn't close. That's not a policy. It's just true."

"Mm." Whittle. Whittle. "File says seven from a dead universe. Fox princess. A saint. Snack god on the carpet."

"Your file," said Issei, who had also done the reading, "says you delivered children."

The knife stopped.

In the theatre, several thousand people stopped breathing, and Stakar Ogord — who had exiled this man from everything that had ever loved him for exactly this line item — leaned forward with his jaw set like a verdict he was suddenly less sure of.

"Yeah," said sim-Yondu. Flat. Factual. He set the whittling down with great care, as if it were the thing that might break. "Fella named Ego paid good rates for his own offspring, ferried to him nice and safe. I told myself it was cargo runs. Cargo, though." He looked into the fire. "Cargo don't cry in the hold. That hold cried. I turned the music up. Did it again anyhow. Kept doin' it till I finally asked what happened to 'em after drop-off, and the answer was *nothing happened to 'em after,* on account of there wasn't no *after.*" A muscle moved in his jaw. "You want to hit me, boy, the file says you got a punch."

Issei didn't hit him. The theatre watched him not do it — watched the gauntlet flex once and go still — and watched him ask, instead, in the voice he used on battlefields when the fight was already over:

"Why did you keep the last one?"

"He was skinny," said Yondu immediately. "Could fit places. Good for thievin'."

"Why did you keep him?"

"I *said*—"

"You knew," said Issei. "By then you knew what the deliveries were. So you looked at one more kid crying in your hold and you didn't turn the music up. Why?"

The fire cracked. Out in the dark of the theatre, Peter Quill had both hands laced on top of his head, elbows in, the posture of a man in a decompression drill.

"Because I couldn't do it again," said sim-Yondu at last, and his voice had rust all through it. "That's it. That's the whole damn epiphany. Weren't noble. I just hit the bottom of myself and there was a kid standing on it. So I kept him. Raised him wrong. Yelled. Threatened to eat him — that was a joke, mostly, crew loved it, he didn't. Never once said the thing out loud." He picked the whittling back up so his hands had somewhere to be. "Man like your file, you probably said it daily. Big house. Rice bowls."

"I died," said Issei, "at sixteen, on a riverbank, because the first person who ever said she wanted me lied. So no. I know exactly what it is to be the kid nobody comes back for." He let that sit in the firelight. "That's why I go back for them. Every time. It's not because I'm good. It's because I know the exact size of the hole."

Yondu looked at him then. Really looked — the way the theatre had watched him size up marks and mutinies — and found neither.

"The ones I ferried," he said. "The wing closed on them. Nothin' opens it back up. You gonna tell me the one I kept balances the ledger? 'Cause he don't. I done the math nightly."

"No," said Issei, and the gentleness in it was worse than judgment. "It doesn't balance. It's not that kind of book. The ones you failed are failed forever. I've got names like that too — fewer, but I'm seventeen, give me time." A breath. "But it works the other way around too. The ones you *saved* don't get erased by the failure. You don't get to use your guilt to un-raise him. He got raised. He got a father with terrible music and a magic arrow who taught him to fly. That happened. You did that. Guilt doesn't get a vote on it."

"Weren't *supposed* to be me raisin' him," said Yondu, quiet. "Supposed to be his mama. His granddad. Anybody decent."

"Nobody's ever *supposed to,*" said Issei. "I wasn't supposed to raise seven kids from a dead sky. I'm a teenage devil with a dragon in my arm and a life expectancy my fiancées track on a ledger. There's no 'supposed to.' There's just a kid, and you, and whether you put them down once you've picked them up." He shrugged, seventeen again for a second. "You didn't put him down. Twenty-six years. That's not an accident, old man. That's a choice you made every single morning."

The fire burned. Yondu turned the whittling over in his hands, and the theatre finally saw what it was becoming, unplanned, the way his hands apparently defaulted: small, crude, unmistakable. A ship.

"You'd have killed me," he said. "Back in them ferry years. Man who did what I did — your file says slavers burn."

"Probably," said Issei. "The me from back then, if he could've. The me now—" he considered it honestly, because he was incapable of anything else, "—the me now would have stopped you, broken your arrow, taken every kid off your ship." Beat. "And then fed you dinner, because you'd have that look you have right now, and my mom feeds that look. It's a whole thing. We have a big table."

And Yondu Udonta laughed — a startled, rusted-out bark, the laugh of a man ambushed by it — and in the theatre, in the real rows, the real Yondu made no sound at all, which everyone near him was careful not to see.

**ASSESSMENT,** printed the screen, softly, in the margin. **SUBJECTS DISAGREE ON EVERYTHING EXCEPT THE ONLY THING.**

---

"Enough of this weepy garbage," announced sim-Yondu, standing, cracking his neck. "File says you shoot dragon blasts. I got one arrow. Bet you the ship I'm whittling you can't touch me."

**PULL TWO: WHISTLE VS. DRAGON SHOT — COMEDY-CLASS.**

The theatre, emotionally waterlogged, grabbed the tonal life raft with both hands.

What followed was, by unanimous later agreement, the greatest fight nobody won. Yondu whistled — that first shrill note the Ravager rows knew in their spines — and the Yaka arrow came off his hip like a living thing with opinions. Issei's gauntlet flared, **[BOOST.]**, and the first Dragon Shot roared across the black sand — and the arrow *herded* it, nudging the blast's edge with contemptuous little taps until it curved politely into the sea.

"That's cheating," said Issei.

"That's *piloting,*" said Yondu.

**[BOOST.] [BOOST.]** Issei split his next shot into a fan of five. The arrow went through all five gaps in the fan, spelled a word in afterglow that the machine hastily pixelated, and knocked his ahoge flat on the way past. In the theatre, Kraglin was making a high continuous wheeze. Rocket was standing on his seat yelling trajectory advice at both of them.

Issei tried to whistle back. What came out was a wet *fffhp.* The arrow stopped mid-air and rotated toward him slowly, the way one turns to look at a man who has insulted one's mother.

"Did—" sim-Yondu wiped his eyes, "—did you just try to *steal my arrow* with a *spit noise*—"

"IT WORKS FOR YOU!"

"I got a FIN, boy, I got CYBERNETICS, you got *enthusiasm and a hairstyle*—"

**[BOOST.] [BOOST.] [BOOST.]** — and then Issei did the thing the theatre should have seen coming, because it was the thing he always did, the thing that had rewritten half the room's tactical doctrine across four arcs. He stopped shooting *at* Yondu. He caught the arrow gently in a gauntleted fist — took the puncture through the palm without flinching, because that was the toll — and said, "**Transfer.**"

**[TRANSFER.]**

Ten stacked Boosts went out of the Red Dragon Emperor and into the Yaka arrow.

It came out of his hand like a sunrise learning to fly. The arrow went up — trailing not its usual red thread but a broad, blazing crimson-gold banner of light — and Yondu's whistle chased it, note after note, and the arrow *answered* like it had been waiting its whole metal life to have this much sky in it. He wrote with it. Loops. Spirals. The theatre would argue for weeks about what the shapes were, but the Ravager rows knew fireworks when they saw them, and Peter Quill, in the dark, knew *these* fireworks, from a funeral the rest of the theatre had not been told about, and sat there silently coming apart at a seam twenty-six years old.

"She never flew like that," said sim-Yondu, head all the way back, firelight and arrow-light on his face. "Not once. Not for me."

"She always flew like that for you," said Issei, watching the sky. "I just turned the volume up."

The arrow came down, spent and dim, into Yondu's palm. He looked at it. Then he tossed the little whittled ship across the fire, underhand, and Issei caught it two-handed like something holy.

"Draw," said Yondu.

"Draw," agreed Issei.

**SIMULATION END,** stamped the screen. **RESULT: DRAW. CLASSIFICATION OF COMBATANTS: FATHERS. NEITHER WAS SUPPOSED TO BE. BOTH ARE.**

---

The lights came up on a theatre that had learned, over forty-three chapters, many ways of being loud, and chose none of them.

Peter Quill stood up. The whole room watched him do it — watched him step over Rocket, walk down three rows and across one aisle, past twenty-six years of never quite having the conversation, and sit down next to the real Yondu Udonta, who was studying the dead screen with ferocious blankness.

"You never said it out loud," Quill said. "For the record. You didn't have to. The arrow was never gonna hurt me and I knew it by the time I was ten, and everybody on that ship knew I knew, and you *definitely* knew I knew, so all the eating-me threats were basically bedtime stories, and—" his voice did the thing his father's rust had done, "—and he may have been my father, but he wasn't my daddy, and I have literally said that out loud before and I'm saying it again where the whole multiverse can hear it."

"...Kid saved my whittlin' knife hand about eight fights back," said Yondu, hoarse, to the room, apropos of nothing, because it was the only exit available. "That's all this is."

"Sure it is," said Kraglin, wet-eyed, and got an elbow.

Stakar Ogord rose, across the Ravager section, and the captains went still, because Stakar rising was law. He looked at Yondu a long moment — the man he'd exiled, for the cargo, for the code — and said, gravely: "The exile stands for what the hold carried. It always will; the boy said it true — that book doesn't balance." A pause. "But I watched a man hit his own bottom and find a kid standing on it, and raise him twenty-six years off one choice a morning. That goes in the *other* book. Ravagers keep both. We always did."

From the X-Men's block, Logan's voice, gravel-gentle, not looking at anyone: "Told you all in Arc Three. Takes one to know one." Jubilee, beside him, had given up on her mascara entirely. Laura was watching Quill and Yondu the way she watched everything that proved a thing could be survived.

And from the Guardians' rows: "Some of us," said Nebula, into the quiet, each word placed like a blade set down, "were taken by hands that never learned this. That built us into weapons and called the building love." She was looking at the little whittled ship still rendered on the frozen screen. "I used to think the difference between Thanos and any other man who takes children was rate of survival. It isn't. It's this. It's whether the hold cries and the man *turns the music down.*"

Gamora took her sister's hand. Neither looked at the other. It held.

In the villains' dark, Mephisto sat with steepled fingers and, for the second time in the fic, wrote nothing down — because guilt that refuses to be traded against love was, contractually speaking, the single most worthless commodity in his portfolio, and the theatre kept *producing* it.

The betting board from Arc Three flickered on, briefly, unbidden, and appended one line to the old adoption market: **UDONTA, Y. — MARKET CLOSED. POSITION FILLED IN 1988. PAID IN FULL.** Then it dimmed, respectfully, before anyone could laugh or cry, and most of the room did some of both.

High in the dark, the crack in the screen ticked — once, soft, patient — and beneath the frozen image of a whittled ship, the machine printed the next banner in treasure-chest gold:

**NEXT: PLANET-SIZE VAULT RAID.**

"...Okay," said Rocket thickly, already standing on his seat, "feelings are DONE, who's got a pen—"

**HE IS THE ONE WHO WILL STAND BEFORE THE THINGS YOU CANNOT,** said the frame under the gold, just once, and was gone.
